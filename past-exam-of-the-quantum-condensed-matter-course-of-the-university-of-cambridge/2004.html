The radial distribution function is the conditional number density of neighbors at separation r, divided by the bulk number density n. In an isotropic three-dimensional liquid, the mean number in a thin spherical shell is dN=4πnr2g(r)dr. Strong short-range repulsion makes g(r) very small inside the effective particle core. A first maximum marks the nearest-neighbor coordination shell. More distant shells give weaker oscillations, which decay toward g(r)=1 as long-range positional correlations disappear. A typical simple liquid has no indefinitely repeated sharp lattice peaks.
The static structure factor measured by diffraction is related to the Fourier transform of g(r)−1. Thus the broad diffraction maxima of a liquid and the damped real-space coordination shells describe the same short-range organization.

In a p-n junction, diffusion and electron-hole recombination near the contact leave a depletion region of ionized dopants. Its built-in electric field opposes further majority-carrier diffusion. At thermodynamic equilibrium drift and diffusion currents cancel. Forward bias lowers the electrostatic barrier and injects electrons and electron holes across the junction; reverse bias raises the barrier and suppresses that majority-carrier injection. For an ideal diode below breakdown,
I=Is​(eeV/(kB​T)−1).​
(2)
Thus a modest forward voltage gives a rapidly increasing current, whereas reverse current remains approximately −Is​. This asymmetric current-voltage relation makes the junction a rectifier. Real leakage, electron-hole recombination and breakdown limit the ideal expression.

This is the Joule-Thomson coefficient, μJT​=(∂T/∂P)H​, describing a temperature change during an isenthalpic pressure change. Here H is enthalpy, CP​=(∂H/∂T)P​ is the heat capacity at constant pressure, and V is volume, all for the same chosen amount of substance. Starting with dH=TdS+VdP and using the Gibbs free-energy Maxwell relation (∂S/∂P)T​=−(∂V/∂T)P​ gives
dH=CP​dT+[V−T(∂T∂V​)P​]dP.
(7)
Setting dH=0 proves
μJT​=CP​1​[T(∂T∂V​)P​−V].​
(8)
In a steady insulated throttle or porous plug, negligible kinetic- and potential-energy changes make the upstream and downstream enthalpies equal. Real-gas interactions permit cooling and an inversion curve, making the effect useful in gas refrigeration and liquefaction.
The measurement of the Joule-Thomson effect with a porous plug compares pressures and thermometers on the two sides of an insulated flow restriction. Suppressing external heat exchange and separating the readings from high-speed jets are part of making the enthalpy argument applicable; an arbitrary adiabatic expansion need not be isenthalpic.

The Clapeyron equation gives the slope of a two-phase phase coexistence curve. Let sl​,sv​ and vl​,vv​ be entropy and volume per molecule, or consistently per mole. Equality of the two chemical potentials and dμ=−sdT+vdP imply
0=d(μv​−μl​)=−(sv​−sl​)dT+(vv​−vl​)dP.
(9)
At reversible coexistence the latent heat in the same amount convention is L=T(sv​−sl​). Therefore
dTdP​=TΔvL​,Δv=vv​−vl​.​
(10)
The relation assumes thermodynamic equilibrium between two bulk phases of a pure substance, neglecting curvature and other work terms. For vaporization both latent heat and volume change are positive, giving a positive vapor-pressure slope. If the vapor is approximately ideal and its volume dominates, the ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation gives dlnP/dT≃L/(kB​T2) with latent heat per molecule.
Experimentally, a sealed two-phase sample in a temperature-controlled bath supplies the coexistence pressure as temperature changes. Independent calorimetry and phase-density measurements supply L and Δv, allowing the pressure slope to test the equation. The formula connects those distinct measurements and is a practical route to latent heats.

Entropy is a thermodynamic state function. For a reversible heat transfer, dS=δQrev​/T; the Clausius inequality generalizes this to irreversible processes. The combined entropy of a system and its surroundings does not decrease. A subsystem can lose entropy by exporting heat or matter, so the second-law statement must specify the whole isolated combination. In a simple compressible system,
dU=TdS−PdV+μdN,
(13)
which identifies temperature, pressure and chemical potential as derivatives of the energy representation of the state function. Thermodynamic equilibrium maximizes entropy under fixed total energy, volume and particle number. A heat reservoir instead leads to minimizing the Helmholtz free energy at fixed T,V,N, or the Gibbs free energy at fixed T,P,N.
Statistical mechanics explains the state count. In a microcanonical ensemble, equally probable compatible microstates give Boltzmann's entropy formula, S=kB​lnΩ. With general discrete probabilities, the Gibbs entropy is
S=−kB​∑j​pj​lnpj​.
(14)
The microcanonical formula follows by setting every nonzero probability to 1/Ω. For a canonical ensemble, pj​=e−βEj​/Z, so
S=kB​lnZ+TU​,F=U−TS=−kB​TlnZ.​
(15)
This turns microscopic energy levels and their populations into a measurable thermodynamic quantity. Classical phase-space counting needs a consistent microscopic normalization and indistinguishability convention; quantum state counting supplies that normalization. Independent systems have multiplicative state counts and therefore additive entropies.
For an ideal gas freely expanding from volume V to 2V in an insulated enclosure, energy remains fixed while each particle gains twice the accessible position volume. The state count increases by 2N, giving ΔS=NkB​ln2​. The actual irreversible process transfers no heat. A reversible isothermal comparison path transfers heat NkB​Tln2 and computes the same entropy change because the initial and final thermodynamic equilibrium states are the same.
Calorimetry supplies another connection to experiment: at fixed pressure and within one phase, S(T)−S(0)=∫0T​CP​(T′)dT′/T′, with latent-heat contributions L/T at first-order transitions. The third law of thermodynamics fixes S(0)=0 for an ideal perfect crystal with a unique ground state. Degeneracy or frozen disorder can produce residual entropy. Entropy thus links heat-capacity measurements, phase thermodynamic equilibrium and the number of accessible microscopic configurations.

Charge carrier mobility measures weak-field drift speed per electric field. For electrons and electron holes,
ve​=−μe​E,vh​=+μh​E,σ=e(nμe​+pμh​).​
(16)
Both mobilities are positive, with SI units m2V−1s−1; the opposite drift signs reflect the opposite carrier charges. In a simple isotropic relaxation-time model, electron mobility is μe​=eτe​/me∗​ and hole mobility is μh​=eτh​/mh∗​. These are momentum relaxation times, not the minority carrier lifetime for electron-hole recombination.
Scattering by phonons, ionized impurities and defects limits mobility. Increasing phonon populations commonly lowers mobility at high temperature; ionized-impurity scattering can dominate at low temperature and high semiconductor doping. Approximate addition of independent inverse relaxation times is the Matthiessen's rule. At sufficiently strong field, velocity saturation invalidates the constant-mobility drift law.
The Hall effect provides a measurement: a current-carrying bar in a perpendicular magnetic field develops a transverse voltage. In a one-carrier model ∣RH​∣=1/(en) and μ=σ∣RH​∣, combining the transverse voltage with longitudinal electrical conductivity. Multiple carrier types and scattering-dependent Hall factors require a more detailed interpretation.

b OurBigBook.com
Generation increases the mobile electron or hole populations; interband excitation creates an electron-hole pair. Thermal excitation and absorbed photons are important sources, and impact ionization can multiply carriers in a strong field. Ordinary single-photon interband absorption needs enough photon energy to bridge the band gap, with the required momentum balance.
Electron-hole recombination removes a pair. Radiative electron-hole recombination emits a photon, with simple rate Bnp; trap-assisted electron-hole recombination uses localized defect levels and transfers energy to the lattice; Auger recombination transfers the electron-hole recombination energy to another carrier. The last process becomes especially important at high injection because its rate contains products such as n2p and np2. The detailed band structure and available defects determine which channel dominates.
At thermal thermodynamic equilibrium, generation and electron-hole recombination continue at equal rates. For the simple radiative model the net electron-hole recombination rate is B(np−ni2​), enforcing the semiconductor mass-action law. Under weak excitation with the majority density nearly fixed, an excess minority density often decays as Δn(t)=Δn(0)e−t/τ, defining its minority carrier lifetime.
A short light pulse followed by time-resolved electrical conductivity or luminescence measures the relaxation. Spatial diffusion, trapping and instrumental response must be separated from electron-hole recombination before identifying a single fitted decay constant with the intrinsic lifetime. These processes control photodetector response and light-emitting-device efficiency.

An n-channel MOSFET has two heavily n-doped source and drain regions in a p-type body, with an insulated gate above the intervening surface. A positive gate voltage first depletes surface electron holes and then induces an electron semiconductor inversion layer. Above threshold VT​ this layer connects source to drain. Gate voltage controls the channel charge through the oxide capacitance, while the insulating gate permits a very small steady gate current.
For the long-channel MOSFET current law, let Cox​ be oxide capacitance per area, W,L channel width and length, and use constant mobility μn​. In the gradual-channel approximation,
ID​=μn​Cox​LW​[(VGS​−VT​)VDS​−2VDS2​​],0<VDS​<VGS​−VT​.
(17)
At larger drain voltage the ideal channel pinches off near the drain and the current saturates at
ID,sat​=21​μn​Cox​LW​(VGS​−VT​)2.​
(18)
Carriers still flow through the pinch-off region; pinch-off does not mean zero drain current. These formulas neglect channel-length modulation, short-channel effects and velocity saturation. A p-channel device reverses the carrier type and operating polarities. Gate control makes MOSFETs useful as electronic switches and amplifiers.

Exchange interactions arise from quantum electronic motion, Coulomb interactions and fermionic antisymmetry. A localized-spin description can be written H=∑i<j​Jij​Si​⋅Sj​. In this explicit plus-sign convention J<0 favors parallel spins and J>0 favors antiparallel spins; many texts put a minus sign into the Hamiltonian and reverse that convention.
Direct exchange involves overlapping orbitals and their Coulomb exchange matrix elements. The Pauli exclusion principle correlates spin symmetry with spatial symmetry, changing the electronic energy. Orbital occupation and competing kinetic processes determine which spin state is favored; the preference is not universally ferromagnetic.
Superexchange couples moments through virtual hopping, often via a nonmagnetic ligand. For two half-filled orbitals in the simple repulsive one-band model, the antiferromagnetic coupling is J≃4∣t∣2/U: virtual double occupation lowers the antiparallel-spin state more effectively. Orbital geometry and intermediate-state interactions can change the sign in other configurations. Real charge transport can remain insulating while this magnetic coupling acts.
Double exchange instead uses actual carrier motion between mixed-valence sites. Local Hund's coupling ties the mobile electron's spin to a core moment. Parallel neighboring cores allow a larger effective hopping amplitude and a lower carrier kinetic energy, favoring ferromagnetism. The original model explicitly connected conduction and magnetic order in manganese perovskites.[ref]
Conduction-electron-mediated exchange couples localized moments through a metal's electronic spin response. Its strength and sign depend on separation and the Fermi surface; in a simple three-dimensional asymptotic model the range factor oscillates as cos(2kF​r)/r3. The original nuclear-moment calculation was motivated by resonance-line broadening in silver.[ref] In an itinerant metal, an alternative description compares the kinetic-energy cost of unequal spin populations with the exchange-energy gain; the Stoner criterion expresses when spontaneous polarization is favorable.
The resulting order also depends on the lattice and competing couplings. In ferromagnetism, moments align to give a nonzero moment within a domain. In antiferromagnetism, opposing sublattices cancel their moments. In ferrimagnetism, oppositely ordered sublattices have unequal moments and leave a net one. A bulk zero magnetization alone cannot distinguish antiferromagnetic order from a disordered state or canceling magnetic domains.
The manganese monoxide neutron diffraction experiment makes that distinction experimentally. A monochromatic neutron beam scatters from powdered material, and a counter records angular intensity. Comparing the same compound at room temperature and 80K reveals additional low-temperature magnetic reflections, indexed by a larger magnetic unit cell, while nuclear reflections persist. That spatial periodicity is evidence of alternating order even though the net moment cancels.[ref]

For a bulk liquid and vapor at common fixed temperature and pressure, transfer dNl​ molecules from vapor to liquid while holding their sum fixed. The Gibbs free energy change is dG=(μl​−μv​)dNl​. Thermodynamic equilibrium with both phases present requires stationarity under either sign of transfer, hence μl​=μv​​. This is the bulk coexistence condition; an interface's curvature adds a surface-energy cost.
In the capillarity approximation, take an incompressible drop with constant liquid number density nl​, bulk chemical potential μl​ at the specified reservoir conditions, and size-independent surface tension Γ. The liquid bulk is extensive, so its Gibbs thermodynamic free energy is Nl​μl​. Creating interface area reversibly at fixed temperature adds ΓA. Equivalently, along growth at fixed bulk parameters the stated differential integrates as dGd​=μl​dNl​+ΓdA, with the no-drop reference set to zero. For a sphere,
Gd​=34​πR3nl​μl​+4πR2Γ.​
(19)
This approximation uses bulk properties for the interior and neglects the molecular interface thickness, curvature dependence of surface tension and higher compressibility corrections. It does not apply quantitatively to drops only a few molecules across.
The vapor contains N−Nl​ molecules. Treat its chemical potential as approximately unchanged by forming the drop, as for a large surrounding reservoir or negligible vapor depletion. Then
Gtot​(R)=Nμv​+34​πnl​(μl​−μv​)R3+4πΓR2.​
(20)
The radius is limited by the available molecules in a finite sample; the constant-reservoir expression is a local nucleation model, not a description of arbitrarily complete vapor depletion. The excess over the vapor reference has derivative
ΔG′(R)=4πR[2Γ+nl​(μl​−μv​)R].
(21)
If μl​>μv​, it increases for every R>0, leaving only the stable boundary minimum R=0. If μl​<μv​, R=0 is a metastable local minimum and the additional stationary radius is
Rc​=nl​(μv​−μl​)2Γ​.​
(22)
At that radius ΔG′′=−8πΓ<0, so it is an unstable maximum. The classical nucleation theory barrier is 16πΓ3/[3nl2​(μv​−μl​)2].
A fluctuation below Rc​ tends to shrink because reducing its surface cost lowers thermodynamic free energy. A supercritical drop tends to grow, because its bulk free-energy gain outweighs the added surface cost. Growth continues while material supply and supersaturation allow it, eventually changing the vapor state or leading to macroscopic liquid. Curved-interface pressure and chemical thermodynamic equilibrium can also be described through the Young–Laplace equation and the Kelvin equation.

For an isotropic free-electron gas at zero temperature, the Fermi wavevector is the radius of the occupied region in wavevector space. Its energy is EF​=ℏ2kF2​/(2m). The first Brillouin zone is the Wigner-Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice; it contains one distinct crystal momentum per primitive cell and per band, with spin counted separately.
For a two-dimensional sample of area A, allowed wavevectors have density A/(2π)2. Including two spin states, filling a circle gives N=2[A/(2π)2]πkF2​. Dividing by area therefore yields
kF​=2πn​.​
(29)
The square lattice has one atom per area a2, so for valence z the free-electron density is z/a2. Its Brillouin zone has ∣kx​∣,∣ky​∣≤π/a. Thus
kF(1)​=a2π​​≃8.36×109m−1,kF(2)​=a4π​​≃1.18×1010m−1.​
(30)
The monovalent Fermi circle lies inside the square. The divalent circle crosses the faces but misses the corners; its area equals the zone's area. These are the free-electron contours before distortion by the periodic potential.
At a Bragg plane, the periodic potential mixes degenerate plane waves differing by a reciprocal lattice vector. The two standing-wave combinations have different charge densities relative to the ions and split in energy, opening the gap. In a two-wave approximation, the boundary energies are EB​±Eg​/2, with Eg​=2∣VG​∣.
The boundary wavevector magnitude is π/a along [10] and 2​π/a along [11]. The free-electron boundary energies are therefore
EX​=2ma2ℏ2π2​≃4.18eV,EM​=2EX​≃8.36eV.
(31)
Within the stated direction-independent gap picture, the upper band's lowest face-edge energy is approximately EX​+2eV=6.18eV, while the lower band's corner-edge energy is approximately EM​−2eV=6.36eV. There is consequently a small overlap, about 0.18eV, rather than a global insulating gap. The divalent Fermi level lies in that overlap, producing occupied upper-band states and unfilled lower-band states.
Thus the intended isotropic-gap approximation predicts a metal, with small electron and hole pockets. Positive direct gaps can coexist with band overlap. A fully specified square-lattice potential would require simultaneous treatment of the corner degeneracies; a numerical boundary splitting alone does not uniquely determine an arbitrary two-dimensional band structure. The conclusion here uses the given simplified gap picture, rather than electron count alone.

(35)
For the given sample ND​=2×1021m−3 and NA​=1021m−3. With complete donor ionization and occupied acceptors, charge neutrality gives n−p=ND​−NA​=1021m−3. At 293K the given masses and gap give
Nc​≃3.92×1023m−3,Nv​≃8.56×1024m−3,ni2​≃1.26×1024m−6.
(36)
Solving the compensated semiconductor equations, or using ni​≪ND​−NA​, yields
n≃1.00×1021m−3,p=nni2​​≃1.26×103m−3.​
(37)
The very small minority density is consistent with the wide gap and the strongly n-type balance. Also n/Nc​≃2.6×10−3≪1 and p/Nv​≪1, checking the nondegenerate approximation. The acceptor levels are occupied in this n-type regime; complete acceptor ionization in the compensation equation is appropriate to the model.
At low temperature dopant freeze-out reduces the conduction-electron population; a quantitative curve would require dopant binding energies and occupation factors. Once donors are ionized while intrinsic excitation is negligible, n≃ND​−NA​ gives an extrinsic plateau. At higher temperature intrinsic pairs eventually dominate, so n≃p≃ni​(T)∝T3/2e−Eg​/(2kB​T) if the gap and masses are treated as constant. Real gap and mass changes modify that last expression.
