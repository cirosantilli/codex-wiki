Finite element method

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 23
The finite element method replaces an infinite-dimensional weak formulation by finitely many unknowns attached to a finite element mesh. Simple local functions, often piecewise polynomial functions, join to form a global approximation. The attraction is that each finite element can be integrated separately, while the assembled equations enforce a global balance. A piecewise-linear hat function only meets its immediate neighbors, so a large problem can have a sparse matrix rather than a dense one. Gilbert Strang's MIT lecture develops this connection from the one-dimensional weak formulation through local hats to the stiffness matrix and load integrals.[ref]
For the conforming Galerkin method, let V be a real Hilbert space, a a bounded coercive bilinear form, and ℓ a bounded linear functional. Given a conforming finite element space Vh​⊂V, the continuous and discrete problems are
u∈V:uh​∈Vh​:​a(u,v)=ℓ(v)a(uh​,vh​)=ℓ(vh​)​​for every v∈V,for every vh​∈Vh​.​

Writing uh​=∑j​cj​ϕj​ in a basis of Vh​ gives the linear system
Kc=F,Kij​=a(ϕj​,ϕi​),Fi​=ℓ(ϕi​).
(112)
Here K is the stiffness matrix and F is the vector of loads tested against the basis functions. The Galerkin method uses the same trial and test space in this construction. Finite element methods also use the Petrov-Galerkin method, with different trial and test spaces; testing against the trial space is therefore a property of this construction, rather than a definition of every finite element method.

Refining the finite element mesh then improves the approximation by adding local degrees of freedom. For a symmetric coercive bilinear form, Galerkin orthogonality makes the discrete solution the best approximation in the energy norm; the geometry of orthogonal projection explains why this local construction yields a globally meaningful answer.
