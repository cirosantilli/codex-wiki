Cavendish torsion balance

 ... Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics Newton's law of universal gravitation Newtonian gravitational field Cavendish experiment
OurBigBook.com Words: 585
A Cavendish torsion balance is a torsion balance arranged to measure gravitational torque from nearby source masses. Its suspended horizontal rod rotates about a vertical fiber. In the linear elastic regime, a twist angle θ, measured from the fiber's untwisted orientation, gives a restoring torque τfiber​=−κθ, where κ>0 is the torsion constant. The minus sign is essential: the fiber resists the twist. Under an approximately constant gravitational torque, the rotational equation using moment of inertia I and angular acceleration θ¨ is
Iθ¨=τg​−κθ,θeq​=κτg​​.
(43)
At equilibrium, the two torques balance. The angle is a displacement from the fiber's reference orientation, not the largest excursion during an oscillation. [ref]
For the toy parameters in the Cavendish experiment, the predicted equilibrium twist is
θeq​=1.0×10−85.33944×10−11​=0.00533944rad≃0.3059∘.
(44)
Here L∣θeq​∣/r≃0.0107, consistent with using a small geometric displacement in the toy model, but not an estimate of its total measurement error. Reversing the source positions ideally takes the equilibrium angle from +θeq​ to −θeq​: the magnitude of the change is 2∣θeq​∣≃0.6119∘. A stable zero offset then cancels in the difference. A real reading may track a reflected light spot; the law of reflection doubles a mirror's rotation in the outgoing beam, so the spot's angular change must not be confused with the rod's angle. [ref]
How can the fiber's torsion constant be found without already knowing the gravitational force? Measure the oscillation period and calculate the moment of inertia. For the same toy rod, neglecting its mass and treating both test masses as point masses, I=mL2+mL2=2mL2. With the source torque absent or treated as constant, let ϕ=θ−θeq​ be a small departure from equilibrium. Neglect damping and any change of gravitational torque with angle. The rotational equation becomes simple harmonic motion:
Iϕ¨​+κϕ=0,ϕ(t)=Acos(ωt+δ),ω=Iκ​​,T=2πκI​​,κ=T24π2I​.
(45)
Here A is the angular amplitude, δ specifies the initial phase, and ω is the angular frequency. A softer fiber gives a larger equilibrium twist but also a longer oscillation period: greater sensitivity costs observation time. An undamped oscillation does not settle at equilibrium; damping is needed for settling. [ref]
The toy moment of inertia and undamped oscillation period are
I=2(0.010)(0.10)2=2.0×10−4kgm2,T=2π1.0×10−82.0×10−4​​≃888.5766s≃14.81min.
(46)
Timing this oscillation period would recover the assumed torsion constant. The units check explains the result: torque divided by torsion constant is an angle in radians, and I/κ has units of time squared when radians are treated as dimensionless in SI.
Combining the equilibrium twist with the period calibration, the two-near-pair model would infer the gravitational constant as
G=2LmMκ∣θeq​∣r2​=MT24π2Lr2∣θeq​∣​.
(47)
The test mass cancels in the second expression because it enters both the gravitational torque and the toy moment of inertia. If the measured quantity is the magnitude ∣Δθ∣ of the change between opposite source positions, use ∣θeq​∣=∣Δθ∣/2. Substituting the toy twist and oscillation period returns the input G; it is a consistency check, not independent experimental evidence.
The sensitivity of a Cavendish torsion balance also explains its practical difficulty. Air motion can exert unwanted torque; unequal temperatures can drive that motion. Cavendish discusses this effect on p. 471 and describes observing from outside a closed room to avoid disturbing the instrument. [ref] Mechanical vibrations can excite the same slow oscillations used to calibrate the torsion constant, while changes in the fiber or source separations alter the inferred force. Precision therefore depends on resolving the equilibrium angle, calibrating the full suspended moment of inertia and torsion constant, and modeling the actual source geometry. The two-near-pair model omits opposite-source attraction, rod contributions, finite-size effects, damping, and the variation of gravitational torque as the rod turns. [ref]
