Radical of an ideal is the intersection of the prime ideals containing it (I​=⋂P⊇I, P prime​P)

 ... Area of mathematics Algebra Commutative algebra Ring Ideal Radical of an ideal
OurBigBook.com Words: 123
For any ideal I in a commutative ring, every prime ideal containing I contains I​, because an∈P implies a∈P. Conversely, if a∈/I​, the multiplicatively closed set T={1,a,a2,…} is disjoint from I. By Zorn's lemma, there is an ideal P⊇I maximal among those disjoint from T.
This P is a prime ideal: if bc∈P but b,c∈/P, maximality makes both P+(b) and P+(c) meet T. Multiplication of representatives from these two intersections puts an element of T in P, a contradiction. Thus there is a prime ideal containing I but avoiding a, establishing the identity. For I=R, the intersection is empty and means R. It follows that an ideal is a radical ideal exactly when it is an intersection of ideals that are all prime ideals.
