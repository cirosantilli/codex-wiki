Spark-excited damped oscillation (q(t)=Ae−αtcos(ωd​t+ϕ))

 Home Electrical engineering Telecommunication Radio communication Wireless telegraphy
OurBigBook.com Words: 214
A spark gap can rapidly discharge a charged capacitor through an inductor. After excitation, the stored energy runs down through Joule heating, radiation and other losses. A key held down can permit repeated discharges: each discharge starts a damped train of rapid oscillations. The spark repetition frequency, the electrical oscillation frequency within each train, and the rate of keyed symbols are different quantities. Repetition does not make each train a maintained sinusoid.
An original lumped model replaces the conducting spark gap and other losses by a constant total electrical resistance R>0 in an isolated series RLC circuit. For electric charge q on the capacitor, the Kirchhoff cycle law gives
Lq¨​+Rq˙​+Cq​=0,ω0​=LC​1​,α=2LR​,Q=Rω0​L​.
(91)
For Q>1/2, its characteristic roots are −α±iωd​, with ωd​=ω02​−α2​, proving the stated damped form. The electric current q˙​ has the same exponential decay scale. Its wave envelope falls by exp(−2πα/ωd​) per cycle; the energy envelope falls twice as fast in the exponent. A maintained sinusoid instead requires continuing input power balancing losses.
With L=5mH, C=2nF and R=100Ω, the model gives f0​=ω0​/(2π)=50.329kHz, Q factor Q=15.811, amplitude time constant 1/α=100μs, and damped frequency ωd​/(2π)=50.304kHz. These are chosen component values. A real spark gap switches nonlinearly, an antenna radiates, and coupling exchanges energy between circuits; one constant-R free decay does not model the complete transmitter. Rapid damping also broadens the frequency spectrum, which helps explain why short trains make sharp receiver selection difficult.
