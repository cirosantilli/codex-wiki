Coupled resonant circuit demonstration

 Home Electrical engineering Telecommunication Radio communication Wireless telegraphy
OurBigBook.com Words: 733 Articles: 1
MIT's demonstration places two 120-turn coils face to face, each with a capacitor. A swept sinusoid drives one circuit; an oscilloscope observes the other circuit's capacitor electric potential difference. The sheet specifies L=5mH and C=3300 or 2500pF, giving nominal resonant frequencies near 39 and 45kHz. It presents a procedure, not a measured dataset.[ref]
A reproducible extension can test the same selection principle with the identical components of the coupled resonant circuit model. Prepare two separate series loops, measure their actual inductances, capacitances and coil electrical resistances, and include generator Thévenin resistance in the driven loop's total R1​. Choose added resistors to make R1​=R2​=100Ω if the measured losses permit it. Include a known Rs​=50Ω sense resistor within R2​. Drive the first loop with a small function generator sinusoid; read the second loop's electric current as ∣I2​∣=∣U∣/Rs​ using an oscilloscope across the sense resistor. Here U denotes the sense reading, distinct from the source electric potential difference V in the model.
Sweep 35–68kHz slowly enough for transients to settle; record source frequency, source amplitude at the circuit terminals, and sense amplitude. Repeat at fixed coil spacing after changing one capacitor, then at fixed components after changing spacing. Turn the drive off to establish the background; rotate the receiving coil toward a right angle to test inductive coupling; restore its orientation and repeat to check drift. These are proposed controls, not observations reported by MIT. Keep probe loading small and account for it if not negligible. A grounded oscilloscope can electrically join the loops if attached to both: use an appropriately isolated differential input for any simultaneous primary measurement, or measure the primary separately.
Recovering deliberately chosen long and short generator bursts on the receiver trace demonstrates symbol timing. The essential inference is narrower than a long-distance radio test: nearby coils primarily exchange energy through mutual inductance in the near and far field regime dominated by the near field. Coil spacing, detuning and background controls distinguish this coupling from a direct wire connection or incidental pickup. They do not by themselves demonstrate reception of propagating radio waves in the radiation zone.
