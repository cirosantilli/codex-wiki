For nonzero momentum, physical helicity is the spin projection along the direction of motion,
h^=∣p∣S⋅p​=21​(σ⋅p^​0​0σ⋅p^​​),h=±21​.​
(1)
The convention without 1/2 labels the same two eigenspaces by ±1. Write p^​=(sinθcosφ,sinθsinφ,cosθ). Normalized two-component eigenvectors are
χ+​=(cos(θ/2)eiφsin(θ/2)​),χ−​=(−e−iφsin(θ/2)cos(θ/2)​),(σ⋅p^​)χ±​=±χ±​.
(2)
Each can carry an arbitrary phase. Before imposing an energy equation, the general four-component helicity eigenvector is (aχ±​,bχ±​)T, with arbitrary complex a,b; unit Norm imposes ∣a∣2+∣b∣2=1. For a positive-energy free electron, the Dirac equation fixes their ratio and gives
u±​(p)=2EE+m​​​χ±​±E+m∣p∣​χ±​​​,u±†​u±​=1.​
(3)
Multiplying by an arbitrary overall amplitude and the common plane wave supplies the corresponding free helicity eigenstate. Helicity has no preferred direction at p=0. For a massive particle a boost can overtake its momentum and change its helicity; it should not be identified with Lorentz-invariant chirality.

The charged weak current contains ψˉ​γμPL​ψ. For an ultrarelativistic external fermion, it selects negative helicity; its antifermion partner has positive helicity. Wrong-helicity charged-current amplitudes vanish in the massless limit and are suppressed by powers of m/E at finite mass. The active neutrino is consequently left-helicity and its antineutrino right-helicity in this limit.
This is a charged-current statement, not a prohibition on every right-helicity Standard Model fermion. Electromagnetic and strong vector interactions act on both chiralities and conserve helicity along a massless fermion line. A neutral-current Z vertex also couples to both charged-fermion chiralities with unequal strengths. For example, a right-helicity electron can scatter electromagnetically: it is not excluded from the Standard Model. In vector-current pair annihilation the massless fermion and antifermion have opposite helicities; a purely charged-current vertex keeps only the left-fermion/right-antifermion choice. Yukawa interactions connect left and right fields and must be distinguished from these vector-current selection rules.

The charged two-pion and three-pion channels can be assigned to the same spin-zero kaon by their parent mass and lifetime. For two spinless pions, total angular momentum zero forces their relative orbital angular momentum to zero, so the product of their intrinsic parities gives
P2π​=(−1)2(−1)0=+1.​
(22)
For three spinless pions, use Jacobi orbital angular momenta l,L. Coupling them to total zero requires l=L, and hence
P3π​=(−1)3(−1)l+L=−1.​
(23)
Thus both channels cannot conserve parity in decays of one parent with a definite parity. The weak decay interaction permits the observed coexistence.
Dalitz plots identify the three-body events, determine their internal kinematics, and allow amplitude and angular-momentum analyses through their populations and resonance structures. Together with the spin and parent-identity evidence, they support the parity assignment. They are not a standalone parity-odd observable: reversing all three momenta leaves every sij​, and therefore every point on the plot, unchanged. A flat distribution by itself cannot establish parity violation without the channel and spin information.

The lepton mixing matrix relates charged-current neutrino flavour fields to mass eigenfields: ναL​=∑i​Uαi​νiL​. Nondegenerate masses then give relative propagation phases and flavour oscillations. This is the three-massive-neutrino extension of the minimal massless-neutrino Standard Model.
A unitary three-by-three matrix has nine real parameters: three rotation angles and six phases. For Dirac leptons, the independent field rephasings remove five phases, because one common phase acts trivially. Three angles and one physical Dirac phase remain. For Majorana neutrinos two additional phases cannot be removed, but they cancel from ordinary oscillation probabilities.
The phases printed in the lower two rows do not give a unitary matrix for general δ. A consistent convention is U=R23​U13​(δ)R12​, where the real rotations use the indicated mixing angles and
U13​(δ)=​c13​0−s13​eiδ​010​s13​e−iδ0c13​​​.​
(24)

At low charged-lepton energy, collisional ionization and excitation dominate over radiation. A slowing Positron also eventually annihilates, usually yielding photons. Low-energy photons lose energy through Compton scattering and, at sufficiently low energy, photoelectric absorption; pair production is kinematically unavailable below its threshold.
The electron critical energy Ec​ is the energy where mean bremsstrahlung and collisional losses are equal. With −(dE/dx)rad​≃E/X0​ and a slowly varying collisional loss,
​dxdE​​rad,Ec​​=​dxdE​​coll,Ec​​.​
(30)
Above this energy, radiation and pair creation multiply shower particles efficiently. Below it, charged particles mainly deposit their energy by collisions and the shower dies out. Critical energy is material dependent; it is not a new photon-production threshold.

In the binary branching model of an electromagnetic shower, after n ideal equal-sharing generations, the model has Nn​=2n particles of energy En​=E0​/2n, at depth nX0​. Stop the branching when En​≤Ec​. Thus
nstop​=⌈log2​Ec​E0​​⌉=⌈log2​(104)⌉=14.​
(31)
The continuous estimate is 13.29X0​. At 13 generations the particles still have approximately 12.2MeV, whereas at 14 they have approximately 6.10MeV. The ideal branching model therefore suggests roughly 13–14X0​ before multiplication stops.
This is not an accurate containment criterion. Real conversion and radiation distances fluctuate, bremsstrahlung energy sharing is broad rather than always one half, some energetic branches penetrate beyond the average, and particles below critical energy still have stopping and absorption ranges. The simplified stopping depth is closer to its own model's shower-maximum scale than a depth containing all deposited energy. A calorimeter needs additional longitudinal depth to control leakage and event-to-event tails. These effects explain why approximately 25X0​ can be required even though the equal-sharing estimate is smaller. The toy model does not predict the realistic shower maximum or leakage fraction quantitatively.

The principal passive-material requirement is a short radiation length in centimetres, normally obtained using a dense, high-atomic-number absorber such as lead or tungsten. It produces and contains an electromagnetic shower in a compact detector. Mechanical construction, transverse spread, cost, radiation tolerance and inactive gaps also affect the choice, but the absorber is selected for shower formation rather than signal production.
Liquid-argon gaps are a suitable active sampling medium. Ionization electrons drift in an electric field and induce a measurable charge signal. Advantages include radiation tolerance, stable response and useful spatial segmentation. Disadvantages include cryogenic infrastructure, high-voltage and purity requirements, signal collection times and electronic-noise control. Only the active fraction is measured directly, so fluctuations of energy shared between absorber and active gaps introduce a sampling contribution to the resolution. A high active fraction and fine sampling help, at the price of construction complexity and additional channels.

For independent contributions the parametrization means
σ2(E)=A2E+B2+C2E2.​
(32)
The stochastic coefficient A includes fluctuations in the sampled shower fraction, the finite number of signal quanta, and intrinsic shower fluctuations. A number of detected quanta proportional to E has fluctuations proportional to E​, giving relative resolution proportional to E−1/2. The noise coefficient B describes an approximately fixed energy-equivalent readout uncertainty, including electronic noise and, for specified running conditions, Pile-up contributions. The constant term C represents residual fractional nonuniformity, calibration and response variations, imperfect correction for inactive material, and leakage effects over the fitted range. The simple three-term model is an approximation; leakage and Pile-up need not follow these powers exactly.
A homogeneous calorimeter uses the showering material itself as the active medium, for example a scintillating crystal. It removes the absorber-versus-active sampling fluctuation and can have a much smaller stochastic term, but it does not eliminate signal statistics or intrinsic shower fluctuations. Its noise and constant terms are not automatically smaller: they depend on light collection, calibration, temperature, radiation damage, electronics and geometry. Homogeneous detectors can offer excellent energy resolution and compact construction, but large crystal volumes can be costly and fine longitudinal segmentation difficult. Sampling detectors often provide economical large coverage and flexible segmentation with robust absorbers, at the cost of sampling fluctuations and inactive material. If energy is expressed in GeV, A has units GeV1/2, B has units GeV and C is dimensionless.

The weak charged current couples an up-type quark to the rotated down field diL′​=∑j​Vij​djL​. A neutral current is flavour universal within equal-charge quarks. Rotating it into the mass basis therefore gives
∑i​dˉiL′​γμdiL′​=∑j,k​dˉjL​γμ(V†V)jk​dkL​=∑j​dˉjL​γμdjL​.
(43)
The off-diagonal terms vanish by CKM unitarity, so no tree-level flavour-changing neutral current is generated. In the two-generation description, the orthogonal strange-charm partner completes the rotation whose single down-strange combination alone would have produced an off-diagonal neutral coupling. The complete multiplet supplies the cancellation.
At loop level, a flavour-changing amplitude has the structure
Aa→b​∝∑i​Vai​Vbi∗​F(mi2​/mW2​),a=b.
(44)
If all internal masses were equal, F would be common and ∑i​Vai​Vbi∗​=0. Subtracting an arbitrary common value makes the suppression explicit:
Aa→b​∝i∑​Vai​Vbi∗​[F(mi2​/mW2​)−F(mref2​/mW2​)].​
(45)
This is the Glashow-Iliopoulos-Maiani mechanism. Unequal masses leave small loop-level flavour-changing neutral currents, so the statement that they are never observed would be too strong: the mechanism suppresses them and forbids them at tree level. The cancellation requires complex CKM products; the rounded table of absolute values alone must not be summed as if every phase were positive.

For a t→cZ search, the cleanest neutral-boson reconstruction is Z→e+e− or μ+μ−: require an isolated opposite-sign same-flavour pair near the Z mass and a jet forming the top mass with that pair. In top-pair events, tagging the other top through its ordinary bW decay provides a bottom jet and, if its W is leptonic, another isolated lepton and missing transverse momentum. Kinematic reconstruction and heavy-flavour information help reject vector-boson-plus-jets and other backgrounds. Hadronic or invisible Z decays have higher rate but less distinctive backgrounds or incomplete mass reconstruction.
For the literal t→cZγ channel, additionally require a prompt isolated photon and reconstruct the top with Z+γ+jet. For the alternative t→cγ, use an isolated energetic photon and jet near the top mass, again exploiting the other top as a tag. These are search signatures; no present-day observation claim is needed to interpret the historical question.
