At p=600 MeV/c, the velocities are β=p/p2+m2c2​, giving approximately 1.0000, 0.98485 and 0.53874 for electrons, muons and protons. The glass threshold is β>1/1.55=0.64516. Thus the first two produce Cherenkov radiation, with internal angles
θe​≃49.82∘,θμ​≃49.07∘,
(46)
whereas a primary proton at this momentum is below threshold.
If an optical arrangement transports those cone angles without changing them, a plane L=2 m downstream would intersect rings of radius
R=LtanθC​=Ln2β2−1​,Re​≃2.369 m,Rμ​≃2.307 m​.
(47)
These are conditional geometric estimates, not the ray trace for a flat slab in air. The 0.1 m emission depth alone spreads the radius by order 0.12 m, already larger than the approximately 0.062 m separation. The slab is one radiation length: electrons lose appreciable energy by bremsstrahlung and begin an electromagnetic shower, producing a broader and less clean pattern. Muons mainly ionize and retain a comparatively clean cone, with some Multiple Coulomb scattering. A below-threshold primary proton gives no direct cone, although energetic delta electrons and other secondary interactions can produce light.
There is an essential optical qualification. For a normal beam through a parallel-faced glass slab followed by air, the internal critical angle is θcrit​=arcsin(1/1.55)≃40.18∘. Both emission angles exceed it. Snell's law would require sinθair​=1.55sinθC​>1, so the ideal photons undergo total internal reflection at the downstream face and do not form those direct downstream rings. Optical coupling, a prism or different surface geometry must be specified to extract them. The stated material parameters alone do not define a unique detected pattern; this flat-slab geometry is a concrete counterexample to silently assuming straight transmitted rays.
