For an electron-positron collider of fixed bending radius R, synchrotron radiation rapidly becomes the principal obstacle. Multiplying the supplied radiated power by the revolution period 2πR/c gives
U0​=3ϵ0​Re2γ4​,U0​[GeV]≃8.85×10−5R[m]E[GeV]4​.
(54)
A bending radius of 3 km gives losses of about 2.95 GeV per turn at 100 GeV per beam, but 115 GeV per turn at 250 GeV. At 100 GeV, a 5 mA beam already radiates about 15 MW. The radiofrequency system must replace this energy, and the vacuum chamber must absorb the radiation. The centre-of-mass energy is twice the beam energy for equal opposite beams.
For a collider with two proton beams, the same energy gives a much smaller Lorentz factor. At fixed energy and radius the radiated power scales as m−4, so the suppression relative to electrons is (me​/mp​)4≃8.8×10−14. The main practical limit is the dipole magnetic field through magnetic rigidity:
p[GeV/c]≃0.2998B[T]R[m].
(55)
With R≃2.8 km and B≃8.3 T, the beam energy is about 7 TeV, or 14 TeV in the centre of mass. Fields near 16 T would give approximately 13.4 TeV per beam at that radius. These are realistic superconducting-magnet scales; a 27 km circumference includes straight sections, so its bending radius is less than C/(2π). The proton radiation loss near 7 TeV is only a few keV per turn, though high beam currents still create substantial cryogenic heat loads. Magnet technology, aperture, protection and stored beam energy therefore dominate before the electron-like radiation limit.
