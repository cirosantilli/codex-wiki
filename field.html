Field

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 34
A field is a commutative ring with 0=1 in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. Examples include the rational numbers, real numbers, and complex numbers. The integers are a ring but not a field: dividing 1 by 2 leaves them. For a prime number p, the prime field Fp​=Z/pZ is a finite example, because every nonzero residue is coprime to p and hence has a modular inverse.
In F5​, the nonzero inverse pairs are 1−1=1, 2−1=3, 3−1=2, and 4−1=4. Thus 2x=3 has solution x=3⋅3=4 in this field; the real number solution would be 3/2. Dividing by a nonzero coefficient is valid in both settings, but it means division according to the chosen field's operations.
A field has no zero divisors: if ab=0 and a=0, multiplying by a−1 gives b=0. This short argument supports polynomial factorization arguments and pivot division in Gaussian elimination. It does not hold in every commutative ring: modulo 6, the nonzero residues 2 and 3 multiply to zero. The distinction is visible in the figure under algebraic structure.
