Asymptotic expansion (f(z)∼∑k≥0​ak​ϕk​(z))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 61
An asymptotic expansion describes a function near a specified limit by successively smaller scales. Each fixed finite truncation has a remainder smaller than its last retained scale. The central question is how the approximation improves as the parameter approaches the limit; convergence of the entire infinite series at a fixed parameter is a separate question. For the inverse-power scale 1,z−1,z−2,…,
f(z)∼∑k=0∞​ak​z−k(z→+∞)
(1)
means that, for every fixed nonnegative integer m,
f(z)−∑k=0m​ak​z−k=o(z−m).
(2)
The little o notation makes the accuracy requirement precise. With expansions to all orders, this is also equivalent to an O(z−m−1) remainder after the mth term; the implied bound may depend on m.[ref]
For a quick convergent example, the finite geometric series identity gives
1+z−11​=1−z1​+z21​−⋯+zm(−1)m​+1+z−1(−1)m+1z−m−1​.
(3)
For z>1, both the asymptotic expansion as z→+∞ and the ordinary convergent geometric series describe this function. The exact remainder displays the required extra inverse power.
The more surprising example under a divergent factorial series can approximate a convergent integral has a convergent integral but a divergent series at every fixed positive z:
F(z)=∫0∞​1+t/ze−t​dt∼1−z1​+z22!​−z33!​+⋯.
(4)
A few terms can estimate F(10) well, even though adding sufficiently many terms eventually destroys the approximation. Optimal truncation explains where to stop.
This makes asymptotic expansions useful when an exact expression is a complicated integral or differential equation solution, but a parameter is very large or very small. The leading term reveals the scale; corrections reveal which effects matter next. The asymptotic sequence must be specified: integer powers, fractional powers, logarithms, and exponential functions can encode different kinds of behavior. A good finite approximation includes a remainder estimate or an independently checked range of use.

Uniform asymptotic approximation

Words: 53
Asymptotic expansion OurBigBook.com
An approximation is uniform on a specified domain when its error bound holds throughout that domain as the small parameter tends to zero. In a matched asymptotic expansion, adding the inner and outer approximations and subtracting their common overlap produces a composite approximation. Checking only a fixed point away from a boundary layer does not establish uniformity.

Asymptotic power series (f(x)−∑k=0N​ck​(x−a)k=o(∣x−a∣N))

Words: 399 Articles: 1
Asymptotic expansion OurBigBook.com
An asymptotic power series uses powers as its asymptotic sequence. Near a finite point a, the condition in the title applies along the specified domain for every fixed N; at infinity, inverse powers are convenient. It need not be a convergent power series. Its coefficients record the successive limiting responses, as the integral example below demonstrates.
The finite-order Taylor theorem supplies an asymptotic power series for a smooth function near a point, but smoothness does not imply that its Taylor series converges to the function. Define g(x)=e−1/x for x>0 and g(x)=0 for x≤0. Every right-hand derivative is an exponential function times a polynomial in 1/x, tending to zero as x↓0. Consequently g is a smooth function with every derivative zero at the origin. Its all-zero Taylor series misses g(x)>0 to the right. Zero and g also have the same all-zero asymptotic power series on that side, despite being different functions. An asymptotic expansion can determine all its own coefficients without determining the represented function uniquely.

1+t/z1​=∑k=0N−1​(−1)kzktk​+(−1)N1+t/ztN/zN​.

F(z)=SN​(z)+RN​(z),SN​(z)=∑k=0N−1​zk(−1)kk!​,

RN​(z)=zN(−1)N​∫0∞​1+t/ze−ttN​dt,∣RN​(z)∣≤zNN!​​.

This remainder formula proves the asymptotic expansion at every fixed order: RN​=O(z−N)=o(z−N+1) as z→+∞. Its sign also gives alternating bounds: an odd N overestimates F(z), and an even N underestimates it.
At z=10, an independent evaluation of the integral gives F(10)≈0.9156333394. Three retained terms give
S3​(10)=1−0.1+0.02=0.92,∣F(10)−S3​(10)∣<0.006.
(9)
Five retained terms give S5​(10)=0.9164 with error bounded by 5!/105=0.0012. The actual errors are about 0.004367 and 0.000767, respectively. The error bound is conservative, but established without relying on the numerical integral evaluation.
Nevertheless, the formal infinite series diverges at every fixed z>0. The magnitudes Tk​=k!/zk satisfy Tk+1​/Tk​=(k+1)/z, so they eventually increase without bound. In particular, the terms fail the necessary zero-term condition for a convergent series. The finite remainder identity remains valid after convergence has failed; this is precisely what makes the example useful.

Compactified approximation from endpoint asymptotics

Words: 69
Asymptotic expansion OurBigBook.com
Suppose a continuous function F(λ) on [0,∞) has a known power-law tail F(λ)∼aλ−p. Put u=λ/(1+λ) and h(u)=(1+λ)pF(λ). When h extends continuously to u=1, polynomial approximation on [0,1] gives a global approximation F(λ)≈(1+λ)−phN​(u) using only elementary operations. Taylor series at zero and the tail asymptotic expansion can constrain endpoint coefficients. Agreement at the two endpoints alone does not guarantee accuracy in the intervening interval; uniform error control or intermediate calibration is needed.

Optimal truncation

Words: 280
Asymptotic expansion OurBigBook.com
Optimal truncation chooses how many terms to retain for a useful finite approximation. In a divergent asymptotic expansion, increasing the truncation order eventually makes the terms larger rather than smaller. Stopping near a smallest term often gives the best direct approximation, but the actual remainder must be checked. A small last term alone is not a universal error bound.[ref]
For a divergent factorial series can approximate a convergent integral, Tk​=k!/zk decreases while k+1<z and increases while k+1>z. Thus the useful cutoff is near k=z, rather than at arbitrarily large order. At z=10, T9​=T10​=0.00036288 are the two smallest terms. Retaining N=10 terms gives
S10​(10)=0.91545632,∣F(10)−S10​(10)∣≤0.00036288.
(11)
The actual error is about 0.0001770. Retaining twenty terms instead gives S20​(10)≈0.9076137084, with error about 0.008020: more work produces a worse answer.
For this example the bound N!/zN is explicit, so a requested tolerance can be tested before using a truncation. For a different integral, a nearby saddle point or simple pole can change the relevant remainder scale. This is why the smallest-term rule is a useful guide, rather than a replacement for remainder analysis.
The cutoff for factorial terms n!z−n has n=∣z∣+O(1). Better analysis can expose contributions smaller than every retained inverse power, linking optimal truncation to the Stokes phenomenon. Such contributions can be important even when many asymptotic coefficients agree. Directly summing ever more terms does not automatically recover them.

Sectorial fundamental solution

Words: 61
Stokes phenomenon OurBigBook.com
At an unramified irregular singular point with distinct leading eigenvalues, a sectorial fundamental solution is a fundamental matrix whose entries are holomorphic functions asymptotic to a prescribed formal matrix on a sector of the logarithm cover. For competing cubic exponentials, the usual construction uses six overlapping sectors with basic angle π/3. A formal power series need not converge, and two sectorial realizations may differ by exponentially small terms across a sector boundary.

Stokes matrix (Yj+1​=Yj​Sj​)

Asymptotic sequence

Words: 288 Articles: 3
Asymptotic expansion OurBigBook.com
An asymptotic sequence orders the scales against which successive errors are compared. On a specified domain approaching x0​, suppose each ϕn​ is nonzero sufficiently close to the limit and
ϕn​(x)ϕn+1​(x)​⟶0.
(14)
Equivalently, ϕn+1​=o(ϕn​) in little o notation. The notation
f∼∑n≥0​an​ϕn​
(15)
means that for every N≥0,
f−∑n=0N​an​ϕn​=o(ϕN​).
(16)
Each N stays fixed while the parameter tends to its limit.[ref]
As z→+∞, 1,z−1,z−2,… is an asymptotic sequence, because each adjacent ratio is 1/z. Fractional scales 1,z−1/2,z−1,… work too, with ratio z−1/2. By contrast, 1,1+z−1,z−1,… fails at its first pair: the second scale is not smaller than the first. Ordering by written position alone does not create a hierarchy of sizes.
Successive division of the remainder by ϕN​ uniquely recovers every coefficient:
aN​=limx→x0​​ϕN​(x)f(x)−∑n=0N−1​an​ϕn​(x)​.
(17)
If two proposed expansions first differ at order N, subtracting them and dividing by ϕN​ would give a nonzero constant tending to zero, a contradiction. This is uniqueness of the coefficients in a fixed scale, not uniqueness of the represented function.
For the factorial integral example, the first three limiting responses are limF(z)=1, limz(F(z)−1)=−1, and limz2(F(z)−1+z−1)=2. They extract exactly the coefficients 1,−1,2!. Choosing an asymptotic sequence therefore turns “the next small correction” into a precise and reusable operation.
