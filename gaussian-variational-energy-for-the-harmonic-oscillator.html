Gaussian variational energy for the harmonic oscillator (E(σ)=1/(8σ2)+σ2/2)

Use the quantum harmonic oscillator in units with reduced Planck constant ℏ=1, mass m=1, and angular frequency ω=1. Its Hamiltonian operator and trial Gaussian wave packet are
H=−21​dx2d2​+2x2​,ψσ​(x)=(2πσ2)−1/4exp(−4σ2x2​),σ>0.
(98)
This is the same Gaussian wave packet used for the Heisenberg uncertainty relation: ∣ψσ​∣2 is a normal distribution with mean zero and variance σ2. The Gaussian integral gives wavefunction normalization and ⟨x2⟩=σ2. These smooth, rapidly decaying trials lie in the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator form domain and even in the operator domain.
Since ψσ′​=−xψσ​/(2σ2), integration by parts with vanishing boundary terms gives the kinetic energy and potential energy:
⟨T⟩=21​∫R​∣ψσ′​∣2dx=8σ21​,⟨V⟩=21​⟨x2⟩=2σ2​,E(σ)=8σ21​+2σ2​.
(99)
Narrow trials cost kinetic energy, while broad trials cost potential energy. The exact difference
E(σ)−21​=8σ2(2σ2−1)2​≥0
(100)
shows that the unique optimal width is σ2=1/2, with E=1/2. At σ=1/2 and σ=1, the trial energies are both 5/8=0.625; the kinetic energy and potential energy contributions exchange places.
Minimizing within this trial family alone establishes E0​≤1/2. To establish exactness, use the Heisenberg uncertainty relation to obtain Eψ​≥1/2 for every admissible quantum state, as in its existing oscillator example. Together the bounds give E0​=1/2. Direct substitution also yields Hψ1/2​​=ψ1/2​​/2, agreeing with the Gaussian eigenstate for a quadratic potential. This is a useful distinction: the variational method supplies the upper bound, while the Heisenberg uncertainty relation supplies the complementary lower bound.
