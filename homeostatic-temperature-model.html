Homeostatic temperature model (τx˙=d−x−u)

 Home Biology Physiology Homeostasis
OurBigBook.com Words: 474
Here is an explicitly synthetic mathematical model of a warming disturbance and its compensatory cooling. It is a teaching analogy for thermoregulation, not a measured human heat budget. Write x=(T−Tr​)/ΔT, where T is a lumped internal temperature, Tr​ is a fixed reference and ΔT>0 is a chosen temperature scale. Let d be an additional heating rate and u an additional cooling rate, both scaled to the same reference rate. All three are dimensionless variables, while τ>0 is a time constant in the chosen time units. The ordinary differential equation is
τx˙=d−x−u,u=kx,k≥0.
(2)
The term −x idealizes increased passive heat transfer with increased internal temperature, around the baseline. The term −u opposes warming through proportional control with feedback gain k. This local signed model does not treat a negative u as negative sweating: it would represent decreased cooling relative to baseline. Changes of the environment and multiple body compartments are omitted.
For a constant disturbance d=D and initial condition x(0)=0, the exponential function solution is
x(t)=1+kD​(1−e−(1+k)t/τ),x∗​=1+kD​,u∗​=1+kkD​.
(3)
Substitution checks the ordinary differential equation directly. Increasing k reduces the lasting offset and, in this instantaneous model, shortens the response time constant to τ/(1+k). It does not make x∗​ zero for a nonzero disturbance and finite k: a persistent cooling command requires a persistent displacement under proportional control. At the equilibrium point, the extra heating is balanced by passive loss x∗​ plus active cooling u∗​. Zero change of temperature means balanced rates, rather than zero rates, consistent with conservation of energy.
The first diagram uses D=1, τ=4, and a pulse from t=2 to t=10, comparing k=0 with k=3. The latter approaches x∗​=1/4 during the pulse, compared with 1 without active feedback control. After the pulse ends, each displacement decays as x(10)e−(1+k)(t−10)/τ. These are computed curves, not physiological measurements. The k=0 curve still has passive loss; it is not a claim that an unregulated body warms without limit.
Real physiological effectors take time to respond. To isolate that issue, set τ=1 and replace the instantaneous command by another ordinary differential equation:
x˙=1−x−u,u˙=4x−u,x(0)=u(0)=0.
(4)
Here the disturbance remains 1 from t=0 onward and the effector time constant is also 1. The equilibrium point is still (x∗​,u∗​)=(1/5,4/5), but the characteristic polynomial is (λ+1)2+4. Its eigenvalues −1±2i give decaying oscillations and asymptotic stability. The exact response is
x(t)=51​+e−t(−51​cos(2t)+52​sin(2t)),u(t)=54​+e−t(−54​cos(2t)−52​sin(2t)).
(5)
In particular, x˙=e−tcos(2t), so the first maximum occurs at t=π/4, with x=1/5+(2/5)e−π/4≃0.3824. The response exceeds its eventual displacement while remaining above its baseline. The return has the same negative sign as before; the extra state changes the timing. A finite effector time constant is distinct from the fixed time lag in the delayed negative feedback model.
