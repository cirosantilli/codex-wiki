Nonzero residue permutation (x↦ax(modp))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory Fermat little theorem
OurBigBook.com Words: 297
For a prime number p and p∤a, multiplication by a gives a permutation of the p−1 nonzero residue classes. For a=3 modulo seven, the representatives 1,2,3,4,5,6 map to 3,6,2,5,1,4: the same collection in a different order. Repeating this permutation from one visits all six representatives before returning.
For a=2 modulo seven, the permutation cycles are instead (1,2,4) and (3,6,5). The powers of two visit only the permutation cycle containing one, so their multiplicative order is three. Both permutation cycles return after three steps; applying the permutation six times still fixes every nonzero residue class. Fermat's little theorem guarantees a return at six, not that every base visits every nonzero residue class.
More generally, every permutation cycle of this map has length equal to the multiplicative order of a: starting at a nonzero residue class x, a return means akx≡x(modp), and its modular multiplicative inverse allows cancellation of x. Thus the p−1 nonzero residue classes split into equal-length permutation cycles, which also explains the divisibility of the multiplicative order into p−1.
The product argument gives a short proof outline. Since multiplication by a is a permutation of the nonzero residue classes,
