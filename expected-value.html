Expected value (E[X])

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 6k Articles: 79
An expected value is the probability-weighted arithmetic mean of a real-valued random variable. On a probability space (Ω,F,P), its general definition is the Lebesgue integral
E[X]=∫Ω​XdP=∫R​xdμX​(x),
(1)
where μX​ is the probability distribution of X. For an integrable random variable, this is a finite real number. With a probability mass function pX​, use ∑x​xpX​(x); with a probability density function fX​, use ∫R​xfX​(x)dx. These are two ways of computing the same weighted average, rather than different definitions for different experiments.
For example, suppose X takes the values −1,2,8 with probabilities 1/2,3/10,1/5. Then
E[X]=(−1)21​+2103​+851​=1017​=1.7.​
(2)
The most likely outcome is −1, and 1.7 is not a possible outcome at all. The expected value summarizes all outcomes and their weights; it need not predict the result of one trial. Geometrically, placing masses equal to the probability masses at the outcome positions gives a center of mass at 1.7, since ∑x​pX​(x)(x−1.7)=0. This balance-point interpretation also explains the connection to a weighted arithmetic mean. [ref].
For the dimensionless random variable Y with probability density function fY​(y)=2y on [0,1], the expected value is E[Y]=∫01​y(2y)dy=2/3. Integrating the probability density function itself gives one; to compute the expected value, multiply by the outcome first. [ref].
Why is this useful? Linearity of expectation lets us compute an average total by adding average contributions, even for dependent random variables. And for independent and identically distributed integrable random variables, the strong law of large numbers says their sample mean approaches their common expected value almost surely. The theorem supplies the link to repeated observations; the weighted-average definition itself does not promise that any particular finite run will be close to the expected value.
There is a useful historical connection to valuing uncertain rewards. In his 1657 De ratiociniis in ludo aleae, Christiaan Huygens assigns the value (a+b)/2 to equally likely rewards a,b, and (pa+qb)/(p+q) when they have p,q equally likely chances. These are the finite weighted-average formulas for expected value. His opening discussion asks how to divide the stakes when a game stops before completion, connecting probability to a concrete question about an unfinished contest. [ref].

Law of the unconscious statistician (E[g(X)]=∫g(x)dμX​(x))

Words: 105
Expected value OurBigBook.com
The law of the unconscious statistician computes the expected value of a measurable function of a random variable directly from the original probability distribution. If g(X) is an integrable random variable, then
E[g(X)]=∫R​g(x)dμX​(x).
(3)
Use ∑x​g(x)pX​(x) for a probability mass function, or ∫R​g(x)fX​(x)dx for a probability density function. There is no need to derive the probability distribution of g(X) first. The identity is the integration rule for a pushforward measure, and also holds with an infinite value when g is nonnegative. [ref].
For X taking −1,2,8 with probabilities 1/2,3/10,1/5, choosing g(x)=x2 gives E[X2]=21​+4103​+6451​=14.5. Its variance is therefore 14.5−1.72=11.61. Squaring the expected value would instead give 2.89, because squaring does not commute with averaging.

Outer expectation (E∗Z=inf{EY:Y≥Z, Y measurable})

Words: 72
Expected value OurBigBook.com
For a bounded possibly nonmeasurable real function Z on a probability space, its outer expectation is the infimum of expected values of measurable majorants. Its inner expectation is E∗​Z=−E∗(−Z). They agree with the usual expected value for measurable Z. This permits function-space convergence in distribution for an empirical distribution process whose paths are not Borel-measurable in a nonseparable norm topology: both expectations of each bounded continuous test functional must approach its ordinary expectation under the tight measurable limiting law.

Mean-zero function (Ef=0)

Expected value OurBigBook.com
A mean-zero function has expectation zero with respect to the specified probability measure. On a finite set with uniform measure this means ∑x​f(x)=0. A balanced subset indicator is an example. In Fourier analysis on a finite group, this is equivalent to vanishing of the component at the trivial representation.

Integrable random variable (E∣X∣<∞)

Words: 170
Expected value OurBigBook.com
A real or complex random variable X is integrable when E∣X∣<∞. This is integrability with respect to its probability measure, and ensures that its expected value is finite. A bounded random variable is always an integrable random variable; so is the example taking −1,2,8, for which E∣X∣=2.7.
For a real random variable, put X+​=max(X,0) and X−​=max(−X,0), its positive part and negative part. Each has a nonnegative Lebesgue integral, possibly infinite. The expression E[X]=E[X+​]−E[X−​] is defined when at least one of these two expected values is finite; if both are infinite, the expected value is undefined. Integrability makes both finite. A nonnegative random variable can have expected value +∞, even though it is not an integrable random variable.
The standard Cauchy distribution is a useful warning. With its probability density function f(x)=1/[π(1+x2)], the positive contribution is ∫0R​xf(x)dx=log(1+R2)/(2π), which tends to infinity; the negative contribution has the same magnitude. Integrating symmetrically over [−R,R] gives zero for each R, but this cancellation does not define an expected value. The Cauchy sample mean has an unchanged distribution, showing why the integrability hypothesis in the strong law of large numbers matters.
For an integrable random variable, conditional expectation preserves integrability, and linearity of expectation can safely combine finite averages without forming ∞−∞.

The nth raw moment of a random variable X is E[Xn] when this expectation exists.
More generally, a moment is an integral weighted by a power of a coordinate against a measure or density; raw, central, and standardized moments are distinguished by the coordinate and normalization used.

Indicator random variable (1A​)

Words: 79
Expected value OurBigBook.com
An indicator random variable is the indicator function of an event A in a probability space: 1A​ is one when A occurs and zero otherwise. It has a Bernoulli distribution with parameter p=P(A), so E[1A​]=p and Var(1A​)=p(1−p). Even an event described without numbers becomes a numerical random variable in this way.
For a finite collection of events, their indicator random variables add up to the number that occur. Linearity of expectation therefore turns an average count into a sum of probabilities, without requiring independence. The fixed point count of a uniform random permutation provides an example where the indicators are dependent random variables.

Tail-sum formula (E[Y]=∫0∞​P(Y>t)dt)

Words: 805 Articles: 2
Expected value OurBigBook.com
The tail-sum formula expresses an expected value as the area under a survival function. For every nonnegative random variable Y,
E[Y]=∫0∞​P(Y>t)dt.​
(4)
This Lebesgue integral and the expected value may both be +∞; a probability density function is unnecessary. For a random variable N taking values in the nonnegative integers, the corresponding sum is
E[N]=k=1∑∞​P(N≥k)=k=0∑∞​P(N>k).​
(5)
The events {N>k} and {N≥k+1} coincide because N takes integer values. Thus the strict sum starts at zero, while the inclusive sum starts at one.[ref]
For an original example, let the probability mass function of N be
P(N=0)=51​,P(N=1)=103​,P(N=3)=21​.
(6)
Its expected value computed directly is 0(1/5)+1(3/10)+3(1/2)=9/5=1.8. The strict tail probabilities at thresholds 0,1,2,3 are 4/5,1/2,1/2,0: their sum is again 1.8. There is no probability mass at two, yet the event {N>2} still has probability 1/2. The survival function has heights 4/5,1/2,1/2 on the successive intervals [0,1),[1,2),[2,3); each interval has length one, so its integral is the same sum.
The indexing matters numerically. Starting the inclusive sum at zero adds P(N≥0)=1, giving 2.8 for this example. Starting the strict sum at one omits P(N>0)=4/5, giving 1. For a general nonnegative random variable, replacing > with ≥ inside the integral is harmless: the difference is P(Y=t), nonzero at only a countable set of thresholds. For each positive integer m, at most m thresholds can have probability mass at least 1/m; their countable union contains all thresholds with positive probability mass and has Lebesgue measure zero. This reasoning concerns an integral, not equality of the two survival function conventions at every point.
For a continuous original example, take the uniform distribution on the interval [0,4]. Its survival function is S(t)=1−t/4 for 0≤t≤4 and zero for t>4. The area is a triangle of base four and height one:
E[Y]=∫04​(1−4t​)dt=2.
(7)
Directly integrating against the probability density function gives the same expected value, ∫04​y(1/4)dy=2. The integral of the probability density function alone is one; it is the survival function whose area is the expected value.
The proof counts layers of each outcome. For a realized value y≥0, the indicator function 1{y>t}​ equals one over an interval of length y, so
Y=∫0∞​1{Y>t}​dt,E[Y]=∫0∞​E[1{Y>t}​]dt=∫0∞​P(Y>t)dt.
(8)
Tonelli theorem permits the interchange because the indicator function is nonnegative; no finite expected value is assumed. Similarly, an outcome N=n contributes one indicator random variable for each of the n events {N≥k} that occur:
N=∑k=1∞​1{N≥k}​,E[N]=∑k=1∞​E[1{N≥k}​]=∑k=1∞​P(N≥k).
(9)
Again Tonelli theorem applies to the nonnegative sum, or equivalently the monotone convergence theorem applies to its partial sums. This exchanges counting layers within each outcome with averaging across outcomes. The existing integer-valued hypothesis in the tail-sum formula explains what changes when the values are not integers; the tail integral formula for moments weights the layers to obtain higher moments.
For an integrable random variable X that can have either sign, apply the nonnegative formula separately to its positive part X+​=max(X,0) and negative part X−​=max(−X,0):
E[X]=∫0∞​P(X>t)dt−∫0∞​P(X<−t)dt.
(10)
Integrability makes both integrals finite. More generally the difference defines an extended expected value when at least one is finite; if both are infinite, subtraction does not define an expected value.[ref]
Tail probabilities are often easier to measure or bound than a whole probability density function. For a device's survival time, the survival function records the probability of still operating at each threshold; its integral gives the mean operating time. A finite observation window naturally gives the expected value of a capped nonnegative random variable. For a mathematical bound, P(Y>t)≤e−t/2 for all t≥0 implies E[Y]≤∫0∞​e−t/2dt=2. The tail-sum formula turns control of a whole family of events into control of an average.

Integer-valued hypothesis in the tail-sum formula (∑m≥0​P(X>m)=E⌈X⌉(X≥0))

Words: 57
Tail-sum formula OurBigBook.com
For a nonnegative integer-valued variable, X=∑m≥0​1{X>m}​ and the tail-sum formula gives EX=∑m≥0​P(X>m). For arbitrary positive real values, that integer-indexed sum equals E⌈X⌉ instead. Two independent constants equal to 3/2 demonstrate that the product of such tail sums need not equal the tail sum of their product: the two sides are four and three.

Expected value of a capped nonnegative random variable (E[min(Y,a)]=∫0a​P(Y>t)dt)

Words: 116
Tail-sum formula OurBigBook.com
For a nonnegative random variable Y and a fixed cap a≥0, the tail-sum formula gives
E[min(Y,a)]=∫0a​P(Y>t)dt,E[(Y−a)+​]=∫a∞​P(Y>t)dt.
(11)
Below the cap, the events {min(Y,a)>t} and {Y>t} coincide; at and above the cap, the former event is empty. For the second identity, the positive part (Y−a)+​ exceeds t≥0 precisely when Y>a+t. Thus the two integrals divide the area under the survival function, just as Y=min(Y,a)+(Y−a)+​ divides each outcome. The capped expected value is always finite, while the excess expected value may be infinite.
For the uniform distribution on the interval [0,4] and cap a=2, the capped expected value is ∫02​(1−t/4)dt=3/2 and the excess expected value is ∫24​(1−t/4)dt=1/2. Directly averaging the capped values also gives ∫02​y(1/4)dy+2P(Y>2)=1/2+1=3/2. Capping therefore measures a different average from the uncapped expected value two, even when both are finite.

Linearity of expectation (E[aX+bY]=aE[X]+bE[Y])

Words: 254 Articles: 1
Expected value OurBigBook.com
For integrable random variables X1​,…,Xn​ on the same probability space and fixed real numbers a1​,…,an​, linearity of expectation states
E[∑i=1n​ai​Xi​]=∑i=1n​ai​E[Xi​].
(12)
The random variables can be dependent random variables. The formula follows by the linearity of integration; a finite linear combination of integrable random variables remains integrable. For example, if X takes −1,2,8 with probabilities 1/2,3/10,1/5, then X and 2X are dependent random variables, yet E[X+2X]=3E[X]=5.1.
An especially useful application counts how many events occur. Put Ii​=1Ai​​, an indicator random variable, and N=∑i​Ii​. Since E[Ii​]=P(Ai​), we get E[N]=∑i​P(Ai​). There is no need to compute the whole probability distribution of N, or to assume independence. For a uniform random permutation of n≥1 objects, each object stays fixed with probability 1/n, so the fixed point count of a uniform random permutation has expected value one for every n. [ref].
Linearity of expectation concerns addition and multiplication by fixed scalars. Products instead satisfy E[XY]=E[X]E[Y] under the hypotheses of expectation of a product of independent random variables. For the example above, E[X2]=14.5, whereas (E[X])2=2.89. Applying a nonlinear function before averaging can therefore change the answer substantially; use the law of the unconscious statistician to compute that average.

Expectation of an absolutely summable random series (E[∑i​Xi​]=∑i​E[Xi​]if ∑i​E∣Xi​∣<∞)

Words: 81
Linearity of expectation OurBigBook.com
If random variables Xi​ satisfy ∑i​E∣Xi​∣<∞, then ∑i​Xi​ converges absolutely almost surely, is integrable, and its expected value equals ∑i​E[Xi​]. Indeed, the monotone convergence theorem gives E[∑i​∣Xi​∣]=∑i​E∣Xi​∣<∞, so that nonnegative sum is finite almost surely. It dominates every finite partial sum in absolute value; the dominated convergence theorem then justifies passing the limit through the expected value. No independence is required.
For example, if ∣Xi​∣≤Mqi for a fixed M and 0<q<1, the geometric series bound proves the hypothesis directly. This is useful when repeated observations are multiplied by geometrically shrinking distances or weights.

Variance of a sample mean (Var(X)=σ2/n)

Words: 185
Expected value OurBigBook.com
Let X1​,…,Xn​ be an IID sample of real-valued random variables with finite expected value μ and finite variance σ2, where n≥1. Its sample mean X=n−1∑i​Xi​ satisfies
E[X]=μ,Var(X)=nσ2​.​
(13)
Linearity of expectation gives the first identity. The variance of a sum and vanishing cross covariances give Var(∑i​Xi​)=nσ2; scaling by 1/n scales the variance by 1/n2. Pairwise independent random variables with the same expected value and variance suffice for these identities, even without full independence. Grinstead and Snell state the corresponding finite-variance independent-trials result.[ref]
The standard deviation of the sample mean is σ/n​. Four times as many observations halve this scale; they do not divide it by four. In the fair Bernoulli distribution example, σ=1/2, so sample sizes 4 and 64 give standard deviations 1/4 and 1/16, respectively. The common-coin averaging example shows why merely repeating observations with a common marginal law does not justify this calculation.
Chebyshev's inequality turns the variance identity into a probability bound: for ε>0,
P(∣X−μ∣≥ε)≤nε2σ2​.
(14)
This proves convergence in probability of the sample mean to μ in this finite-variance setting, a form of the weak law of large numbers. It is an upper bound, so values above one convey no improvement over the universal probability bound one. Cauchy sample mean stability lies outside these hypotheses.
