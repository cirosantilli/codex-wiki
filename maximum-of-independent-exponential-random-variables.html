Maximum of independent exponential random variables (Mn​=max1≤i≤n​Xi​,Xi​∼Exp(λ))

Let n≥1 be an integer and let X1​,…,Xn​ be independent and identically distributed random variables with an exponential distribution of rate λ>0. Their sample maximum Mn​ is nonnegative. Substituting the exponential distribution's cumulative distribution function into the distribution functions of independent minima and maxima gives the exact law
P(Mn​≤x)={(1−e−λx)n,0,​x≥0,x<0.​
(3)
For example, at x=(log2)/λ each observation is below the threshold with probability 1/2, but all n observations are below it with probability 2−n. Thus an individual observation's median becomes an increasingly unlikely upper bound for the whole batch. The general order statistic density in Siegrist's treatment, with k=n, also gives this law on integration. [ref].
Solving (1−e−λx)n=p gives the exact quantile function, for 0<p<1,
qn​(p)=−λ1​log(1−p1/n),mn​=qn​(1/2)=−λ1​log(1−2−1/n).
(4)
In a toy model with λ=1s−1, the median maximum is 0.693147 seconds for n=1, 2.703555 seconds for n=10, and 4.975147 seconds for n=100. All three use the exact distribution function; the increase comes from taking a maximum over more observations. The n=2 case connects to the existing maximum of two exponentials as a scaled sum.
The Gumbel distribution explains the logarithmic shift visible here: after subtracting (logn)/λ and measuring deviations in units of 1/λ, the distribution functions approach one fixed shape. The next derivation makes that convergence in distribution explicit.
