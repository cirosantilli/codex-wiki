Helmholtz free energy (F)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Thermodynamic potential Thermodynamic free energy
OurBigBook.com Words: 273
The Helmholtz free energy is
F=U−TS,
(1)
where U is internal energy, also denoted E elsewhere here, T is absolute temperature, and S is entropy. For the closed system of fixed composition and only pressure-volume work described above, spontaneous relaxation at fixed temperature and volume cannot increase F. Stable thermodynamic equilibrium is a global minimum over the permitted alternatives, including different thermodynamic phases. A rigid vessel fixes the total volume; it does not require each coexisting thermodynamic phase to retain its own volume.
For a constructed comparison at 300K, suppose one candidate has 1.5kJ more internal energy but 6JK−1 more entropy than another at the same temperature and volume. Its difference is
ΔF=1500−300(6)=−300J.
(2)
Thus the candidate with higher internal energy has lower Helmholtz free energy. This is the practical reason for retaining the entropy term when selecting a state.
For equilibrium states within a smooth branch of fixed composition, the first law of thermodynamics gives dU=TdS−pdV, hence
dF=−SdT−pdV,S=−(∂T∂F​)V​,p=−(∂V∂F​)T​.
(3)
These derivatives extract entropy and pressure; the differential is not an evolution equation for relaxation. If chemical species amounts ni​ vary, add ∑i​μi​dni​, with molar chemical potentials μi​. A closed system can still change chemical species amounts through chemical reactions; the fixed-composition simplification then needs the conserved-inventory constraints of chemical equilibrium. See MIT 5.60, Lecture 14, fundamental equations and chemical potentials.
The minimum principle has a short explanation. At fixed volume, no boundary work occurs, so Q=ΔU. A thermal reservoir at positive temperature T loses entropy Q/T. Consequently
ΔStotal​=ΔS−TΔU​=−TΔF​≥0,
(4)
by the Second law of thermodynamics. Here both endpoint states share the imposed temperature. See MIT 5.60, Lecture 12, criteria for spontaneous change.
The microscopic counterpart is F=−kB​TlnZ in the canonical ensemble, with Boltzmann constant kB​ and partition function Z. The partition function examples compute the balance between internal energy and entropy; the chemical equilibrium example already displays an ideal-mixture Helmholtz free energy minimum.
