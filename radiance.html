Radiance (L)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Radiometry
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 10
Radiance resolves both position and direction: it is radiant flux per projected area and per solid angle. For a surface element of area dA, and a small outgoing cone dΩ at angle 0≤θ<π/2 to its outward unit normal,
d2Φ=L(x,s)cosθdAdΩ,[L]=Wm−2sr−1.
(10)
Here x locates the patch and s is a unit vector along the outgoing direction. The factor dAcosθ is the patch's apparent area viewed along that direction. The same local definition applies to transmitted or received radiation, choosing the positive projected area for the crossing direction. [ref].
For a planar patch of area A with spatially uniform, direction-independent radiance L, integrating over the patch gives
I(θ)=LAcosθ.
(11)
Take L=10Wm−2sr−1 and A=0.020m2. The radiant intensities are I(0)=0.20Wsr−1 and I(60∘)=0.10Wsr−1. A sufficiently narrow 0.010sr cone therefore carries approximately 2.0mW or 1.0mW, respectively. Both views have the same radiance L: the oblique view presents half the projected area. This is Lambert's cosine law, not a loss of radiance with viewing angle.
Radiance is useful because it separates a source's local directional output from the geometry of an observation. Integrating over outgoing directions gives radiant exitance; integrating incoming radiance at a receiver gives irradiance. In the stationary vacuum setting of vacuum conservation of specific intensity, a resolved uniform source retains its radiance along each light ray, while the radiant flux received from the whole source can decrease as its apparent solid angle shrinks.
