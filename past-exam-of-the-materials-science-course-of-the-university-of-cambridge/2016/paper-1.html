Use y/b horizontally and z/c vertically, and label each molecular center by its fractional height x/a along the viewing direction. The four centers in one periodic cell are (0,0) with height 0 and orientation A, (1/2,0) with height 1/2 and orientation A, (0,1/2) with height 1/2 and orientation B, and (1/2,1/2) with height 0 and orientation B. Boundary images complete the projected rectangle. The A and B bonds have opposite slopes; the atoms in each bond are indistinguishable.
The height labels matter: a symmetry of the unlabelled projection need not be a translation or reflection symmetry of the three-dimensional crystal. The drawing uses the given 0.267nm molecular bond length and an illustrative inclination, since no exact inclination angle is specified.

This is a C-centered orthorhombic lattice. The translation (1/2,1/2,0) takes each A molecule to another A molecule and each B molecule to another B molecule. Thus the conventional cell has two lattice points.
A two-molecule crystal motif can be chosen as A at (0,0,0) and B at (1/2,0,1/2). Attaching that same motif to both C-centered lattice points produces all four molecules, hence eight iodine atoms per conventional cell.
The centers alone appear face-centered, but (1/2,0,1/2) exchanges A and B orientations. It is not a translation symmetry of the oriented molecular solid. An F-centered assignment would discard essential motif information.

There are 21​ screw axes parallel to [100] through (y/b,z/c)=(1/4,0),(3/4,0),(1/4,1/2),(3/4,1/2), with periodic repeats.
For the first axis the operation in fractional coordinates is
(x,y,z)⟼(x+1/2,1/2−y,−z).
(1)
It rotates by 180∘ about the axis and translates by a/2. For example, it sends the A center at (0,0,0) to the A center at (1/2,1/2,0), and sends B at (1/2,0,1/2) to B at (0,1/2,1/2) modulo integer translations. A half-turn preserves each unoriented diatomic bond. A second application gives one full a translation.
There are also ordinary twofold axes through the molecular-center rows; those should not be confused with the screw axes halfway between the corresponding rows.

The relevant planes contain [100], so their normals lie in the bc plane. The symmetry operations can be checked directly on the height-labelled molecules:

Eutectic solidification is the coupled transformation of a liquid into two distinct solid phases at the eutectic temperature and composition, L→α+β. At fixed pressure in a binary system the three-phase equilibrium is invariant. An exactly eutectic liquid can therefore finish freezing at one temperature, instead of over a broad liquid-plus-solid interval.
Eutectic compositions are useful in joining alloys, for example silver-copper brazing filler: the comparatively low melting temperature and narrow freezing interval help liquid flow into a joint and then solidify. Alternating growth of the two phases often produces a fine lamellar eutectic microstructure. The eutectic is a two-phase aggregate, not a new single solid phase.

At equilibrium at room temperature the phases are pure Ag and pure Cu, with mass fractions 0.80 and 0.20. A representative solidification structure contains primary Ag-rich grains or dendrites surrounded by a finer two-phase eutectic aggregate. Subsequent equilibrium solute rejection modifies both constituents as they cool.
The 42.1% primary-α and 57.9% eutectic-constituent fractions calculated at the eutectic arrest describe how the liquid froze. They are not the final pure-Ag and pure-Cu phase fractions. For example, just below the arrest the total copper-rich phase fraction is (20−9)/(92−9)=11/83=0.1325; it rises to 0.20 as solubility is lost on further equilibrium cooling. The distinction separates a microstructural constituent from a thermodynamic phase.
Faster solidification usually refines the dendrite and eutectic spacings. If solid-state diffusion cannot keep up, composition gradients, supersaturated solids and delayed precipitation can persist. Their exact fractions then depend on the thermal history; faster cooling does not change the alloy's overall 20% copper inventory. Complete long-term equilibration would restore the same equilibrium phase compositions and mass fractions.

a OurBigBook.com
The alloy shear yield stress can be roughly 70/0.78≃90 times the stated pure-aluminium critical resolved shear stress. Strengthening raises the stress required for dislocation glide rather than requiring homogeneous shearing of a perfect crystal.
  • Solid-solution strengthening: substitutional solutes create elastic and chemical disturbances that interact with dislocations. A moving dislocation must escape or drag these disturbances.
  • Precipitation hardening: suitably spaced second-phase particles obstruct glide. Small shearable precipitates resist cutting; larger nonshearable obstacles require dislocation bowing and bypass. Particle spacing and coherency matter, so overaging can reduce strength.
  • Work hardening: prior plastic deformation increases dislocation density. Intersections, junctions and forest dislocations impede subsequent glide; a common scaling is Δτ∼αGbρd​​.
  • Grain boundary strengthening: smaller grains shorten the distance over which dislocations can pile up and make slip transfer across differently oriented grains more difficult. In its ordinary regime, the Hall-Petch relation is σy​=σ0​+ky​d−1/2.
These mechanisms can coexist. The numerical comparison is an order-of-magnitude comparison of shear strengths; it is not a conversion of the alloy's shear yield stress directly into a tensile yield stress.

For a spherical homogeneous nucleus, the capillarity approximation gives
ΔG(r)=4πr2γ+34πr3​ΔgV​.
(36)
The first term penalizes creating an interface; below the melting point the bulk free-energy change per volume ΔgV​ is negative and favors growth. Near Tm​, neglect the temperature dependence of the latent heat. Since the free-energy difference vanishes at Tm​,
ΔgV​≃ΔHV​Tm​ΔT​,ΔT=Tm​−T>0,ΔHV​<0on freezing.
(37)

drdΔG​=8πrγ+4πr2ΔgV​=0,r∗=−ΔgV​2γ​=−ΔHV​ΔT2γTm​​.​
(38)
The nonzero stationary point is a maximum, since ΔG′′(r∗)=−8πγ<0. Subcritical nuclei lower free energy by shrinking; supercritical nuclei lower it by growing. The positive radius and its inverse-supercooling dependence follow from the negative freezing enthalpy. This derivation assumes an isotropic, size-independent interfacial energy and a bulk-like spherical nucleus; it is not a molecular-scale kinetic theory.

Material creep is time-dependent permanent deformation under sustained stress. Its rate is often appreciable at a large homologous temperature T/Tm​, because diffusion and obstacle-escape processes become rapid. In an activated regime a useful form is
ε˙=Aσnd−pexp[−Qc​/(RT)],
(39)
with additional elastic, geometric and temperature prefactors absorbed into A for the chosen approximation. The activation energy belongs to the rate-controlling process: bulk diffusion, grain-boundary diffusion and dislocation motion need not have the same value.
  • A: power-law dislocation material creep. Dislocations glide and climb around obstacles; vacancy transport enables climb and recovery. The rate is strongly stress-dependent, typically with n several times greater than one. It dominates the higher-stress, hot field below the glide-strength boundary.
  • B: Nabarro-Herring creep. Stress-directed vacancy diffusion through the grain interiors transfers material between differently stressed boundaries, extending grains along tension. In the simple equiaxed-grain model ε˙∝DV​σ/(Td2). It occupies the high-temperature, low-stress field.
  • C: Coble creep. The analogous transport runs predominantly along grain boundaries. The characteristic scaling is ε˙∝δDGB​σ/(Td3), where δ is an effective boundary thickness. The lower activation cost of boundary diffusion makes it competitive at lower temperature than bulk-diffusion material creep.
Boundary sliding can accommodate grain-shape changes during diffusional material creep; it is not a reason to identify either diffusion field as dislocation glide. The supplied map's A/B/C placement, including the boundary between B and C, determines these assignments. Its numerical contours apply to the material state used to construct that map, not to arbitrary grain sizes.

Lower the operating temperature or stress, suppress diffusion, and remove or stabilize the paths that accommodate material creep. Increasing the melting temperature can reduce T/Tm​ at a specified operating temperature. Chemistry that reduces diffusivity or impedes dislocation climb can reduce the material creep rate; stable precipitates or dispersoids can resist glide and climb, provided they do not dissolve or coarsen rapidly in service.
Large grains reduce both Nabarro-Herring and Coble creep, according to their d−2 and d−3 dependencies. Directional solidification can reduce boundaries normal to the dominant tensile stress, while a single crystal removes grain-boundary transport and sliding from the ordinary polycrystal mechanism inventory. It does not remove bulk diffusion or dislocation material creep.
This is why room-temperature grain-refinement strengthening is not automatically a high-temperature material creep strategy: fine grains create more boundary transport paths. Any protective microstructure must remain stable at the service temperature, and the design must also avoid stress concentrations and unwanted thermal loading.

At height z above the free lower end, a uniform vertical filament carries the weight of length z. With cross-sectional area A,
F(z)=ρAgz,σ(z)=ρgz,σmax​=ρgL.​
(40)
The maximum stress is at the top and is independent of diameter. The diameter cancels because both mass and load-bearing area scale with A. Neglecting self-heating gradients, evaporation and initial fabrication stress, this top point sets the first creep-strain limit.
For a clearly labelled external reference estimate, take ρ=1.93×104kgm−3. Then σmax​=1.89×104Pa=0.0189MPa; the area is 3.14×10−8m2 and the total suspended mass is about 6.06×10−5kg. The density is independently tabulated, not invented as a supplied 2016 value.[ref]
Use the original map's top temperature axis at 2500∘C and its right-hand tensile-stress axis, so no uncertain shear-stress conversion or additional shear modulus is required.
