A Bravais lattice is a set of equivalent points generated by integer combinations of three independent translation vectors. A crystal motif is the arrangement of atoms attached identically to every lattice point. Together they specify the periodic crystal structure: lattice translations repeat the motif without changing its internal arrangement.
A unit cell is a region whose translations tile the structure. A primitive unit cell contains one lattice point after sharing boundary points with neighboring cells. A conventional cell can contain more than one lattice point to display the crystal's symmetry with simple axes and angles; for example, a conventional face-centered cube has four lattice points but a less visually symmetric primitive cell has one.
Rotational symmetry compatible with a periodic three-dimensional lattice has order 1,2,3,4 or 6, as expressed by the crystallographic restriction theorem. A crystallographic fivefold axis is excluded from an ordinary periodic crystal. This statement concerns exact lattice symmetry; it does not exclude local fivefold atomic arrangements or nonperiodic ordered structures.

The 020 reflection is extinguished by the diamond basis, even though it satisfies the face-centered lattice parity rule. Write the diamond structure as an FCC lattice with identical atoms at basis positions (0,0,0) and (1/4,1/4,1/4).

For a conventional initially random, untextured piezoceramic, orientational averaging suppresses the macroscopic linear response. Electrical poling of ferroelectric domains is the usual way to generate a lasting net response. Under that conventional preparation assumption, a ferroelectric constituent is the expected route.
However, the word polycrystalline alone does not logically require ferroelectricity. A deliberately textured aggregate of nonswitchable piezoelectric grains can have a nonzero effective tensor if their orientations and polar directions do not cancel. Ferroelectricity means electrically switchable spontaneous polarization, whereas piezoelectricity means linear stress–charge coupling. Thus the unconditional statement that every polycrystalline piezoelectric must be ferroelectric is too strong.

No. A piezoelectric single crystal need not be ferroelectric. A noncentrosymmetric crystal can couple strain to polarization without having a spontaneous polarization that an electric field can switch. Quartz is a standard example.
A single crystal already has an oriented crystallographic tensor, so the random-grain cancellation issue does not arise. The relevant tests are crystal symmetry and the nonzero piezoelectric coefficients; ferroelectric switching is a separate property.

If removal restores the original random-domain magnetic state, the magnetostrictive strain and induced voltage return toward their original values. Elastic strain transfer then relaxes the stress on the central layer.
A ferromagnetic nickel layer can retain remanent magnetization and an altered domain population, so removing the external field does not guarantee removal of all magnetostriction. The final observed voltage is therefore history- and circuit-dependent.

Sample B should show two FCC peak families, one from a copper-rich phase and one from a silver-rich phase. Their lattice parameters are set by the compositions of the coexisting phases, not by one lattice parameter at the overall alloy composition.
For corresponding Miller indices the silver-rich phase has a larger lattice parameter and hence a larger interplanar spacing, so its reflection occurs at smaller 2θ. Some reflections may overlap or be weak. Relative intensities depend on phase fractions, atomic scattering factors, absorption and texture; a raw peak-height ratio is not directly a mass fraction.
At room temperature the mutual solubilities in the supplied Cu–Ag diagram are small, so the two families would be near the elemental copper and silver positions. Slow cooling is important: a metastable supersaturated single phase would be a different interpretation. Quantitative phase or composition analysis would require calibrated diffraction and an appropriate refinement, not merely counting the visible peaks.

SEM with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy can identify gold versus silver through their characteristic X-ray energies. Acquire spectra and maps from representative regions: two compositionally distinct regions would support the two-phase interpretation of B. With standards or validated matrix corrections it can estimate local compositions and, with representative sampling, an overall composition. Surface topography and the interaction volume limit spatial interpretation.
X-ray fluorescence provides a complementary more bulk-averaged elemental measurement. Gold and silver produce different characteristic lines; calibrated intensities can yield their mass fractions, with corrections for absorption, excitation and specimen geometry. It does not by itself determine whether an element occupies one or two crystallographic phases.
Both methods supply elemental information missing from a lattice-parameter-only identification. A purely morphological micrograph could reveal phase separation but would not alone give a reliable numerical chemical composition.

Pearlite is a microstructural arrangement of two phases, ferrite and cementite, rather than a single phase. Ferrite is an iron-rich solid solution; cementite is the carbide Fe3​C. A pearlite colony contains alternating lamellae of these constituents formed by transformation of austenite.
A thermodynamic phase has a specified crystal structure and composition range with locally homogeneous properties on the appropriate scale. The word pearlite instead describes how distinct phases are arranged. Its lamellar spacing and orientation can change while the identities of its two phases remain the same.

The danger is identifying an etched height interval with the original planar phase intersection. Preferential ferrite dissolution exposes inclined cementite faces and can produce overhangs. A conventional AFM tip cannot follow a reentrant underside; its finite size also broadens ridge tops and may not enter the intervening troughs. A thresholded height width can consequently differ from both the true plate thickness and its original surface intersection.
A uniform inclination at an ideal flat cut does not itself invalidate a line-fraction estimate: both phases acquire the same geometrical width factor, which cancels in their ratio. The problem is differential relief and probe accessibility after etching, together with uncertain local angles and limited sampling. Using only the apparent ridge occupancy can overestimate cementite. An independently polished section, calibrated probe geometry, several directions and colonies, or another phase-sensitive method is needed to test the inference.

Assume a thin cylindrical wall, uniform internal pressure and closed ends carrying the pressure load. The radial stress is small compared with the membrane stresses when t≪r.
For an end cap, the outward pressure force is Pπr2. The wall's axial tensile force is σz​(2πrt). Equilibrium therefore gives
σz​=2tPr​.​
(25)
For a longitudinally split half-cylinder of length L, pressure has resultant P(2rL) across the projected rectangular area. Two cut wall sections each carry σθ​tL, so
σθ​=tPr​=2σz​.​
(26)
Thus hoop stress is twice the closed-end axial stress in this model. An open-ended tube without an external end load does not acquire the same pressure-induced axial membrane stress.

Linear elastic fracture mechanics predicts a near-tip tensile stress varying approximately as
σyy​(r,0)≃2πr​KI​​.
(27)
A ductile material yields near the tip before that mathematical singularity can be realized. Plastic deformation blunts the crack and redistributes stress over a finite process zone, as shown in the right-hand diagram. Constraint can sustain stresses above the uniaxial yield stress, so the schematic plateau should not be interpreted as a universal exactly-σy​ cap.
A useful process-zone scale is rp​∼(KI​/σy​)2 with a geometry- and constraint-dependent coefficient. Advancing the crack then requires energy for plastic deformation and other irreversible processes as well as forming new surfaces. Consequently the critical energy release rate is much larger than the reversible surface-energy term 2γ of a perfectly brittle idealization:
Gc​≃2γ+Gplastic​+Gother​.
(28)
In a small-scale-yielding plane-strain description, Gc​=KIC2​/E′ with E′=E/(1−ν2). Large-scale yielding requires an elastic-plastic measure rather than treating a linear-elastic stress-intensity criterion as exact. The increased dissipation and crack-tip blunting explain the larger energy requirement.

Let a be a characteristic crack depth and Y its geometry factor. A simple fast-fracture condition is
KI​=Yσπa​=KIC​,ac​=π1​(YσKIC​​)2.
(29)
The crack should penetrate a wall of thickness t before reaching the critical size, so the simplified leak-before-break criterion is t<ac​, with appropriate geometry and stable-through-wall-crack assumptions.
The conventional material-based maximum thickness uses the yield stress as a conservative limiting operating stress:
t<tmax(σy​)​=π1​(Yσy​KIC​​)2.​
(30)
It ensures that the depth required for penetration is subcritical even at the stated limiting stress. Geometrical factors depend on crack shape and orientation; this is an educational screening estimate rather than a full crack-growth or leak-detection analysis. The handbook's fracture formula uses the same stress-intensity and geometry-factor convention.[ref]
There is an important distinction if the actual pressure and radius are fixed while thickness varies. Substituting σ=Pr/t at penetration a=t gives
KI​(t)=YPrtπ​​,t>π(KIC​YPr​)2.​
(31)
That is a lower, not upper, bound on thickness under the fixed-pressure model. Yield avoidance additionally requires t≳Pr/σy​. It would be inconsistent to derive the fixed-pressure expression and call it a maximum. The standard maximum quoted above answers a different, yield-limited material-selection comparison.

Within the rubbery regime, viscosity usually decreases as temperature increases, because segmental rearrangement becomes easier. The spring's entropic contribution generally increases approximately with absolute temperature at fixed effective network density, so k tends to increase modestly under that assumption.
Consequently τ=η/k decreases. The storage-modulus crossover moves to larger squared frequency,
ωcross2​=τ−2,
(44)
and the high-frequency plateau moves upward if k increases. The warmer curve in the figure illustrates those two changes. At intermediate frequencies the reduced relaxation time can lower the storage modulus even though its eventual plateau is higher; there is no requirement that the entire curve move upward.
These trends assume the same rubbery material state and no degradation, crystallization or change in effective network density. Close to other transitions the simple entropic-spring and viscosity picture needs modification.

a OurBigBook.com
A displacive transformation changes structure through coordinated atomic displacements, whereas a reconstructive transformation changes the bonding/connectivity arrangement more substantially.
In a displacive change, neighboring atoms move cooperatively without the long-range compositional redistribution characteristic of diffusion-controlled precipitation. The change can preserve many near-neighbor relationships and can occur rapidly when its kinetic conditions are met.
A reconstructive change requires breaking and re-forming the relevant structural connections to establish a different arrangement, often with a larger activation barrier and slower nucleation and growth. It may consequently show substantial kinetic persistence of a metastable phase. The distinction concerns the atomic mechanism, not merely whether the final phase has a different symmetry; neither label by itself supplies a universal transformation rate.

For two phases of the same pure substance at equilibrium, their chemical potentials are equal. Along their coexistence boundary,
μ1​=μ2​,dμ=−sm​dT+vm​dp.
(45)
Differentiating the equality gives
−sm,1​dT+vm,1​dp=−sm,2​dT+vm,2​dp.
(46)
Therefore the Clapeyron equation is
dTdp​=Δvm​Δsm​​=ΔvΔs​=TΔvL​.​
(47)
Molar or mass-specific quantities give the same slope if a consistent basis is used. L=TΔs follows from equal Gibbs free energies at coexistence.
Solid phases are relatively incompressible and have modest thermal expansion over many intervals. If their entropy and volume differences vary slowly enough that Δs/Δv is approximately constant, the boundary is approximately straight. Straightness is an approximation, not a thermodynamic requirement. Heat-capacity differences, pressure dependence of volume, other transitions and proximity to special points can curve the boundary. The ideal-vapor integrated Clausius-Clapeyron approximation is not appropriate for these two solid phases.
