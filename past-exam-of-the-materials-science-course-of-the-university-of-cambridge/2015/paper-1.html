Use fractional coordinates in a conventional cubic cell with an atom at the origin. The diamond cubic structure consists of an FCC Bravais lattice with the two-atom basis
(0,0,0),(41​,41​,41​).
(1)
The FCC translations within the conventional cell are (0,0,0), (0,21​,21​), (21​,0,21​) and (21​,21​,0). Adding the basis gives eight atoms per conventional cell. Equivalently, two identical FCC sublattices are displaced by a[111]/4. It is not a monatomic close-packed arrangement: every atom has four tetrahedrally arranged nearest neighbors rather than twelve.
In the projection, label each atom by its fractional height. The (0,0) and (1/2,1/2) columns have height0; (0,1/2) and (1/2,0) have height1/2. The additional four columns are (1/4,1/4) and (3/4,3/4) at height1/4, and (1/4,3/4) and (3/4,1/4) at height3/4. Boundary columns repeat periodically; they must not be counted as full extra atoms.

The vertical mirrors of the diamond cubic structure have the required orientation: their normals lie in the (001) plane. In the chosen atomic-origin convention, their traces satisfy
x−y=2n​,x+y=2n​,n∈Z,​
(2)
with x,y expressed as fractions of a. These are the two orientations of vertical {110} mirrors and their translated copies. For example, the plane x=y interchanges the first two coordinates and leaves every diamond site in an equivalent site. Reflection in x+y=1/2 sends (x,y,z) to (1/2−y,1/2−x,z), which also preserves the full three-dimensional motif.
The projected traces alone do not test a mirror: the atom heights must also agree under reflection. In particular, a vertical {100} trace can appear plausible in an unlabelled projection but does not preserve all heights as a pure mirror; glide operations are different symmetry elements.

An unstressed glass is optically isotropic, so it does not split incident light into differently phased in-plane components; it remains dark between crossed polars. A natural explanation of the colored regions after heating is devitrification: anisotropic crystallites have nucleated in the glass. Edges and surfaces provide heterogeneous nucleation sites, making their greater concentration there plausible.
Each approximately uniform crystallite can give a nearly uniform interference colour. Regions with similar thickness and effective birefringence have similar retardation and therefore similar spectral colour, while differing azimuths multiply the intensity by sin2(2α) and change brightness. Identical hue does not prove identical three-dimensional crystallographic orientation.
Stress-induced birefringence is a possible alternative in a heat-treated glass. The observations alone do not uniquely prove crystallinity; rotating-stage extinction, morphology or diffraction could distinguish the explanations. The crystallite interpretation is the straightforward phase-transformation explanation, without inventing a composition or heat-treatment schedule.

On rotating a fixed crystallite around the microscope axis, the angle α to the fixed polarizer changes. The factor sin2(2α) repeats every90degrees. The quartz compensator is a birefringent optical compensator: a retarder of known principal directions. With its principal axes at45degrees to the polarizer and analyser, the previously dark isotropic background acquires the compensator's own interference colour. The crystals are now viewed through two retarders.
When a specimen's slow axis is parallel to the compensator's slow axis, the retardations add. A90degree relative principal-axis rotation pairs the specimen's slow axis with the compensator's fast axis, and the signed retardations subtract:
Γtotal​=Γq​+Γs​orΓtotal​=Γq​−Γs​.
(17)
Thus different crystallites can shift to higher or lower interference orders even if their original hue was similar. In the subtracting orientation, equal magnitudes can compensate and yield extinction. These shifts identify fast and slow specimen directions.
At a general relative angle the full polarization transformation is required; simple scalar addition is only valid for aligned principal axes. The source does not specify the compensator retardation, so no unique list of resulting named colours can be assigned. The robust predictions are a changed background, orientation-dependent addition or subtraction, and possible compensation.

The materials interpretation is a cooling-rate gradient in a near-eutectoid steel. Before transformation, the parent thermodynamic phase is austenite. A small section can cool rapidly enough to suppress diffusional decomposition and form martensite, a supersaturated, highly strained transformation product. A much thicker interior cools more slowly and can transform diffusively to pearlite: alternating ferrite and cementite lamellae grouped into colonies. Bainite or mixed products are also possible for intermediate histories.
The likely contrast is therefore a martensite-rich rapidly cooled region and a more pearlitic slowly cooled interior. Brittle fracture is promoted by the highly strained martensitic state, residual stresses and local stress concentration. A larger intact interior need not share the same microstructure or local loading. The observations are consistent with this contrast, not a measurement proving pure thermodynamic phases everywhere.
The sketches below are generic steel microstructures, rather than a component design or fabrication prescription. No cooling curve, dimensions or transformation diagram is provided, so exact thermodynamic phase fractions, plate sizes and a universally pearlitic centre cannot be inferred.

In tempered martensite, the metastable carbon-supersaturated martensitic matrix can evolve toward a lower-carbon ferritic matrix containing fine carbides. Carbon segregation and precipitation reduce supersaturation and tetragonal lattice distortion; recovery can reduce internal strain while the original plate or lath pattern remains recognizable. This is tempering, rather than a fresh austenite-to-pearlite transformation.[ref]
A generic sketch consequently replaces a uniform highly strained plate interior by a lower-carbon matrix with dispersed carbide particles, as in the third panel above. This provides a qualitative materials explanation for a changed fracture response. It does not specify a manufacturing optimization, and tempering is not a guarantee of monotonic toughness improvement in every steel: carbide morphology, impurities and retained austenite matter. The source reports the observed outcome, so the explanation must not be turned into a universal process recommendation.

The original diagram is a Kelvin-Voigt model: a spring and a dashpot act in parallel. The spring is an ideal elastic element, σs​=Eεs​; under its own sinusoidal stress its strain is in phase with stress and energy is stored reversibly. The dashpot is viscous, σd​=ηε˙d​; its strain under an oscillating stress lags by90degrees and mechanical energy is dissipated.
In parallel the strain is common and the branch stresses add:
σ=Eε+ηε˙,τ=η/E.
(29)
For imposed harmonic stress, the strain amplitude and phase lag are
∣ε∣=E2+(ωη)2​σ0​​,δ=tan−1(ωτ).
(30)
The short- and long-timescale limits follow under the printed roman labels below.
A sudden constant stress σ0​ gives delayed, bounded creep:
ε(t)=Eσ0​​(1−e−t/τ),t≥0.​
(31)
The strain is initially zero because an instantaneous change would require an infinite dashpot stress. Initially the dashpot carries the load, with finite strain rate σ0​/η; the spring gradually carries more of it as the rate decays. On unloading, the strain recovers exponentially to zero. The network does not produce the instantaneous strain jump or unbounded constant-stress creep of a series Maxwell element.

For a rubber-like solid, a spring is a first model of recoverable elasticity, while a viscous element can represent rate-dependent loss and delayed recovery. An immediate extension followed by bounded delayed material creep is represented by series spring and Voigt-branch creep, a standard linear solid model:
ε(t)=E0​σ0​​+E1​σ0​​(1−e−E1​t/η).
(32)
The first term is immediate and recoverable; the second is delayed and also recoverable. For a load held until t=th​, recovery after a finite-duration stress load predicts an immediate drop of σ0​/E0​ on unloading, followed by
ε(t)=E1​σ0​​(1−e−th​/τ)e−(t−th​)/τ,τ=E1​η​,t>th​.​
(33)
This supplies a direct comparison with a loading-and-unloading observation: the model returns completely to its original length, with separate immediate and delayed recovery. A single Kelvin-Voigt model misses the immediate compliance, while a free series dashpot would predict unbounded flow that an intact crosslinked rubber need not show. A spring alone suffices over a timescale on which the measured response is effectively immediate and elastic.
These are local small-strain analogues. Large-strain rubber elasticity is nonlinear, nearly incompressible and sensitive to temperature, loading history and internal damage. A small set of linear springs and dashpots cannot reproduce every amplitude, timescale or hysteresis loop.
No numerical rubber trace or fitted constants are supplied, so these are explicit qualitative model predictions for comparison with the practical observations.

A physical mechanics model of wood must at least acknowledge anisotropy: stiffness and creep depend on loading direction. Direction-dependent elastic elements can represent different small-strain moduli, and delayed elastic branches can approximate time-dependent strain. A linear-solid network can therefore be a local fit under specified direction, moisture and temperature, rather than a universal scalar constitutive law.
An immediate strain followed by delayed material creep is captured locally by a spring plus a Kelvin-Voigt model branch. If residual deformation remains after unloading, a Burgers material adds a free series dashpot η0​. Under a constant load,
ε(t)=E0​σ0​​+E1​σ0​​(1−e−t/τ)+η0​σ0​t​.
(34)
After a hold time th​, the two elastic contributions recover and the dashpot retains
ε(∞)=η0​σ0​th​​​.
(35)
Thus immediate recovery, delayed recovery and a residual set identify different roles for the components; an observed permanent set would rule out a purely recoverable linear-solid fit. A viscous dashpot does not itself model a yield threshold or structural damage. Strength loss, splitting, moisture-dependent response and nonlinear compression cannot be represented by one fixed linear Kelvin-Voigt model. No direction-specific measured trace is supplied, so selecting numerical parameters requires the practical data; the constitutive predictions and their limitations are the model comparison that can be made from the paper.

At equilibrium between two thermodynamic phases of a pure substance, their molar Gibbs free energies are equal. Along a coexistence line,
g1​(T,P)=g2​(T,P),dg=−sdT+vdP.
(36)
Subtracting the two differentials gives
−(s2​−s1​)dT+(v2​−v1​)dP=0.
(37)
For a nonzero volume change,
dTdP​=ΔvΔs​=TΔvΔh​.​
(38)
The second equality uses Δg=Δh−TΔs=0 on coexistence. Total entropy and volume changes may be used instead if they refer to the same amount of material. The ratio must use one consistent transformation direction, so reversing the labels changes both signs and leaves the slope unchanged. This is the general Clapeyron equation; the ideal-vapour approximation often called Clausius-Clapeyron makes further assumptions not needed for two solids.

A multi-anvil press transmits a large external load through several hard anvils onto a confined pressure medium surrounding the sample. Reducing the loaded area raises pressure, while the surrounding medium and gasket limit lateral escape. A resistive heater within the assembly can heat the sample, with insulation and temperature sensing arranged around it. It provides relatively large sample volumes and useful bulk, high-temperature experimental conditions, but access to the sample and optical paths is restricted, pressure gradients and calibration matter, and the maximum pressure is constrained by anvil strength and geometry.
A diamond anvil cell squeezes a very small sample and pressure medium in a gasket hole between opposed diamond culets. Diamond combines high strength with useful optical transparency, permitting spectroscopy and optical pressure calibration as well as suitable X-ray access. Heating can be external or laser-based. The very small contact area permits high pressure from a modest total force, but gives a tiny sample volume. Nonhydrostatic stress, pressure gradients, gasket deformation, heating gradients, chemical compatibility and diamond failure are important limitations.
Neither device generates pressure by force alone independent of area and confinement. Nor does a nominal force-to-area ratio guarantee a known hydrostatic sample pressure; experimental pressure and temperature must be calibrated. The two assemblies trade sample volume and access against pressure reach rather than one being universally preferable.

Diamond can be metastable: its Gibbs free energy is higher than graphite's under ambient conditions, but transformation is kinetically inhibited. Conversion requires substantial rearrangement of the strong carbon-bond network and the formation and growth of a new thermodynamic phase, with an activation and nucleation barrier.
At room temperature the rate can be extremely small even though the free-energy change favors graphite. Thermodynamic stability specifies the preferred equilibrium state, not the time needed to reach it. Persistence of diamond therefore does not contradict graphite's ambient equilibrium stability.

The positive slope reflects diamond's smaller entropy and volume. Higher temperature favors the higher-entropy graphite thermodynamic phase, requiring greater pressure to compensate in favor of the smaller-volume diamond thermodynamic phase.
This estimate treats the supplied ambient molar entropies and volumes as applicable near coexistence and neglects their changes through thermal expansion, compressibility and heat capacity. It gives an approximate gradient, not the transition pressure or a complete high-pressure thermodynamic phase boundary; an intercept would require an additional free-energy datum.

Orowan bypass bows a dislocation around an unshearable obstacle and leaves a loop. A dislocation line-tension estimate for a segment with tension Tℓ​ gives the limiting shear-stress scale
Δτ≃bℓ2Tℓ​​,
(46)
where ℓ is a free obstacle gap on the slip plane. For an FCC perfect dislocation,
b=2​a​=2​0.36​ nm≃0.2546 nm.
(47)
Using Tℓ​≃Gb2/2 and the simple gap estimate ℓ≃L−d≃0.34 μm gives
Δτ≃ℓGb​≃5.5×107 Pa.​
(48)
Converting to tensile stress requires an orientation factor. With an explicitly assumed polycrystalline FCC Taylor factor M≃3,
Δσy​≃MΔτ∼(1.6–1.7)×108 Pa≈160–170 MPa.​
(49)
The graph and spacing approximations do not justify a precise last digit. A favorably oriented single crystal with Schmid factor1/2 would instead give Δσy​≃2Δτ, around110MPa.

If the quoted pitch is used directly as an effective free spacing, neglecting diameter, the same simplified calculation gives Δτ≃19 MPa and MΔτ≃57 MPa. d OurBigBook.com
A single-domain precipitate has no internal domain wall available to move. Magnetization changes therefore require rotation, curling or reversal of a particle's magnetization, rather than ordinary wall displacement through that particle. Relative to a larger multidomain material this can increase coercivity, but the actual coercivity depends on anisotropy, shape and particle interactions. If thermal activation over the anisotropy barrier is rapid on the observation timescale, superparamagnetic behavior can instead suppress remanence. Ferromagnetic composition plus small size alone does not prove a permanent hard magnet.
For an elementary wall-energy comparison, imagine one planar wall through a sphere of radius r. Its area is Aw​=πr2. If that trial two-domain state removes essentially all of the specified stray-field energy while adding wall energy γw​Aw​, stability of the uniform state requires
γw​Aw​≳Ustray​.
(50)
With r≃0.33 μm and the supplied Ustray​=5×10−18 J,
γw​≳π(0.33×10−6)25×10−18​≃1.5×10−5 Jm−2.​
(51)
This is the requested scale under a full stray-energy-release and one-planar-wall approximation; it is a lower-bound estimate within that model.
A more exact comparison uses the actual energy reduction ΔUstray​ of the competing domain pattern, not necessarily the whole original stray energy:
γw​≳Aw​ΔUstray​​=Aw​ζUstray​​,0≤ζ≤1.
(52)
No domain geometry, anisotropy energy or barrier is supplied to determine ζ or establish that the observed state is the thermodynamic ground state rather than kinetically trapped. Thus the given information alone does not establish an unconditional positive numerical lower bound independent of the competing pattern; the elementary estimate must retain its stated assumptions.
