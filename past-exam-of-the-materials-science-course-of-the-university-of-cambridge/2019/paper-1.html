In crystal polymorphism, the same chemical composition has different crystal structures; for an chemical element, this is a form of allotropy. Diamond has a three-dimensional tetrahedral network of covalent bonds, giving high hardness and a wide band gap. Graphite has strongly bonded planar sheets, with weaker interactions between sheets. Sliding between sheets makes it a useful solid lubricant; mobile electronic states give good electrical conduction within the sheets. Their different properties come from different bonding and structures, despite both being carbon.

Barium titanate has a high-temperature cubic perovskite structure, with Ti centred in its oxygen octahedron on average. This phase is paraelectric. Near room temperature the structure is tetragonal, with relative ionic displacements that give a spontaneous electric polarization. This phase is ferroelectric: an applied electric field can switch between differently polarized domains. Both phases have the same composition, BaTiO3​; the transition near 120∘C changes crystal symmetry and electrical response.

The zinc blende structure has an FCC sulfur sublattice and zinc on half its tetrahedral sites. One conventional unit cell uses S at (0,0,0),(1/2,1/2,0),(1/2,0,1/2),(0,1/2,1/2) and Zn at those positions displaced by (1/4,1/4,1/4), reduced modulo one. The wurtzite structure has ABAB sulfur layers, instead of ABCABC layers. One hexagonal-cell convention uses S at (0,0,0),(2/3,1/3,1/2) and Zn at (0,0,u),(2/3,1/3,1/2+u), with ideal u=3/8. Looking along [001] superposes S and Zn in that latter coordinate convention; the height labels distinguish them.
The closest-packed sulfur planes are {111}, with four distinct plane orientations, for zinc blende structure, and {001}, with one orientation, for wurtzite structure. Opposite normals describe the same unoriented plane orientation. Hexagonal four-index notation writes the latter basal family as {0001}.

The solid trace is wurtzite; the dashed trace is zinc blende. The isolated strong dashed peak is the cubic 111 reflection. The solid trace has hexagonal 100, 002 and 101 reflections in this angular interval; its central 002 peak is the one at 28.65∘, while cubic 111 occurs at 28.53∘. These assignments are consistent with the absence of lower-angle reflections.
In the ideal close-packed model, the cubic 111 and hexagonal 002 reflections probe the same local alternating layer geometry. Their scattering strength per suitably normalized amount of material and their angular factors are therefore very similar, but powder diffraction multiplicity differs: the cubic family has eight directed normals, while hexagonal 002 has two. This explains the approximately fourfold greater intensity of the dashed peak. Comparing unnormalized conventional-cell structure factors alone would be misleading because the cells contain different numbers of formula units. Peak heights additionally depend on line widths; the multiplicity argument applies most directly to integrated intensities.

An optically isotropic film changes the phase of both transverse polarization components equally, so crossed polarizers still extinguish the transmitted light. The etched rod-and-gap composite has form birefringence: its effective permittivity depends on the direction of the electric field, even when the constituent material is intrinsically isotropic. In a simple subwavelength lamellar model,
ϵ∥​=fϵr​+(1−f)ϵg​,ϵ⊥​=(ϵr​f​+ϵg​1−f​)−1.
(2)
The precise effective-medium formula changes with rod geometry, but the anisotropy remains. Resolving incident light along the two principal directions produces a phase retardation. An ideal lossless specimen between crossed polarizers transmits
I=IP​sin2(2α)sin2(πΓ/λ),Γ=(nslow​−nfast​)t,
(3)
where IP​ is the intensity after the first polarizer. Extinction occurs when a principal direction follows either polarizer; transmission is strongest near 45∘.

The local slow direction follows the radial rods. Between crossed polarizers, the angular factor is sin2(2φ), so radial directions parallel to either polarizer are dark. The pattern is a Maltese cross in polarized-light microscopy, with four bright sectors separated by dark arms parallel to the polarizer directions. Its colours depend on the local optical path difference; tapering rods or changing fill fraction can make this vary with radius. The cross belongs to the local radial-axis pattern and does not imply a conoscopic interference figure.

Successive planes normal to [001] are chemically ordered Au and Cu layers. The supplied four-site cell is a convenient doubled description of the primitive tetragonal lattice: valid primitive translations are
a1​=2a​(1,1,0),a2​=2a​(−1,1,0),a3​=(0,0,c).
(6)
They give a square basal cell of side a/2​, containing an Au basis atom at the origin and Cu at fractional (1/2,1/2,1/2). That last vector exchanges chemical species and is not a Bravais translation. Thus a face-centred-looking atomic arrangement does not make the chemically ordered material an FCC Bravais lattice. This is the L10 crystal structure; the following indices retain the larger cell specified in the question.

At a common test temperature and otherwise comparable microstructure, chemical order generally raises the yield strength. An ordinary FCC dislocation displaced by a⟨110⟩/2 can interchange Au and Cu sublattices, creating an antiphase boundary behind it. This additional energy resists slip; paired dislocations can restore the order after the second member passes. The disordered phase does not pay the same ordering penalty. Comparing specimens actually tested at their different phase-stability temperatures also includes temperature-dependent mobility and recovery, so the ordering argument alone does not predict a unique measured strength ratio.

(Bi) and (βSn) are Bi-rich and Sn-rich terminal solid solutions, not intermetallic compounds. At the eutectic composition, cooling through 138∘C gives
L⟶(Bi)+(βSn).​
(10)
The two solids grow together as a fine eutectic microstructure, commonly lamellar or rod-like. Below about 13∘C, the tin-rich component has an equilibrium tendency to transform from metallic tetragonal white tin to diamond-cubic grey tin, with a substantial volume increase. This is tin pest, which can disrupt the microstructure mechanically. The dashed transition does not transform the Bi-rich phase into tin. Actual transformation can be slow and depend strongly on impurities and nucleation; crossing the line does not guarantee immediate disintegration.

For a mixture with phase compositions x1​,x2​, its molar Gibbs free energy is the straight-line combination g=(1−f)g1​(x1​)+fg2​(x2​) at overall x=(1−f)x1​+fx2​. Minimizing over phase compositions gives equal slopes and equal tangent intercepts: the common-tangent construction expresses equality of both component chemical potentials. The equilibrium curve is the lower convex envelope of the candidate phase curves. Phase fractions follow the lever rule.

An FCC crystal slips on {111}⟨110⟩. For each slip system, Schmid's law gives τ=σm, with m=∣l^⋅n^∣∣l^⋅s^∣. Enumerating the twelve systems for l=(5,1,4) gives the maximum at

Initially, one slip system carries most of the deformation. Easy dislocation glide gives the nearly flat stage-I response, although lattice rotation and gradual dislocation interactions can already change the required tensile stress. In tension the loading direction expressed in the rotating crystal approaches the primary slip direction. Its path has the form (5+t,1+t,4) until it reaches the symmetry boundary y=z, at [844]=[211].
At that orientation, (11ˉ1)[110] and (111ˉ)[101] have equal Schmid factors, 1/6​. Conjugate slip therefore becomes competitive. Intersections between the two dislocation families generate obstacles, junctions and forest hardening, giving the sharp stage-II rise in flow stress. This is the ideal single-slip-to-conjugate-slip construction; real activation stresses can be shifted by latent hardening and imperfections.

b OurBigBook.com
Energetic neutron collisions create displacement cascades and Frenkel pairs: displaced atoms become self-interstitials, leaving vacancies. During migration, some opposite defects recombine, while others cluster or reach sinks. Interstitials can condense into extra atomic planes bounded by dislocation loops; vacancy clustering can instead give missing-plane loops. Their subsequent growth is driven by defect absorption and dislocation climb.
Randomly oriented loops do not select a macroscopic shear direction in an initially isotropic polycrystal. That does not prove that every external dimension remains unchanged: interstitial and vacancy loops have opposite relaxation-volume contributions, and an imbalance can change the volume. Macroscopic void swelling requires the additional vacancy-cavity mechanism; it must not be identified automatically with the mere presence of loops. Texture, stress-biased absorption and unequal loop populations can also give anisotropic dimensional change.

c OurBigBook.com
The annealed sample initially has relatively few obstacles to dislocation glide. After its elastic response it yields, undergoes appreciable plastic deformation and develops strain hardening. Irradiation produces dislocation loops and defect clusters that obstruct gliding dislocations, usually raising the yield strength. In a simple dispersed-obstacle model the strengthening scales as Δσ∼MαGbNd​, with obstacle density N and diameter d.
Irradiated material often has less uniform plastic elongation and weaker subsequent hardening because slip localizes into channels that have cleared irradiation obstacles. Earlier necking or fracture can then reduce ductility. The elastic slope is much less affected than the yield stress in this schematic comparison; the exact strength and elongation depend on dose and temperature.

Irradiation both creates dislocation loops and supplies mobile point defects that allow existing dislocations to climb, rearrange and annihilate. At low initial dislocation density, net production can dominate; at high initial density, sink absorption and recovery can dominate. A simple illustrative rate equation is
dDdρ​=A−Bρ,ρ(D)=BA​+(ρ0​−BA​)e−BD.
(18)
Thus both initial states approach ρss​=A/B in this fixed-condition model. Here D is irradiation dose, not a diffusion coefficient. The asymptote reflects a dynamic balance, not the cessation of damage. It need not be the same for different alloys, temperatures, dose rates or applied stresses.
