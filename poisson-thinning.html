Poisson thinning

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Poisson process
OurBigBook.com Words: 895 Articles: 6
Poisson thinning splits a Poisson process by independently marking each arrival as retained or rejected. Let the original Homogeneous Poisson process have rate λ>0, and let p(s)∈[0,1] be a deterministic measurable function of time. Attach independent uniform distributions U1​,U2​,… on (0,1), independent of the entire original Poisson process. At arrival time Ti​, retain the arrival if Ui​≤p(Ti​). Given all arrival times, these decisions are independent and have the specified retention probabilities. Define the two counting processes using an indicator function:
Rt​=∑i:Ti​≤t​1{Ui​≤p(Ti​)}​,Dt​=Nt​−Rt​.
(77)
The Poisson thinning theorem gives independent retained and rejected Inhomogeneous Poisson processes. Their rates are λp(s) and λ(1−p(s)), respectively, in the sense of the intensity function of a point process. In particular, for 0≤a<b,
Rb​−Ra​∼Poisson(λ∫ab​p(s)ds),Db​−Da​∼Poisson(λ∫ab​(1−p(s))ds).
(78)
The independence is of the two whole processes: every finite collection of retained counts is independent of every finite collection of rejected counts. It is not merely zero covariance for one interval. For constant p, the streams are Homogeneous Poisson processes of rates λp and λ(1−p); the endpoints p=0,1 include the identically zero stream.[ref]
For an exact toy calculation, take λ=2 per minute and retain with p(s)=1/4 on (0,2] and p(s)=3/4 on (2,4]. In the first two minutes, the retained and rejected expected values are 2⋅2⋅1/4=1 and 2⋅2⋅3/4=3. In the next two minutes they are 3 and 1. Over all four minutes, R4​,D4​ are independent Poisson distributions with mean 4 each, and their sum N4​ has mean 8. Thus
P(R4​=0,D4​=0)=e−4e−4=e−8,P(R4​=2,D4​=3)=e−82!42​3!43​=3256​e−8.
(79)
These probabilities and expected values describe the model, not empirical measurements. In the diagram, the original nine synthetic arrivals split into five retained and four rejected arrivals; those realized counts need not equal their expected values.
The mechanism explains why mutually exclusive labels can yield independent counts: randomness of the original total offsets the dependence from allocating a fixed total. For constant p and an interval with original mean m, the conditional retained count has a binomial distribution given N=n. The joint probability generating function is
E[zRwD]=E[(pz+(1−p)w)N]=exp{m(pz+(1−p)w−1)}=emp(z−1)em(1−p)(w−1),0≤z,w≤1.
(80)
This factorization proves the two independent Poisson distributions; disjoint original intervals give independent pairs. For varying p(s), the Independent marking theorem for Poisson point processes gives the same conclusion with the two integrated intensities. The joint red and green Poisson count formula gives the corresponding direct calculation with probability mass functions.
Independence here is unconditional. After conditioning on N=n, the identity D=n−R gives covariance Cov(R,D∣N=n)=−np(1−p) for constant p. Thus the retained and rejected counts are dependent for n>0 and 0<p<1, even though their unconditional covariance is zero. Poisson thinning can model independent routing of arrivals, or independent detection of arrivals with a known efficiency schedule; using only a retention fraction without checking the marking mechanism can give a wrong count law.
