Divisor (d∣n)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory Divisibility
OurBigBook.com Words: 447 Articles: 7
A divisor of an integer n is an integer d satisfying the divisibility relation d∣n. Equivalently, n=dk for some integer k. The word positive matters when listing or counting divisors: unless signs are restricted, both d and −d qualify.
The positive divisors of 12 are
1,2,3,4,6,12.
(3)
Their negatives are also divisors of 12, giving twelve integer divisors altogether. The same list of positive divisors works for −12. One has only the positive divisor 1, while zero has every integer as a divisor, including zero under our divisibility convention. A nonzero integer never has zero as a divisor.
For a positive integer n, the positive divisors come in pairs (d,n/d) whose product is n. For 12, the pairs are (1,12), (2,6) and (3,4). Each pair contains a divisor at most n​, so searching through candidate divisors up to the square root suffices to find all pairs. A pair repeats its own member only when n is a perfect square; for 36, the middle pair is (6,6). Consequently a positive integer has an odd number of positive divisors exactly when it is a perfect square.
The prime factorization gives another complete description. Since 12=22⋅3, its positive divisors are precisely 2i3j with 0≤i≤2 and 0≤j≤1. These independent choices give (2+1)(1+1)=6 positive divisors, the value of the divisor function τ(12). A prime factor is one particular kind of divisor; 4, 6 and 12 show that divisors need not be prime numbers.
Thinking in pairs connects exact grouping with integer factorization: twelve objects can form three groups of four or four groups of three. Thinking in shared divisors gives the greatest common divisor; for 12 and 18, the positive common divisors are 1,2,3,6, and the largest is 6.
