Entropy (S)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 10
Entropy is an extensive quantity and a state function of an equilibrium system, measured in JK−1. Differences between equilibrium states are calculated along a reversible thermodynamic process:
S2​−S1​=∫12​TδQrev​​.
(99)
Here heat entering the system is positive and T is its absolute thermodynamic temperature. The reversible path used for calculation need not be the path actually taken. Dividing the actual heat by a single temperature generally fails for an irreversible process.
For example, put two rigid bodies, each with constant heat capacity C=100JK−1, at 600K and 300K in an otherwise isolated system. Assume each body has a uniform internal temperature, no phase transition occurs, and no work is extracted. Their final thermal equilibrium is at 450K, because the internal energy lost by one equals that gained by the other. Using entropy from heat capacity,
ΔSh​=Clog(450/600)=−28.77JK−1,ΔSc​=Clog(450/300)=40.55JK−1,
(100)
so ΔStot​=Clog(9/8)=11.78JK−1. One body's entropy decreases while the total increases. [ref]
In statistical mechanics, Boltzmann's entropy formula counts equally likely microstates compatible with a macrostate; Gibbs entropy handles a specified probability distribution over microstates. If the number of equally likely microstates doubles, entropy increases by kB​log2. The logarithm makes multiplicative counts additive for independent systems. These are precise statements about states and probabilities, not a universal measure of visible messiness. Calling entropy merely "disorder" obscures the thermal calculation above and hides which microstates are being counted. [ref]
The usefulness of entropy is that measurable heat capacities determine its changes, and the Second law of thermodynamics turns those changes into constraints on processes. It also connects macroscopic measurements to microscopic descriptions without requiring us to track every particle.
