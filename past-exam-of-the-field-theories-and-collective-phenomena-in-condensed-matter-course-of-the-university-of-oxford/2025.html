Make the Wick rotation t′=−iτ, and write TE​=βℏ for the final imaginary time, where β is inverse temperature and ℏ is the reduced Planck constant. Then dt′=−idτ and dx/dt′=idx/dτ. Thus the continued action is
S=i∫0TE​​[2m​(dτdx​)2+V(x)]dτ=iSEuc​,SEuc​[x]=∫0βℏ​[2m​x˙2+V(x)]dτ.​
(1)
Both the kinetic energy and the potential energy enter this Euclidean action with positive signs relative to their real-time definitions. Also e−iH(−iβℏ)/ℏ=e−βH, so the Euclidean path integral is
⟨xf​∣e−βH∣xi​⟩=∫x(0)=xi​x(TE​)=xf​​Dxe−SEuc​[x]/ℏ.​
(2)
Its boundary conditions fix the two endpoint positions. They need not coincide: identifying and integrating over them would instead give the thermal trace.
The normalization of Dx can be fixed without continuing a formal measure. Apply the Trotter product formula to H=p^​2/(2m)+V(x^), divide TE​ into M intervals of size ϵ=TE​/M, and integrate the intermediate positions. The resulting time-sliced configuration-space path integral is

(3)
This also explains the upper limit βℏ rather than β when the reduced Planck constant has not been set to one.

The integrated stochastic differential equation reads
Y(t):=y(t)−y(0)=∫0t​η(s)ds.
(12)
The Gaussian white noise has zero expected value, so
⟨y(t)−y(0)⟩=0.​
(13)

The Brownian motion displacement has a normal distribution with variance Γt. Its transition probability density function is therefore
p(yf​,t∣yi​,0)=2πΓt​1​exp[−2Γt(yf​−yi​)2​].
(15)
Comparison with the free particle heat kernel obtained in part (c) gives the required matching:
Γt=mβℏ2​.​
(16)
This matches whole path distributions after a linear reparametrization, not merely endpoint variances. To see this, divide the Brownian duration t into M slices of size δt. The independent increments have normal distributions with variance Γδt, so their joint density contributes
∏j=1M​2πΓδt​1​exp[−∑j=1M​2Γδt(yj​−yj−1​)2​].
(17)
For the Euclidean path integral, the corresponding expression is
∏j=1M​2πℏδτm​​exp[−∑j=1M​2ℏδτm(xj​−xj−1​)2​],δτ=Mβℏ​.
(18)
The matching condition makes Γδt=ℏδτ/m, so both the exponents and all normalization factors coincide. In particular, identifying Brownian time with imaginary time, t=βℏ, requires Γ=ℏ/m, or diffusion coefficient D=ℏ/(2m).
With both endpoints fixed, the normalized path law is a Brownian bridge. The Euclidean path integral itself is the endpoint density times that conditional path law. In the normalized time coordinate s=τ/(βℏ), its expected value and covariance are
E[x(s)]=xi​+s(xf​−xi​),Cov(x(s),x(s′))=mβℏ2​[min(s,s′)−ss′].
(19)
The classical path is therefore the bridge's expected value, while individual Brownian bridge paths fluctuate about it. Typical Brownian motion paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable: the kinetic Euclidean action is interpreted through the time-sliced diffusion path integral, rather than by assuming that a typical path has an ordinary velocity.

For J>0 each bond minimizes its energy when its two Ising spins are opposite, giving bond energy −J. An even ring allows every bond to do this simultaneously, including the closing bond required by the periodic boundary conditions. Its two ground states are
Sj​=σ(−1)j−1,σ=±1;E0​=−NJ,g0​=2.​
(20)
The two configurations are related by spin inversion symmetry. There is no geometrical frustration.

Let k count parallel bonds. Then N−k bonds are antiparallel and the energy is
H=Jk−J(N−k)=−NJ+2Jk.
(21)
The periodic boundary conditions impose
∏j=1N​(Sj​Sj+1​)=∏j=1N​Sj2​=1.
(22)
Hence N−k must be even. For odd N, k is odd, so its minimum is one: one bond must be parallel. All other bonds alternate. Choosing that bond in N ways and then choosing either value of S1​ determines every Ising spin, giving
E0​=−(N−2)J,g0​=2N.​
(23)
This is an odd antiferromagnetic Ising ring: geometrical frustration forces one defect, whose location is unrestricted. The ground-state degeneracy includes both its possible locations and global spin inversion symmetry. The formula also includes the self-bond case N=1 under the stated Hamiltonian convention.

Set K=βJ. The transfer matrix for the one-dimensional Ising model assigns a Boltzmann factor to each bond. In the Ising spin order (+1,−1), it is
Tss′​=e−Kss′,T=(e−KeK​eKe−K​).
(24)
Multiplying these bond factors and summing every Ising spin implements the canonical ensemble. The periodic boundary conditions close the product into a trace:
ZN​=∑S1​,…,SN​​∏j=1N​TSj​Sj+1​​=trTN.
(25)
The eigenvectors ∣+⟩=(1,1)T/2​ and ∣−⟩=(1,−1)T/2​ form an orthonormal basis, with eigenvalues
λ+​=e−K+eK=2coshK,λ−​=e−K−eK=−2sinhK.
(26)
The negative second eigenvalue encodes alternating correlations. Defining q=λ−​/λ+​=−tanhK, the exact partition function and total Helmholtz free energy are
ZN​=(2coshK)N+(−2sinhK)N=(2coshK)N(1+qN),​
(27)
FN​=−β1​lnZN​=−βN​ln(2coshK)−β1​ln(1+qN).​
(28)
The parity-dependent term must be retained for finite N; in particular it nearly cancels the leading term of ZN​ at low temperature when N is odd.
Represent insertion of one Ising spin by Σ=diag(1,−1). In the eigenbasis of T, this matrix swaps ∣+⟩ and ∣−⟩. Its diagonal entries in that eigenbasis vanish, giving the exact one-point correlation function
⟨S1​⟩=ZN​tr(ΣTN)​=0.​
(29)
Equivalently, configurations pair with their opposites under spin inversion symmetry, with the same Boltzmann factor and opposite S1​.
Two insertions separated by n bonds give the two-point correlation function
⟨S1​Sn+1​⟩​=ZN​tr(ΣTnΣTN−n)​=λ+N​+λ−N​λ−n​λ+N−n​+λ+n​λ−N−n​​.​
(30)
Thus the exact finite antiferromagnetic Ising-ring correlation is
CN​(n):=⟨S1​Sn+1​⟩=1+qNqn+qN−n​,q=−tanh(βJ),0≤n≤N−1.​
(31)
The two numerator terms represent the two arcs between the sites. The formula has CN​(0)=1 and CN​(n)=CN​(N−n), as required by the periodic boundary conditions. Since the one-point correlation function is zero, this is also the connected correlation function. At K=0, every Ising spin is independent, so CN​(n)=0 for distinct sites and CN​(0)=1; powers with exponent zero are understood as one.

For 0<K<∞, ∣q∣=tanhK<1. Taking the thermodynamic limit N→∞ at fixed separation n removes qN and qN−n from the finite antiferromagnetic Ising-ring correlation. Therefore
C∞​(n)=(−tanhK)n=(−1)ne−n/ξ,ξ=−lntanh(βJ)1​=lncoth(βJ)1​.​
(32)
The correlation length ξ is measured in lattice spacings. If the lattice spacing is a, its physical value is aξ. The factor (−1)n expresses antiferromagnetism, while e−n/ξ measures the decay of the staggered two-point correlation function.
At every positive temperature, ξ is finite. At low temperature, tanhK=1−2e−2K+O(e−4K), giving ξ∼21​e2βJ​. At infinite temperature, K=0, distinct Ising spins are uncorrelated; the limiting correlation length is zero.

Put r=tanhK, so r→1− as the temperature tends to zero. For even N, the finite antiferromagnetic Ising-ring correlation becomes
CN​(n)=(−1)n1+rNrn+rN−n​⟹βJ→∞lim​CN​(n)=(−1)n(N even).​
(33)
The two perfectly alternating ground states give exactly the same product S1​Sn+1​=(−1)n; averaging them preserves its magnitude even though the one-point correlation function remains zero.
For odd N the signs instead give
CN​(n)=(−1)n1−rNrn−rN−n​.
(34)
Both numerator and denominator vanish. Set r=1−δ and expand at fixed N,n:
rn−rN−n=(N−2n)δ+O(δ2),1−rN=Nδ+O(δ2).
(35)
Hence
βJ→∞lim​CN​(n)=(−1)n(1−N2n​)(N odd).​
(36)
There is a direct ground state interpretation. The mandatory parallel bond of an odd antiferromagnetic Ising ring is uniformly distributed among its N bonds in the limiting canonical ensemble. Along the directed n-bond arc from site 1 to site n+1, the product is (−1)n if the defect lies outside that arc, and −(−1)n if it lies inside. The respective probabilities are 1−n/N and n/N, giving the displayed two-point correlation function. Global spin inversion symmetry doubles the number of ground states but leaves each product unchanged.
The reduction on an odd ring survives at separations comparable with N, despite the vanishing defect density 1/N. For fixed n, however, taking N→∞ after the zero-temperature limit recovers (−1)n for either parity, consistently with the diverging correlation length. The linear envelope at finite odd N comes from one freely located frustration defect; it is distinct from the exponential decay caused by thermal defects in the thermodynamic limit.

The canonical anticommutation relations imply a2=(a†)2=0. Thus the number operator satisfies
Na2​=a†aa†a=a†(1−a†a)a=Na​.
(37)
It is a orthogonal projection, so its eigenvalues obey na2​=na​ and are na​∈{0,1}​. The same argument gives nb​∈{0,1}​. This is the Pauli exclusion principle for each orbital. The two number operators commute, so they have simultaneous eigenstates.
Take the vacuum state to have unit norm and fix the double-occupation convention ∣11⟩=a†b†∣0⟩. An orthonormal basis of the four-dimensional fermionic Fock space is
state∣00⟩=∣0⟩∣10⟩=a†∣0⟩∣01⟩=b†∣0⟩∣11⟩=a†b†∣0⟩​na​0101​nb​0011​​​
(38)
Each individual number operator has two-dimensional eigenspaces: for Na​ these are spanned by {∣00⟩,∣01⟩} and {∣10⟩,∣11⟩}; for Nb​ they are spanned by {∣00⟩,∣10⟩} and {∣01⟩,∣11⟩}. Higher occupation numbers vanish because the square of a fermionic creation operator is zero. Reversing the order of the two fermionic creation operators changes only the sign convention for ∣11⟩.

The Bogoliubov transformation and its Hermitian conjugate read
a=uc+vd†,a†=uc†+vd,b=−vc†+ud,b†=−vc+ud†.
(39)
Using the canonical anticommutation relations of c,d, the nonzero same-mode anticommutators are
{a,a†}=u2{c,c†}+v2{d†,d}=u2+v2,{b,b†}=u2+v2.
(40)
The equal-type anticommutators satisfy {a,a}={b,b}=0, since every contributing equal-type or mixed-mode anticommutator of c,d vanishes. The potentially nontrivial cross term cancels:
{a,b}=−uv{c,c†}+uv{d†,d}=−uv+uv=0.
(41)
Also {a,b†}=0, and the other cross relations follow by Hermitian conjugation. Therefore the necessary and sufficient condition is
u2+v2=1.​
(42)
The real transformation matrix is then an orthogonal matrix, with inverse
c=ua−vb†,d=va†+ub.​
(43)
This preservation of the canonical anticommutation relations makes it a canonical Bogoliubov transformation.
