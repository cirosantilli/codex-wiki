The Brownian motion displacement has a normal distribution with variance Γt. Its transition probability density function is therefore
p(yf​,t∣yi​,0)=2πΓt​1​exp[−2Γt(yf​−yi​)2​].
(15)
Comparison with the free particle heat kernel obtained in part (c) gives the required matching:
Γt=mβℏ2​.​
(16)
This matches whole path distributions after a linear reparametrization, not merely endpoint variances. To see this, divide the Brownian duration t into M slices of size δt. The independent increments have normal distributions with variance Γδt, so their joint density contributes
∏j=1M​2πΓδt​1​exp[−∑j=1M​2Γδt(yj​−yj−1​)2​].
(17)
For the Euclidean path integral, the corresponding expression is
∏j=1M​2πℏδτm​​exp[−∑j=1M​2ℏδτm(xj​−xj−1​)2​],δτ=Mβℏ​.
(18)
The matching condition makes Γδt=ℏδτ/m, so both the exponents and all normalization factors coincide. In particular, identifying Brownian time with imaginary time, t=βℏ, requires Γ=ℏ/m, or diffusion coefficient D=ℏ/(2m).
With both endpoints fixed, the normalized path law is a Brownian bridge. The Euclidean path integral itself is the endpoint density times that conditional path law. In the normalized time coordinate s=τ/(βℏ), its expected value and covariance are
E[x(s)]=xi​+s(xf​−xi​),Cov(x(s),x(s′))=mβℏ2​[min(s,s′)−ss′].
(19)
The classical path is therefore the bridge's expected value, while individual Brownian bridge paths fluctuate about it. Typical Brownian motion paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable: the kinetic Euclidean action is interpreted through the time-sliced diffusion path integral, rather than by assuming that a typical path has an ordinary velocity.
