Mathematics studies patterns in quantity, structure, space, and change by giving objects precise mathematical definitions and examining what follows from them. Arithmetic starts with operations on numbers; algebra organizes relations and transformations; geometry and topology studies shapes and the structures preserved by maps; analysis studies limits and continuous change. These fields connect because the same structure can appear in different forms.
A small example shows the value of changing viewpoint. A point moving around the unit circle has coordinates x=cosθ, y=sinθ. Regard the plane as complex numbers and the same point is z=eiθ, by Euler's formula. Adding two angles now corresponds to multiplying their complex exponentials: eiαeiβ=ei(α+β). A geometric rotation has become an algebraic operation.
The connection reaches calculus too. Differentiating twice gives x′′+x=0 and y′′+y=0, so the two coordinates solve the harmonic oscillator equation. At angle θ=π/3, z=1/2+i3​/2; one point supplies an exact cosine, an exact sine, and the state of an oscillation. The Pythagorean trigonometric identity records that the modulus stays one. Seeing these as descriptions of one object makes each formula easier to remember and reuse.
This is why a useful mathematical explanation provides both a reliable mathematical proof and a way to see what the result is doing. In his 1994 essay, William Thurston discusses mathematical progress in terms of advancing understanding, and uses the many interpretations of a derivative to illustrate this aim. [ref] A diagram, a computed example, and a concise argument can make different parts of the same idea accessible.
Applications ask us to select a mathematical model of a physical or other system. Its assumptions determine which conclusions transfer to that system. Within mathematics, the same practice of making assumptions explicit lets a result about groups, vector spaces, or probability distributions apply to many concrete examples. The beauty is the combination of precision and reuse: an argument can reveal more than the particular case that first suggested it.
