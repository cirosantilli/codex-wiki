Minimax theorem (maxx​miny​xTAy=miny​maxx​xTAy)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Game theory Zero-sum game
OurBigBook.com Words: 205
For every real m×n payoff matrix A of a two-player zero-sum game, the minimax theorem states
maxx∈Δm​​miny∈Δn​​xTAy=miny∈Δn​​maxx∈Δm​​xTAy=v,
(39)
where Δm​,Δn​ are the players' probability simplices. Both outer optima are attained. The row player's security level payoff is the largest payoff it can guarantee against all opposing choices; the column player can hold that payoff to the same number v, the value of a zero-sum game. For optimal x∗,y∗,
xTAy∗≤v=(x∗)TAy∗≤(x∗)TAyfor all x∈Δm​, y∈Δn​.
(40)
These are exactly the Nash equilibrium inequalities under the row-maximizes, column-minimizes convention. Conversely, a Nash equilibrium of this two-player zero-sum game supplies both guarantees. This equality explains why independent worst-case optimization solves the opposing incentives in this special class.
Allowing mixed strategies is essential. For matching pennies, A=(1−1​−11​). Restricting both players to pure strategies gives maxi​minj​Aij​=−1 and minj​maxi​Aij​=1. Allowing mixed strategies closes the gap: each fair mixture guarantees zero, so v=0. These guarantees hold even if the opponent knows the mixture's probabilities, provided the opponent does not observe the sampled choice before choosing.
John von Neumann proved the two-player zero-sum game result in Zur Theorie der Gesellschaftsspiele, Mathematische Annalen 100, 295–320 (1928); the original article's footnote 7 explicitly discusses the equality of max–min and min–max. Nash's [ref] states that existence of a Nash equilibrium is equivalent to this result in the two-player zero-sum game case, and extends existence to all finite games.
