Polar-coordinate proof of the Gaussian integral (I(1)2=2π∫0∞​re−r2dr=π)

Put I=I(1). The nonnegative integrand allows Tonelli's theorem to write the square as a double integral:
I2=∫R2​e−(x2+y2)dxdy.
(27)
In polar coordinates, x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ, and the Jacobian determinant supplies the area factor r:
I2=∫02π​∫0∞​e−r2rdrdθ=2π[−21​e−r2]0∞​=π.
(28)
Since I>0, take the positive square root to get I=π​. The computation proves finiteness as well as the value. For an explicit limiting argument, first integrate over disks of radius R; their integrals are π(1−e−R2), which increase to π as R→∞. [ref]
The geometric surprise is that radial shells get longer as r increases, even while the Gaussian function gets smaller. Its central height is largest at zero, but the radial contribution 2πre−r2 vanishes there and peaks at r=1/2​. Differentiating gives 2πe−r2(1−2r2), which changes sign at that radius. Keeping the Jacobian determinant is what makes the one-dimensional integral and the plane geometry agree.
