Asymmetric coordination example ((p∗,q∗)=(3/5,2/5))

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Game theory Bimatrix game Coordination game
OurBigBook.com Words: 359
Two players independently choose venues A or B. Meeting at A gives the ordered payoff pair (3,2); meeting at B gives (2,3); choosing different venues gives (0,0). As throughout this article, the row player's payoff comes first:
Row choice / column choiceAB
A(3,2)(0,0)
B(0,0)(2,3)
Against a column player choosing A, the row player gets 3 from A and 0 from B, so A is the unique best response. Against B, the row player gets 0 from A and 2 from B, so B is the unique best response. The column player likewise prefers to match the row choice. Thus (A,A) and (B,B) are pure strategy Nash equilibria, while each mismatched strategy profile has a profitable unilateral deviation.
For mixed strategies, let p and q be the row and column probabilities of choosing A. The pure-choice expected payoffs are
U1​(A,q)=3q,U1​(B,q)=2(1−q),U2​(p,A)=2p,U2​(p,B)=3(1−p).
(31)
The row player is indifferent when 3q=2(1−q), giving q=2/5. The column player is indifferent when 2p=3(1−p), giving p=3/5. The best responses are therefore
BR1​(q)=⎩⎨⎧​{0}[0,1]{1}​q<2/5,q=2/5,q>2/5,​BR2​(p)=⎩⎨⎧​{0}[0,1]{1}​p<3/5,p=3/5,p>3/5.​
(32)
These give exactly three Nash equilibria: (p,q)=(0,0),(1,1),(3/5,2/5). If either player uses both choices, their indifference condition places the other player's probability strictly between zero and one, so both conditions must hold; otherwise both choices are pure and must match. Notice whose equation determines whose probability: the row player's indifference fixes the column player's q, and the column player's indifference fixes the row player's p.
At the mixed Nash equilibrium, independence gives
Pr(A,A)=256​,Pr(A,B)=259​,Pr(B,A)=254​,Pr(B,B)=256​.
(33)
Thus they meet with probability 12/25, and each receives expected payoff 3(6/25)+2(6/25)=6/5. Holding q=2/5 fixed, every row deviation p′ gives p′(6/5)+(1−p′)(6/5)=6/5; holding p=3/5 fixed, every column deviation q′ gives q′(6/5)+(1−q′)(6/5)=6/5. This directly verifies the Nash equilibrium inequalities.
Both coordinated pure strategy outcomes strictly improve both players' expected payoffs over (6/5,6/5): (3,2) and (2,3) each do so. The mixed Nash equilibrium therefore fails Pareto efficiency, even though no unilateral deviation helps. Switching together can help when switching alone cannot. Neither coordinated outcome improves both players over the other, and both are Pareto efficient, even when a lottery over joint action profiles is allowed: any positive weight on another outcome reduces the payoff of the player receiving 3.
