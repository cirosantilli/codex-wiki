Mean value theorem (f′(c)=b−af(b)−f(a)​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Calculus
OurBigBook.com Words: 493 Articles: 2
The mean value theorem says that an average rate of change is attained as an instantaneous rate somewhere between the endpoints. Let a<b and let the real-valued function f:[a,b]→R be continuous on the closed interval [a,b] and differentiable on the open interval (a,b). Then there exists c∈(a,b) such that
f′(c)=b−af(b)−f(a)​.
(68)
The right-hand side is the slope of the secant line joining (a,f(a)) to (b,f(b)); the left-hand side is the slope of the tangent line at (c,f(c)). The derivative need not be continuous, and the function need not be differentiable at the endpoints. [ref]
For the polynomial f(x)=x2 on [1,3], the secant line has slope (9−1)/(3−1)=4. Since the derivative is f′(x)=2x, the required interior point is c=2. The tangent line there is y=4+4(x−2)=4x−4, whereas the secant line is y=1+4(x−1)=4x−3. Their equal slopes make them parallel, but they are distinct straight lines: the theorem equates rates, not heights.
The practical strength is that the unknown point c can disappear from an estimate. Under the same hypotheses, if the derivative satisfies ∣f′(x)∣≤M throughout (a,b), where M≥0, then
∣f(b)−f(a)∣=∣f′(c)∣∣b−a∣≤M∣b−a∣.
(69)
Applying this to every subinterval proves Lipschitz continuity with Lipschitz bound M. For example, f(x)=x2 on [1,3] has ∣f′(x)∣≤6, so an input change of size at most δ changes its output by at most 6δ, as long as both inputs lie in that interval. A local derivative bound therefore controls finite changes without computing an integral. The sign of the derivative similarly gives the derivative criterion for monotonicity.
Taking M=0 shows that a function with zero derivative throughout a real interval is a constant function, provided it is continuous at any included endpoints. The interval condition is essential: on the domain of a function (−2,−1)∪(1,2), the function equal to zero on the left interval and one on the right interval has zero derivative everywhere. It is a locally constant function, yet not a constant function on the whole domain of a function. The mean value theorem cannot cross the gap.
A concise proof subtracts the secant line: set m=(f(b)−f(a))/(b−a) and h(x)=f(x)−f(a)−m(x−a). Then h(a)=h(b)=0, and Rolle's theorem gives an interior point with h′(c)=f′(c)−m=0. The geometric idea is to remove the average trend and find a horizontal tangent line in what remains.
