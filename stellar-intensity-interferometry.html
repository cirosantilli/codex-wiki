Stellar intensity interferometry

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum theory Quantum optics Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 5
Stellar intensity interferometry measures a star's angular diameter by comparing fluctuations of detected light intensity at separated collectors. Each collector has its own photodetector; an analog multiplier or a digital correlator compares the electrical outputs. Changing the interferometric baseline changes the sampled spatial optical coherence. An unresolved star produces correlated fluctuations; a resolved extended source supplies different mixtures of emission directions, reducing their correlation.
This makes a useful telescope out of a surprising observable: the fluctuations discarded by an ordinary average-brightness measurement. Optical beams need not travel to a common combiner. A relative wave phase perturbation E2​↦eiϕE2​ leaves ∣E2​∣2 unchanged, so pure phase errors do not directly change the local light intensity. Electronic delay alignment remains necessary; intensity changes from atmospheric scintillation, collection losses, and instrumental noise still require controls.
Let b join the collectors and let s0​ be the unit line of sight toward the star. The relevant interferometric baseline is its projection onto the plane of the sky:
b⊥​=b−(b⋅s0​)s0​,B=∣b⊥​∣.
(11)
The reference arrival-time difference has magnitude ∣b⋅s0​∣/c, with speed of light c. Correct that delay, with a sign fixed by channel order, before averaging the residual correlation at τ=0. The physical collector separation equals B only when it is perpendicular to s0​; tracking a star can change the projection even for fixed collectors.
