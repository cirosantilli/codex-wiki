The minimal polynomial of a linear transformation T is the unique monic polynomial of least degree among the nonzero polynomials p∈F[x] satisfying p(T)=0:
mT​ monic,mT​(T)=0,degmT​ least possible.​
(1)
Such a polynomial exists because the endomorphism space is finite-dimensional, so sufficiently many powers I,T,T2,… are linearly dependent. Monic normalization is possible over the field F. Two monic polynomials annihilating T of the same least degree have a difference of smaller degree annihilating T, so their difference is zero. For the zero vector space the convention is mT​=1.

The first isomorphism theorem for rings states that the map
R/kerf⟶imf,r+kerf⟼f(r)​
(21)
is a ring isomorphism. It is well defined because two representatives differ by an element of the kernel exactly when their images agree. It preserves addition and multiplication, is onto its stated codomain, and has trivial kernel. In particular, if f is surjective then R/kerf≅S.

Define Φ:R→R/I×R/J by Φ(r)=(r+I,r+J). It is a ring homomorphism with
kerΦ=I∩J.
(22)
Since I+J=R, choose i∈I,j∈J such that i+j=1. For arbitrary a,b∈R, the element r=aj+bi satisfies r−a=i(b−a)∈I and r−b=j(a−b)∈J. Hence Φ(r)=(a+I,b+J), proving surjectivity. The first isomorphism theorem now gives
R/(I∩J)≅R/I×R/J.​
(23)
This is the two-ideal Chinese remainder theorem for ideals. The hypothesis is comaximality of the two ideals; no assertion that every pair of ideals has this property is needed.

Because g,h are nonconstant, their classes in Q[x]/(gh) are nonzero: each has smaller degree than gh, so neither is a multiple of gh. Their product is zero. Thus the quotient has zero divisors.
If both factors have degree one, each quotient by one factor is isomorphic to Q, by evaluation at its rational root. Coprimality ensures the two roots are distinct. Part (c) therefore identifies the ring with Q×Q. If (r,s)2=(0,0), then r2=s2=0 in a field, so r=s=0.
There is no nonzero square-zero element when both factors have degree one.​
(26)
A ring may therefore have zero divisors without having nonzero nilpotent elements.

A function is holomorphic on an open set when it is complex differentiable at every point of that set. Explicitly, for every z∈U, the limit
f′(z)=h→0, h∈C∖{0}lim​hf(z+h)−f(z)​​
(29)
exists as a finite complex number. The limit is independent of the direction in which the complex increment approaches zero; existence of derivatives only along the real axis would not suffice.

b OurBigBook.com
Cauchy's integral theorem says that if f is holomorphic on an open set U and a closed, piecewise smooth contour γ is null-homotopic in U, then
∫γ​f(z)dz=0.​
(30)
In particular, this holds for every closed contour when U is simply connected, and for a simple closed contour whose boundary and enclosed region lie in U. For the later arguments it suffices to use the triangular version: the integral around a triangle is zero if the triangle and its interior lie in U and f is holomorphic there.
Both the holomorphicity condition and a suitable condition excluding holes inside the contour matter. Continuity alone, which is the starting hypothesis for this question, does not imply the theorem's conclusion; nor does holomorphicity on an arbitrary punctured open set imply zero integral around every loop.

Morera's theorem states that a continuous function on an open subset of C is holomorphic if its integral around every triangular contour, with triangle and interior contained in that open set, is zero.
To prove this, work in any open disk D whose closure lies in U, and fix z0​∈D. Define
F(z)=∫[z0​,z]​f(w)dw,
(31)
where the segment lies in D by convexity. The zero integral on the triangle with vertices z0​,z,z+h gives
F(z+h)−F(z)=∫[z,z+h]​f(w)dw=h∫01​f(z+th)dt.
(32)
Continuity at z implies that the last integral tends to f(z) as h→0: its difference from f(z) is bounded by sup0≤t≤1​∣f(z+th)−f(z)∣. Therefore F′=f on D. The function F is holomorphic, and the allowed theorem that a holomorphic derivative is holomorphic implies that f=F′ is holomorphic on D.
Every point of U lies in such a disk, proving the result throughout U:
continuity plus vanishing triangle integrals implies holomorphicity.​
(33)
The proof is local and does not require U to be simply connected.

First take z∈U−​ and put w=zˉ∈U+​. Reflection symmetry of the function gives
hf(z+h)−f(z)​=hˉf(w+hˉ)−f(w)​​⟶f′(w)​.
(37)
Hence f is holomorphic on U−​, with f′(z)=f′(zˉ)​ there.
It remains to cross the real axis. Fix a real point p∈U and a small disk about p with closure in U. Consider a triangle in a still smaller disk. Intersect its filled interior with the closed upper and lower half-planes and triangulate the resulting convex polygons. This gives finitely many triangles lying on one side of the real axis, possibly with edges on that axis. Degenerate pieces contribute zero.
For an upper piece, translate its three vertices by iε with ε>0. For sufficiently small ε the translated triangle lies entirely in U+​ and remains in U, so its boundary integral is zero by Cauchy's theorem. Part (d), or its segment-integral proof, shows that the integral converges to that around the untranslated piece as ε↓0. Thus the original piece has zero integral. Translate lower pieces by −iε to obtain the same conclusion in U−​.
When all the oriented triangle integrals are added, their internal edges cancel, including the segments on the real axis. The original triangle therefore has zero boundary integral. Continuity of f on U now allows Morera's theorem in the disk, proving holomorphicity near p. Together with the two half-plane arguments this gives
f is holomorphic throughout U.​
(38)
This is a Morera gluing lemma across a straight line; the original continuity hypothesis is essential to the limiting step.

A conformal map preserves oriented angles between regular smooth curves through each point. For a holomorphic map with nonzero derivative, the derivative also describes the local scale and rotation.
Indeed, if two curves γ1​,γ2​ meet at z and have nonzero tangent vectors v1​,v2​, the real chain rule and complex differentiability give
(f∘γj​)′=f′(z)vj​.
(49)
Because f′(z)=0, the image tangents are nonzero and
(f∘γ1​)′(f∘γ2​)′​=v1​v2​​.​
(50)
Taking arguments proves preservation of oriented angles. Multiplication by f′(z) rotates every tangent by argf′(z) and scales every length by ∣f′(z)∣. Thus the hypothesis gives conformality at every point of U; global injectivity is not needed for this local conclusion.

A Möbius transformation maps generalized circles to generalized circles on the Riemann sphere. A generalized circle is an ordinary circle or a straight line together with the point at infinity. Its image is a straight line precisely when it contains infinity, equivalently when the preimage of infinity lies on the extended real axis.
If c=0, the pole is infinity itself and the transformation is the affine map (a/d)z+b/d, taking the extended real axis to a line. If c=0, the pole is −d/c, which is real exactly when d/c is real. Thus the full condition is
c=0orc=0 and d/c∈R.​
(51)
Equivalently, it is Im(dcˉ)=0, a condition also valid when c=0. If it fails, the image misses infinity and is therefore an ordinary circle. The nonzero determinant ensures that none of these generalized circles collapses to a point.
