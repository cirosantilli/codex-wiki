Torsion balance

 Home Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 6
A torsion balance measures a weak torque by suspending a body from a thin fiber and reading its twist angle. The fiber supports the body's gravitational weight while permitting rotation about the suspension axis. In the range of linear elasticity, a twist θ in radians, measured from the untwisted reference orientation, produces a restoring torque
τfiber​=−κθ,κ>0.
(8)
The torsion constant κ sets the sensitivity. At static equilibrium with an applied torque τext​ and no other axial torque, τext​=κθ. A perpendicular force F at lever arm a therefore gives θ=aF/κ. The torsion balance measures a turning effect: a force whose line of action passes through the suspension axis gives no axial torque. The Cavendish torsion balance is the gravitational application; Coulomb's law supplies an electrical one. [ref]
The original schematic separates two ways of reading the same torque. In deflection operation the top attachment stays fixed and the body rotates. In null operation the experimenter rotates the top attachment by α while returning the body's laboratory angle β to its original value. If the initially untwisted configuration has α=β=0, the fiber twist is θ=β−α, not β alone. Thus
τfiber​=−κ(β−α),τext​=κ(β−α).
(9)
For a positive applied torque, holding β=0 requires α=−τext​/κ. Keeping the body at the same angle keeps the lever arm and source geometry fixed, making comparison of different applied forces easier. Either readout still requires a calibrated torsion constant and a stable reference orientation.
An illustrative calculation shows the amplification: with a=0.050m, F=2.0×10−9N, and κ=1.0×10−8Nm/rad, the applied torque is 1.0×10−10Nm and the equilibrium twist is 0.010rad≃0.573∘. These are stipulated values, not measured data. Reducing the torsion constant tenfold increases this twist tenfold, provided the fiber remains in its calibrated linear elasticity range. The cost is a slower response, explained by the oscillation period in torsion constant.
A concrete laboratory experiment is PASCO's Coulomb balance: level its counterbalanced rod, discharge both conducting spheres, and set the unloaded zero. Charge the spheres at maximum separation; switch off the charging supply. Move the second sphere to a known center separation R, then turn the suspension dial until the rod returns to its index mark. Record the dial change and R, recharge, and repeat at several separations. The manual recommends a draft-free room, clean insulating supports, and recharging before each reading: leakage, especially in humid conditions, otherwise changes the electric charges during the sequence. At close separations the conducting spheres redistribute charge, so the point charge approximation needs correction. [ref]
For constant electric charges and a fixed perpendicular lever arm, the Coulomb's law prediction is ∣θ∣∝R−2. Plotting the logarithm of twist magnitude against the logarithm of separation gives log∣θ∣=constant−2logR, so the predicted slope is −2. Absolute force measurement additionally needs the torsion constant; comparing ratios can eliminate it if it remains stable. Repeating a reference separation between other settings distinguishes a separation effect from a changing signal. A declining reading at the reference separation would invalidate the assumption of unchanged electric charges, even if a distance plot looks smooth.
The historical electrical example is Coulomb's first memoir of 1785. His apparatus enclosed a suspended needle with a small ball and a second ball; an adjustable head twisted the suspension. After charging the balls by contact, he reported angular separations 36∘, 18∘, and 8.5∘ with head settings 0∘, 126∘, and 567∘. The total twists were approximately 36∘, 144∘, and 576∘: fourfold restoring torque accompanied approximately halved separation. These are his reported observations, not data from our diagrams. [ref] A precise reconstruction must use center distances and the changing lever arm, rather than identify angular separation exactly with distance; Alberto A. Martínez's replication explicitly analyzes that geometry. [ref]
Sensitivity also exposes changes of the apparatus itself. Fleischer and colleagues' cryogenic torsion balance used a temperature-controlled enclosure to reduce vacuum-vessel top-plate temperature fluctuations to approximately ±0.01K, limiting changes in suspension geometry from thermal expansion. They used a mild bake-out near 40∘C to speed initial fiber relaxation and reduce slow zero motion. Their optical readout compared the pendulum mirror with an internal reference mirror to reject shared optical motion. Room-temperature thermal radiation entering through the optical opening heated the pendulum; a cold filter reduced that load. The authors could not directly measure the pendulum's temperature without disturbing it, and explicitly distinguished the cold shield's temperature from the body's. Cooling therefore requires checking the actual noise and thermal environment, rather than assuming that the fiber and body share the shield's temperature. [ref]
A useful control strategy is to record unloaded readings and temperature before and after each signal sequence, recalibrate the torsion constant under the same conditions, and compare forward and reverse settings. The algebra of torsion balance zero drift shows precisely which offsets such comparisons can cancel. A fine angular scale alone does not establish a small measurement error.
