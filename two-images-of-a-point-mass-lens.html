Choose a signed angular coordinate along the source-lens line. The point-mass gravitational lens becomes
β=θ−θθE2​​,θ=0.

For β=0, multiplication by θ gives the quadratic equation θ2−βθ−θE2​=0. Its positive discriminant yields the two displayed roots. Their product is θ+​θ−​=−θE2​, so they lie on opposite sides of the lens. With β>0, θ+​>θE​ and −θE​<θ−​<0: the outer image lies on the source side. For β<0, the outer image is instead the minus root. These are image directions seen by the observer, not two emitting objects.[ref]
For a dimensionless example, set u=β/θE​=0.6 and x=θ/θE​. Solving u=x−1/x gives x+​≃1.344 and x−​≃−0.744, with separation u2+4​≃2.088. The lensing magnification follows from image parity in gravitational lensing: μ+​≃1.442, μ−​≃−0.442, and total amplification ≃1.884. Changing M or the distances rescales the angles through the Einstein angular radius, while these dimensionless values remain the same.
For u≫1, the quadratic equation gives x+​≃u+1/u and x−​≃−1/u. Using μ=1/(1−x−4), the outer image tends to unit lensing magnification and the inner image has ∣μ−​∣≃u−4. Two mathematical images can therefore look like one at finite sensitivity or angular resolution. Furthermore, the inner impact parameter eventually becomes too small for the stated weak-field approximation or passes through the opaque lens: the ideal formula must then be replaced by an appropriate physical model.
