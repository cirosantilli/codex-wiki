Bézier curve (C(t)=∑i=0n​(in​)(1−t)n−itiPi​)

OurBigBook.com Words: 919 Articles: 3
A Bézier curve is a parametric curve specified by control points P0​,…,Pn​ in a Euclidean space and the Bernstein basis:
C(t)=∑i=0n​bi,n​(t)Pi​,bi,n​(t)=(in​)(1−t)n−iti,0≤t≤1.
(33)
Here (in​) is a binomial coefficient. Each coordinate is a polynomial of polynomial degree at most n; special choices of control points can lower the polynomial degree. The ordered control points form the control polygon. A cubic Bézier curve uses four control points and has the explicit form
C(t)=(1−t)3P0​+3(1−t)2tP1​+3(1−t)t2P2​+t3P3​.
(34)
The curve passes through P0​ and P3​; the interior control points steer its shape and need not lie on it.[ref]
For a worked example, choose
P0​=(0,0),P1​=(1,2),P2​=(3,2),P3​=(4,0).
(35)
Substituting these control points gives
C(t)=(3t+3t2−2t3,6t(1−t)),C(1/2)=81​P0​+83​P1​+83​P2​+81​P3​=(2,1.5).
(36)
Here y(t)=1.5−6(t−1/2)2≤1.5, so neither interior control point, both at height 2, lies on the curve. De Casteljau's algorithm computes the same point using only successive linear interpolations.
The endpoint tangent vectors follow from the Bézier derivative control polygon:
C′(0)=3(P1​−P0​)=(3,6),C′(1)=3(P3​−P2​)=(3,−6).
(37)
Thus the curve leaves P0​ toward P1​ and arrives at P3​ from P2​. These nonzero derivatives determine the endpoint tangent lines; if an endpoint derivative vanishes, that formula alone does not determine its tangent direction. Endpoint handles are useful when arranging a parametric first-derivative join of Bézier curves.
The parameter t is usually not arc length. For this example,
C′(t)=(3+6t−6t2,6−12t),∥C′(0)∥=45​,∥C′(1/2)∥=4.5.
(38)
Equal steps in t therefore do not generally travel equal distances. Distance along the curve is instead the arc length, given by the integral s(t)=∫0t​∥C′(u)∥du; its derivative is the norm of the tangent vector and measures the rate of travel.
Two geometric facts make Bézier curves especially convenient for design. First, the Bernstein basis weights are nonnegative and form a partition of unity, since the binomial theorem gives ∑i​bi,n​(t)=((1−t)+t)n=1. Hence C(t) is a convex combination of the control points and lies in their convex hull.[ref] The convex hull is the smallest convex set containing the control points, not their axis-aligned bounding box. Here it is a trapezoid, strictly smaller than [0,4]×[0,2]: the box corner (0,2) is outside the convex hull. The curve need not fill or touch every edge of either enclosure. The convex hull guarantee concerns 0≤t≤1; outside that interval weights can be negative.
Second, applying an affine map to every control point gives exactly the same result as applying it to the entire curve. For T(x)=Ax+b,
∑i​bi,n​(t)T(Pi​)=A∑i​bi,n​(t)Pi​+b∑i​bi,n​(t)=T(C(t)).
(39)
This is affine equivariance of a geometric basis: a translation, rotation or shear mapping can be performed on a handful of control points. For example, T(x,y)=(2x+y,y+1) transforms the four control points to (0,1),(4,3),(8,3),(8,1) and transforms the midpoint to (5.5,2.5), also obtained from the same weights (1,3,3,1)/8.
These editable handles explain the usefulness of Bézier curves in computer aided geometric design, vector graphics paths and outline fonts: a small collection of points controls an entire smooth stroke, and resizing the design preserves its mathematical shape. Cambridge's computer-graphics notes describe their use in graphic design, typography and PostScript outlines. The industrial context includes Pierre Bézier's work at Renault and Paul de Casteljau's work at Citroën.[ref]
