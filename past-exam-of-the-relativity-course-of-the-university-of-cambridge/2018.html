c OurBigBook.com
The cosmological principle gives spatial homogeneity and cosmological isotropy for the large-scale spatial slices. About any chosen comoving observer, spherical coordinates therefore separate a radial direction from the angular directions. In particular,
dΩ2=dθ2+sin2θdϕ2
(7)
is the metric tensor on the unit sphere. Its appearance expresses cosmological isotropy, rather than a privileged centre of the universe. The factor (1−Kr2)−1 permits the radial geometry to have nonzero spatial curvature of an FLRW universe.
The scale factor a(t) changes every spatial separation between fixed comoving coordinates by the same multiplicative factor. For example, the radial proper distance in cosmology between fixed radial labels at a fixed cosmic time is
D(t)=a(t)∫r1​r2​​1−Kr2​dr​.
(8)
Expansion or contraction through this single function preserves spatial homogeneity and cosmological isotropy; direction-dependent scale factors would generally spoil the latter. The Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric specifies this possible evolution geometrically; a dynamical theory is needed to determine a(t).
The constant K fixes the sign and normalization of the spatial curvature of an FLRW universe. The spatial scalar curvature is (3)R=6K/a2(t). With dimensionless r and a suitably normalized scale factor, the familiar choices are K=+1,0,−1 for spherical, flat, and hyperbolic spatial geometry. A rescaling of r changes the normalization of K and a together; its sign has invariant geometric significance. The local metric tensor alone does not fix global topology.
The coordinates share a cosmological, comoving construction: r,θ,ϕ label the worldlines of the smooth cosmic fluid, and t is the common cosmic time measured as proper time along those worldlines. Observers following that fluid keep their spatial labels while the scale factor changes their physical separations. The coordinate t itself increases; it is not another constant spatial label. Thus the question's final phrase is understood as asking for their common adaptation to the cosmological fluid, rather than suggesting that all four coordinates are time independent or have the same units.

The two postulates of special relativity are: all nongravitational laws have the same form in every inertial frame, and light in vacuum has the same finite speed of light c in every inertial frame, independently of the motion of its source. The first is Einstein's principle of relativity; the second supplies an experimentally identified invariant speed.
For the stated standard configuration, the Lorentz transformation is
x′=γv​(x−vt),t′=γv​(t−c2vx​),y′=y,z′=z,γv​=1−v2/c2​1​.
(9)
Take differentials along the particle's worldline, rather than comparing coordinate displacements at equal times in the two frames. Writing ui​=dxi/dt, one obtains
dt′=γv​(1−c2vux​​)dt,dx′=γv​(ux​−v)dt,dy′=uy​dt,dz′=uz​dt.
(10)
Dividing by the transformed time interval proves the velocity-addition formula:
ux′​=1−vux​/c2ux​−v​,uy′​=γv​(1−vux​/c2)uy​​,uz′​=γv​(1−vux​/c2)uz​​.​
(11)
For a massive particle, ∣u∣<c and ∣v∣<c, so the denominator 1−vux​/c2 is positive.

dtdγu​​=−21​(1−c2u⋅u​)−3/2(−c22u⋅a​)=c2γu3​u⋅a​​.

Aμ=γu​dtd​[γu​(c,u)]=(cγu4​u⋅a​, γu2​a+c2γu4​(u⋅a)u​)​.

Write Γa​(v)=(1−v2/a2)−1/2. The first proposed Lorentz transformation is
x′=Γa​(v)(x−vt),t′=Γa​(v)(t−a2vx​).
(15)
For the next transformation, write Γb​(u)=(1−u2/b2)−1/2 and substitute the first pair into x′′=Γb​(u)(x′−ut′) and t′′=Γb​(u)(t′−ux′/b2). The result is
x′′t′′​=Γa​(v)Γb​(u)[(1+a2uv​)x−(u+v)t],=Γa​(v)Γb​(u)[(1+b2uv​)t−(a2v​+b2u​)x].​
(16)
The direct transformation between S and S′′ must belong to the same standard boost family with the same calibration of space and time units. Such a transformation has one common factor multiplying the coefficient of x in x′′ and that of t in t′′. Equality of these coefficients for generic uv=0 requires
1+a2uv​=1+b2uv​,a2=b2.​
(17)
The case uv=0 alone cannot constrain the scales, but a transformation law must work for arbitrary successive boosts. Once the scales agree, their composed velocity is V=(u+v)/(1+uv/a2) and the common factor is Γa​(V). This establishes the universality of an invariant speed under boost composition within the proposed family.
Finiteness needs physical input. Sending a2 to infinity gives the consistent Galilean transformation x′=x−vt, t′=t. Its boosts compose with V=u+v and satisfy a relativity principle. Thus the composition calculation itself does not prove that a2 cannot be infinite. The finite propagation speed of light in vacuum and its invariance, supplied by the second of the postulates of special relativity, rule out this limit for physical spacetime. Under a Galilean transformation, a ray with laboratory speed c would have speed c−v in the moving frame, in conflict with that postulate.
Finally, the common invariant scale must be identified with the physical speed of light, rather than an arbitrary speed measured in one frame. Requiring a ray x=ct also to obey x′=ct′ gives
1−vc/a2c−v​=c⟹a2=c2(v=0).
(18)
Hence the physical scale is a=c, taking the positive speed. The postulate about light identifies and requires a finite invariant scale; closure alone only establishes its universality. This distinction is necessary for a logically valid answer to the question's request to exclude infinity.

Use the +−−− metric signature and abbreviate f(r)=1−2μ/r, with μ=GM/c2 a length. Dots initially mean differentiation with respect to an affine parameter λ. The geodesic Lagrangian in the equatorial plane is
L=21​(c2ft˙2−fr˙2​−r2ϕ˙​2).
(19)
The planarity of geodesics in spherical symmetry permits this plane to be chosen. More explicitly, the full angular Euler-Lagrange equation is θ¨+2r˙θ˙/r−sinθcosθϕ˙​2=0, so initial data θ=π/2, θ˙=0 preserve it.
Both t and ϕ are cyclic coordinates. Their Euler-Lagrange equations give conserved conjugate momenta pt​=c2ft˙ and pϕ​=−r2ϕ˙​. Defining constants k,h therefore gives
ft˙=k,r2ϕ˙​=h.​
(20)
The corresponding second-order geodesic equations are
t¨+ff′​r˙t˙=0,ϕ¨​+r2​r˙ϕ˙​=0,
(21)
and variation of r gives
r¨−2ff′​r˙2+2c2ff′​t˙2−frϕ˙​2=0,f′=r22μ​.​
(22)
The geodesic Lagrangian has no explicit λ dependence. Its conserved Lagrangian energy function is x˙α∂L/∂x˙α−L=L, since it is quadratic in the tangent components. Thus twice this function is a constant:
c2ft˙2−fr˙2​−r2ϕ˙​2=κ.​
(23)
This is the constant squared tangent norm of an affinely parametrized geodesic.
For a massive particle choose λ=τ, its proper time, and obtain κ=c2. For a photon, the tangent is null and κ=0. A general rescaling of a massive particle's affine parameter rescales a positive κ; the numerical value c2 requires the proper time choice. With that choice, h=Lz​/m is signed specific angular momentum, of units m2s−1, and k=E/(mc2) is dimensionless conserved specific Killing energy. The time coordinate is normalized to proper time at infinity, so this E is the particle's conserved energy referred to infinity. A photon's constants scale with the normalization of its null tangent; it has no rest frame or proper time parametrization.
Substitution of t˙=k/f and ϕ˙​=h/r2 into the norm gives the general radial first integral
r˙2=c2k2−f(κ+r2h2​).
(24)
For a massive particle this becomes
c2(k2−1)=r˙2−r2μc2​+r2h2​(1−r2μ​).​
(25)
Adopting the usual half-normalization for the effective potential for timelike Schwarzschild geodesics, define
21​r˙2+Veff​(r)=2c2​(k2−1),Veff​(r)=−rμc2​+2r2h2​−r3μh2​.​
(26)
All terms have units m2s−2, or energy per unit mass. The first two terms are the Newtonian gravitational and centrifugal contributions; the last is the relativistic correction.
A circular orbit requires both r˙=0 and Veff′​(R)=0. Differentiating at fixed h gives
0=R2μc2​−R3h2​+R43μh2​,h2=R−3μμc2R2​​,R>3μ.
(27)
The radial first integral also gives
k2=R(R−3μ)(R−2μ)2​.
(28)
For the future-directed circular orbit at R=4μ, these constants are ∣h∣=4μc, k=1, and f=1/2. Therefore
t˙=2,∣ϕ˙​∣=4μc​,​dtdϕ​​=8μc​.
(29)
Since Schwarzschild time agrees with the clock time of a static observer at infinity, the intended infinity-referred tangential orbital speed is
v∞​=R​dtdϕ​​=2c​.​
(30)
This uses the circumferential areal radius R with the clock at infinity; it is not a local measurement made at the orbit. A local static observer's clock instead has dτstat​=f​dt and measures vlocal​=v∞​/f​=c/2​​. These two operationally different speeds explain the significance of the phrase “at infinity”.
The effective-potential stability of a circular orbit follows from
Veff′′​(R)=R3(R−3μ)μc2(R−6μ)​.
(31)
Consequently the R=4μ orbit exists but is unstable to radial perturbations. The original plot below shows its position at a maximum, for h=4μc.
For a weak-field elliptic orbit, the relativistic correction makes the direction of closest approach advance after each radial cycle. The derivations under i and ii below give
Δϕ=c2aorb​(1−e2)6πGM​​
(32)
per orbit to first order, where aorb​ is the semi-major axis and e the orbital eccentricity. For Mercury, the additional Schwarzschild perihelion precession is approximately 43 arcseconds per century, over and above the much larger Newtonian precession produced primarily by other planets. It is the unexplained residual after those effects have been included that provides the classic quantitative test of general relativity; one should not identify the whole observed precession with this relativistic correction.[ref]

Local equivalence and tides. In Newtonian mechanics, inertial mass mi​ measures the response to a force, whereas passive gravitational mass mg​ couples to an imposed Newtonian gravitational field. A freely falling body obeys
mi​a=mg​g,a=mi​mg​​g.
(42)
The observed composition independence of free fall says that this ratio is universal. With a common choice of units it is one. This is the Newtonian content of the weak equivalence principle for test particles; it does not by itself specify all nongravitational physics.
The Einstein equivalence principle further says that local nongravitational experiments in a sufficiently small freely falling laboratory have the form predicted by special relativity, independently of the laboratory's location and motion. In such a local inertial frame, the metric tensor is locally the Minkowski metric and the Christoffel symbols vanish at the selected event. The strong equivalence principle extends the universality claim to local gravitational experiments and bodies with significant gravitational self-energy. None of these statements removes measurable tidal forces across a finite apparatus.
In an ideal small freely falling lift, a released ball initially floats relative to the cabin, because both undergo the same free fall. Two separated balls, however, need not retain their separation: they sample different parts of the gravitational field. For a point source, linearizing g=−GMr/r3 across a small separation ξ gives the tidal tensor prediction
dt2d2ξ​=r3GM​[3r(r⋅ξ)−ξ].​
(43)
A radial separation stretches with coefficient 2GM/r3, whereas a transverse separation is compressed with coefficient −GM/r3. Initially parallel velocities therefore develop relative motion even though the individual bodies experience zero proper acceleration.
The relativistic statement is geodesic deviation. With the Riemann curvature tensor convention
Rμνρσ​=∂ρ​Γμσν​−∂σ​Γμρν​+Γμρα​Γασν​−Γμσα​Γαρν​,
(44)
a connecting vector between neighboring freely falling worldlines satisfies
dτ2D2ξμ​=−Rμνρσ​UνξρUσ.​
(45)
Here D/dτ is the covariant derivative along the reference geodesic, and Uμ its four-velocity. Curvature can be nonzero where the Christoffel symbols have been made zero. In the weak, static, slow-motion limit this equation reduces to ξ¨​i=−∂i​∂j​Φξj, recovering the tidal tensor above. A finite falling lift can detect gravity through these relative accelerations; local equivalence does not imply a globally uniform field.
Weak-field connection and potential. Take x0=ct and the +−−− metric signature. There is an index error in the printed formula for the Christoffel symbols: its first differentiated metric component must be gν0​, not gμ0​. The correctly contracted expression is
Γμ00​=21​gμν(2∂0​gν0​−∂ν​g00​).
(46)
The offending term vanishes under the stipulated time independence, so this correction does not change the intended calculation. Write gμν​=ημν​+hμν​ and treat both h and its derivatives as first order. To that order g00=1−h00​, g0i=O(h), and gij=−δij+O(h). A stationary metric tensor gives
Γμ00​=−21​gμj∂j​h00​.
(47)
For the temporal component the inverse-metric coefficient is already first order, so
Γ000​=O(h∂h)≈0.​
(48)
For a spatial component the zeroth-order inverse metric contributes:
Γi00​≈21​δij∂j​h00​=21​∂xi∂h00​​.​
(49)
The time component of the approximate geodesic equation says that dt/dτ is constant to the retained order. In its spatial component, convert the proper time derivatives to coordinate-time derivatives and cancel (dt/dτ)2:
dt2d2xi​≈−c2Γi00​≈−2c2​∂i​h00​.
(50)
Comparison with the Newtonian equation d2x/dt2=−∇Φ identifies h00​=2Φ/c2+C. For an isolated source, choose the gravitational potential to tend to zero at infinity and normalize the time coordinate there, giving C=0. Thus
g00​≈1+c22Φ​.​
(51)
The quantity Φ is the Newtonian gravitational potential, or gravitational potential energy per unit mass, in Jkg−1=m2s−2. For a point source, Φ=−GM/r. A static clock has dτ=g00​​dt≈(1+Φ/c2)dt, connecting the Newtonian limit with gravitational time dilation.
Frequency of the orbiting source. Treat the gas blob as a test particle on a timelike circular Schwarzschild geodesic, with pressure and other nongravitational forces negligible. Treat our very distant location as infinity, so the observer's own gravitational potential is negligible. These are the assumptions that make a unique answer in terms of RS​/r possible.
Put f=1−RS​/r and use a local static observer at the emission point as an intermediate measuring frame. The remote observer lies on the symmetry axis of the orbit. A ray arriving on that axis has zero conserved axial angular momentum, so its local direction is perpendicular to the emitter's azimuthal velocity. Bending within a meridional plane does not alter this orthogonality. The transverse relativistic Doppler effect therefore gives
νstat​=γv​νem​​,γv​=1−v2/c2​1​,
(52)
where v is the orbital speed measured by that local static observer. The gravitational redshift between static Schwarzschild observers then gives
νem​νob​​=γv​1−RS​/r​​.
(53)
A gravitational circular orbit has v2=GM/r+O((GM/r)2/c2) in the weak-field limit, so v2/c2=RS​/(2r)+O((RS​/r)2). Expanding both factors,
νem​νob​​=(1−2rRS​​)(1−4rRS​​)+O(r2RS2​​)=1−4r3RS​​+O(r2RS2​​).
(54)
Solving for the emitted frequency gives
νem​=νob​[1+4r3RS​​+O(r2RS2​​)].​
(55)
The gravitational part is RS​/(2r) and the transverse kinematic time dilation contributes another RS​/(4r); the absence of line-of-sight motion does not remove this second effect.
As an exact check, the local speed on a timelike circular Schwarzschild geodesic satisfies v2/c2=μ/(r−2μ), where μ=GM/c2=RS​/2. Therefore the face-on frequency shift of a circular Schwarzschild source is
νem​νob​​=1−r3μ​​=1−2r3RS​​​,
(56)
whose expansion agrees with the boxed answer. If the gas is supported or accelerated by pressure or magnetic stresses rather than following a geodesic, its orbital speed is not fixed by the mass and radius alone; the appropriate first-order result is instead νem​≈νob​[1+GM/(rc2)+v2/(2c2)], with v supplied independently.
