inertial mass sets the response to force, F=mi​a. Passive gravitational mass sets the response to a given gravitational potential, Fg​=−mg​∇Φ; active gravitational mass describes a body's gravitational sourcing. Universality of the passive-to-inertial mass ratio makes freely falling trajectories independent of composition. torsion balance comparisons and differential free-fall experiments test this weak equivalence principle by searching for composition-dependent acceleration, with environmental forces and instrumental asymmetries controlled.
The Einstein equivalence principle further says that local nongravitational experiments in a freely falling laboratory obey special relativity, independent of the laboratory's location and velocity. A local inertial coordinate system can remove Christoffel symbols at one event, but cannot remove a nonzero Riemann tensor throughout a finite region. Relative acceleration of separated freely falling particles reveals tidal gravity. Thus a uniformly accelerating laboratory in flat spacetime can mimic a local gravitational field, while extended tidal measurements distinguish genuine curvature. Equality of the measured mass responses does not make every gravitational field globally equivalent to acceleration.

The strongest pre-2014 evidence combines large gravitating mass with extreme compactness. In an X-ray binary, companion-star radial velocities determine a binary mass function; orbital inclination, donor properties and distance complete the mass inference. An unseen component above conventional neutron-star masses is a black-hole candidate. For example, a 2011 Cygnus X-1 analysis combined optical photometry, spectroscopy and distance information to infer a compact-object mass about 14.8M⊙​, with model and observational uncertainties.[ref]
Near Sagittarius A*, infrared stellar astrometry and radial velocities trace nearly Keplerian orbits around a dark compact mass. The concentration of several million solar masses in a small volume strongly challenges an extended cluster of ordinary remnants; source confusion, reference-frame errors, distance and possible extended mass must be fitted.[ref] Gas and maser dynamics provide related compact-mass tests in other nuclei.
accretion explains luminous X-rays, rapid variability and broad relativistic disk features. These constrain size and the strong-field environment, but emission alone is not unique to an event horizon. Lack of surface-like signatures is supporting, model-dependent evidence. Mass, compactness and consistent accretion phenomenology together support the black-hole interpretation; large-radius mass measurements alone do not directly demonstrate a horizon.
