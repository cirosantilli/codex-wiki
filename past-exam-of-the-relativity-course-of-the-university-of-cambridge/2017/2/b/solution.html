Nearby freely falling particles can accelerate relative to one another even though each follows a geodesic. A local inertial frame removes the common gravitational acceleration at one event; it cannot remove the variation across an extended laboratory. That variation is a tidal force, measured covariantly by geodesic deviation.
Let uμ be the reference four-velocity and ξμ an infinitesimal separation to a neighboring geodesic, with matching affine parameters. With the convention [∇β​,∇γ​]Vμ=Rμνβγ​Vν,

This relates measured relative acceleration to the Riemann curvature tensor. Reversing the curvature-tensor convention reverses the displayed curvature sign, without changing the physical relative motion.
In a small freely falling frame outside a spherical mass, the weak-field tidal tensor has radial and transverse components proportional to 2,−1,−1. Thus
ξ¨​r​=r32GM​ξr​,ξ¨​⊥​=−r3GM​ξ⊥​.​

An initially spherical cloud stretches radially and compresses in both transverse directions. Two separated test masses, with their changing separation measured by light, form the basic idea of a gravity gradiometer; the common falling motion drops out. A uniform apparent acceleration in an accelerated laboratory can occur even in flat Minkowski spacetime, so it is the separation-dependent tidal response that diagnoses curvature.
