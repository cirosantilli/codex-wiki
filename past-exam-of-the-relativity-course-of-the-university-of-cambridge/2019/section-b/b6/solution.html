A fundamental observer follows the mean cosmic matter flow and is at rest with respect to the homogeneous cosmological frame. Cosmological isotropy permits no preferred spatial acceleration, rotation vector or anisotropic expansion for this flow: the congruence is geodesic, irrotational and shear-free, with isotropic expansion. Its orthogonal homogeneous spatial slices define simultaneity. Choosing fixed spatial labels along these worldlines and their common proper time as t gives synchronous coordinates, gtt​=c2 and gti​=0.
In the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric, t is this cosmic proper time and χ is radial comoving coordinate. On a constant-t radial slice, the proper distance in cosmology from 0 to a fixed χ0​ is D(t)=a(t)χ0​. Therefore
D˙=H(t)D,H(t)=a(t)a˙(t)​.​
(17)
This is the Hubble parameter. The metric describes expansion when a˙>0; its form alone also permits static or contracting histories, so expansion additionally requires that sign of the scale factor derivative.
For a radial photon, the metric components relevant to its motion are gtt​=c2 and gχχ​=−a2(t), both independent of χ. The angular momentum components vanish. The covariant-momentum form of the geodesic equation therefore gives
dλdpχ​​=21​∂χ​gνρ​pνpρ=0.​
(18)
Only this radial contraction is needed: angular metric coefficients can depend on χ, but multiply zero angular components. The conserved quantity is the lower-index p1​=pχ​, rather than the contravariant component p1.
For outward travel put P=−pχ​>0. The null condition c2(pt)2−a2(pχ)2=0 gives
pχ=a2P​,pt=acP​,p0​=pt​=c2pt=a(t)cP​.​
(19)
Here the time coordinate is t, rather than ct. A comoving observer has ut=1 and measures photon energy E=pμ​uμ=pt​. Thus energy and frequency decrease as a−1, giving the cosmological redshift
1+z=νR​νE​​=a(tE​)a(tR​)​.​
(20)
The reciprocal relation for wavelengths follows from their common local propagation speed c.
Finally center the spatial coordinates on the receiving comoving observer. At the source, the transverse spatial line element is dl=a(tE​)SK​(χ)dθ. For a short transverse rod and a small observed angle Δθ,
l=a(tE​)SK​(χ)Δθ=1+za(tR​)SK​(χ)​Δθ.​
(21)
Thus the angular diameter distance is dA​=a(tE​)SK​(χ). Writing the reception epoch as t0​=tR​ gives the displayed formula in the question. The length is the proper transverse length at emission; t0​ cannot be replaced by an unrelated epoch while retaining that expression.
