Euclid's lemma (p∣ab⟹p∣a or p∣b)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 263
If a prime number p divides a product ab of integers a,b, then p divides a or b (possibly both):
p∣ab⟹p∣a or p∣b.
(249)
For example, 5∣6⋅10 and 5∣10. The hypothesis that p is a prime number matters: 6∣2⋅3, although six divides neither factor. When p does not divide a, the greatest common divisor of p and a is one; multiply Bézout's identity up+va=1 by b to see that p∣b. Repeated application makes this the uniqueness step in the fundamental theorem of arithmetic. [ref].
For a completely computed example, 7∣20⋅21 because 20⋅21=420=7⋅60, while 20=7⋅2+6 shows that seven does not divide twenty. Bézout's identity 3⋅7−20=1 becomes, after multiplication by 21,
21=3⋅7⋅21−20⋅21=7⋅63−7⋅60=7⋅3.
(250)
The two terms on the right are divisible by seven, which explicitly recovers the forced divisibility of the second factor. Both alternatives can hold: 7∣14⋅21, and seven divides both 14 and 21.
In fact the property characterizes prime numbers among the integers greater than one. If c is a composite number, choose c=ab with 1<a,b<c; then c∣ab, but c divides neither a nor b. For several factors, repeated application of Euclid's lemma says that a prime number dividing their product divides at least one factor. In a product of prime numbers, that factor must equal it, because a prime number has no other prime factor. This is what prevents a different collection of prime factors from representing the same positive integer in the fundamental theorem of arithmetic.
Book VII, Proposition 30 of Euclid's Elements states the result in terms of a prime number measuring a product; its proof uses coprimality and ratios. The calculation using Bézout's identity above is a modern way to explain the same divisibility statement. [ref]
