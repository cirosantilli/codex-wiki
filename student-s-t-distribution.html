Student's t-distribution (tν​,ν>0)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability distribution Continuous probability distribution
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 6
The Student's t-distribution describes a standard normal random variable divided by an independent estimate of its scale. For any real ν>0, let Z∼N(0,1) have the standard normal distribution and let V∼χν2​ have the chi-squared distribution, with Z and V independent. Then
T=V/ν​Z​∼tν​.
(42)
The parameter ν is called the number of statistical degrees of freedom. It need not be an integer: the chi-squared distribution is a gamma distribution with shape ν/2 and rate 1/2. The independence assumption is essential; specifying only the two marginal distributions does not determine the probability distribution of their ratio.
Its probability density function on the whole real line is
fν​(t)=νπ​Γ(ν/2)Γ((ν+1)/2)​(1+νt2​)−(ν+1)/2,−∞<t<∞,
(43)
where Γ is the gamma function. Every member is a symmetric distribution about zero. At ν=1 it is the Standard Cauchy distribution; as ν→∞ it approaches the standard normal distribution. The essential shape change is the tail: at any fixed finite ν, fν​(t) decays like a constant times ∣t∣−(ν+1), much more slowly than the standard normal density's exponential decay. Thus it is a heavy-tailed distribution. [ref]
The left panel shows the centers; the right panel uses a logarithmic scale to reveal the tails that a linear plot hides. All four curves have location zero and scale one. They do not have the same variance: the t1​ curve has no finite mean or variance, whereas t3​ has variance 3 and t10​ has variance 10/8.
More generally, the absolute moment E∣T∣p for p>0 is finite exactly when p<ν, as follows by comparing the tail integrand with tp−ν−1. Consequently
E[T]=0(ν>1),Var(T)=ν−2ν​(ν>2).
(44)
For 0<ν≤1 there is no finite mean; symmetry alone cannot supply one. For 1<ν≤2 the mean exists but the variance is infinite. For 0<ν≤1 even the centered definition of variance is unavailable because the mean does not exist.
A shifted and scaled version Y=m+bT, b>0, has probability density function b−1fν​((y−m)/b). The scale b is not its standard deviation: for ν>2,
SD(Y)=bν−2ν​​.
(45)
For example, ν=3 and scale b=2 give standard deviation 23​, rather than 2. The distinction matters when using a Student's t-distribution to model data with occasional extreme values, as well as when interpreting a Student t confidence interval.
