Hall's theorem states that a finite bipartite graph with classes L,R has a matching covering L if and only if ∣N(U)∣≥∣U∣ for every U⊆L. Here N(U) is the set of vertices of R adjacent to at least one member of U. Necessity follows because matched partners of U are distinct members of N(U).
For sufficiency induct on ∣L∣; zero or one vertex is immediate. If a nonempty proper U⊂L has ∣N(U)∣=∣U∣, Hall's condition holds within the graph on U,N(U). It also holds on the remaining classes: for T⊆L∖U,
∣N(T)∖N(U)∣=∣N(T∪U)∣−∣N(U)∣≥∣T∣.
(1)
Induction gives matchings in these two disjoint subgraphs, and their union covers L. Otherwise every nonempty proper U has ∣N(U)∣≥∣U∣+1. Choose any edge lr and delete its two endpoints. Every nonempty subset of the remaining left class was a proper subset of L, so deleting r leaves at least as many neighbours as vertices. Induction gives a matching there, and adjoining lr completes the matching. This proves sufficiency in all cases.

In the vertex form of Menger's theorem, for distinct nonadjacent vertices s,t of a finite graph, the maximum number of pairwise internally vertex-disjoint s–t paths equals the minimum size of an s–t vertex separator. A separator is a subset of vertices other than s,t whose deletion leaves no s–t path; internally disjoint paths may share their endpoints but no other vertices. Equivalently, between disjoint vertex sets the maximum number of mutually vertex-disjoint connecting paths equals the minimum size of a separating vertex set, where endpoints may be deleted in that set version.

Add s adjacent to every vertex of V1​ and t adjacent to every vertex of V2​. Suppose an s–t separator C has fewer than n vertices. It cannot contain all of either class. Choose x∈V1​∖C and y∈V2​∖C. No edge can join the remaining two classes, since it would give a three-edge s–t path. Hence every neighbour of x lies in C∩V2​, and every neighbour of y lies in C∩V1​. The minimum-degree bound implies
∣C∣≥∣C∩V1​∣+∣C∩V2​∣≥n/2+n/2=n,
(2)
a contradiction. Menger's theorem therefore gives n internally vertex-disjoint s–t paths. Each uses at least one vertex of each original class, and only n vertices are available in each class. Every path must consequently use exactly one of each. Their middle edges form a perfect matching.

The Turán graph Tr​(n) is the complete r-partite graph whose nonempty part sizes differ by at most one. Write n=qr+s, 0≤s<r: it has s parts of size q+1 and r−s parts of size q.
Turán's theorem states that every simple n-vertex graph with no Kr+1​ has at most e(Tr​(n)) edges, with equality precisely for a graph isomorphic to Tr​(n). Its edge number is half of n2 minus the sum of the squared part sizes.

For a positive integer x, pG​(x) counts proper vertex colorings using a labelled palette of x colors. Choose an edge e=uv. Proper colorings of G−e divide into those in which u,v have different colors, counted by pG​(x), and those in which they have the same color, counted by pG/e​(x). Thus
pG​(x)=pG−e​(x)−pG/e​(x),
(3)
where parallel edges after contraction are suppressed. Induction, using pG​(x)=x∣V(G)∣ for an edgeless graph, produces a polynomial agreeing with every integer coloring count. The polynomial is unique because two polynomials agreeing at infinitely many integers are identical.

The Ramsey number R(s,t) is the least N such that every N-vertex graph contains a clique of size s or an independent set of size t. Take N=R(s−1,t)+R(s,t−1) and choose a vertex v. Among its N−1 other vertices, either it has at least R(s−1,t) neighbours or it has at least R(s,t−1) nonneighbours.
In the first case those neighbours contain an independent t-set or an (s−1)-clique, which joins v to form an s-clique. In the second case the nonneighbours contain an s-clique or an independent (t−1)-set, which joins v to form an independent t-set. This proves
R(s,t)≤R(s−1,t)+R(s,t−1)​.
(7)

The five-cycle and its complement both have no triangle, so R(3,3)>5. In a six-vertex graph, any vertex has at least three neighbours or three nonneighbours. In the first triple an edge completes a triangle with the chosen vertex, while no edges give an independent triple. The complementary argument handles three nonneighbours. Hence R(3,3)=6.
Now suppose a nine-vertex graph contains no triangle and no independent four-set. Each vertex has at most three neighbours, since its neighbourhood is independent. It cannot have at most two neighbours: its at least six nonneighbours contain, by the result just proved, either a triangle or an independent triple; the former is forbidden and the latter joins the vertex to give an independent four-set. Every degree must therefore equal three. But nine vertices of degree three have odd total degree, contradicting the handshake lemma. Thus R(3,4)≤9.

The graph Ramsey number R(G,H) is the least N such that every red-blue coloring of the edges of KN​ contains a red copy of G or a blue copy of H. Copies are ordinary subgraphs, not necessarily induced. Equivalently every graph on N vertices contains G or its complement contains H.

Assume the usual nontrivial case χ(G)≥2 and ∣H∣≥2. On (χ(G)−1)(∣H∣−1) vertices partition into χ(G)−1 equal classes of size ∣H∣−1. Color all edges between distinct classes red and all edges within a class blue. The red graph is (χ(G)−1)-colorable, so it contains no copy of G. Every blue connected component has only ∣H∣−1 vertices, so connectedness prevents a blue copy of H. Therefore one more vertex is necessary:

(8)

∣d(X′,Y′)−d(X,Y)∣<ϵ.
(9)
