Hall's theorem states that a finite bipartite graph with classes L,R has a matching covering L exactly when ∣N(S)∣≥∣S∣ for every S⊆L. Necessity follows because the distinct matched partners of S belong to N(S).
For sufficiency take a maximum matching. If it leaves x∈L unmatched, explore alternating paths starting at x, traversing unmatched edges from left to right and matched edges back to the left. No reached right vertex can be unmatched, since flipping the edges of such an alternating path would augment the matching. Let S,T be the reached left and right sets. Every neighbour of S lies in T: unmatched edges are explored, and a matched edge from a reached left vertex leads to its already reached predecessor. Conversely every member of T is a neighbour of S. Their matched partners are exactly S∖{x}, so ∣N(S)∣=∣T∣=∣S∣−1, contradicting the hypothesis. Thus a matching covering L exists.

If no complete matching exists, Hall's theorem gives a nonempty S⊆V1​, of size k, with ∣N(S)∣≤k−1. The pairs between S and V2​∖N(S) must be nonedges, so there are at least
k(n−k+1)=n+(k−1)(n−k)≥n
(1)
missing edges. Hence e(G)≤n2−n, contradicting the strict edge bound. Therefore a complete matching exists.
The strictness is necessary: isolate one vertex of V1​ and put all edges between the remaining n−1 left vertices and all n right vertices. There are exactly n(n−1) edges and no matching can cover the isolated vertex. This also handles n=1.

First the graph is connected: every component has at least δ(G)+1>n/2 vertices, so two components cannot fit. Take a longest path v1​,…,vk​. Every neighbour of either endpoint lies on that path. In the index set {1,…,k−1} define
I={i:v1​vi+1​∈E},J={i:vi​vk​∈E}.
(2)
Their sizes sum to d(v1​)+d(vk​)≥n>k−1, so they share an index i. The edges at that index give the cycle
v1​,v2​,…,vi​,vk​,vk−1​,…,vi+1​,v1​,
(3)
containing every vertex of the path. The minimum-degree assumption and n≥3 ensure the resulting cycle is nondegenerate. If it omitted any graph vertex, connectedness would supply an edge from outside it to the cycle. Starting with that outside vertex and then following the cycle without closing it gives a path with k+1 vertices, contradicting maximality. Thus the cycle is Hamiltonian. This proves Dirac's theorem in the required case.

For a positive integer x, the chromatic polynomial pG​(x) counts proper vertex colorings from a palette of x labelled colors, where adjacent vertices must have different colors. The counting function agrees with a polynomial, as proved by deletion-contraction in the next part.

For an edge e=uv, colorings of G−e divide into those giving u,v different colors, counted by pG​, and those giving them the same color, counted by pG/e​. Parallel edges produced by contraction may be suppressed. Thus
pG​(x)=pG−e​(x)−pG/e​(x).
(4)
For an edgeless graph on n≥1 vertices, pG​=xn. Induct on the number of edges, allowing smaller vertex counts for contractions. If both smaller expansions have alternating nonnegative coefficients, the contraction's leading degree is one lower, so subtracting it gives
ai​(G)=ai​(G−e)+ai−1​(G/e),
(5)
with absent coefficients interpreted as zero. Each coefficient remains nonnegative. Both terms have zero constant coefficient for nonempty graphs, so the resulting expansion is
pG​(x)=i=0∑n−1​(−1)iai​xn−i,ai​≥0​.
(6)
This induction simultaneously proves the counting function is a polynomial.

The Ramsey number R(k,l) is the least N such that every graph on N vertices has a k-clique or an independent set of size l. Choose a vertex in a graph of order R(k−1,l)+R(k,l−1). It has either at least R(k−1,l) neighbours or at least R(k,l−1) nonneighbours. In the first set, a (k−1)-clique extends with the vertex to a k-clique, while an independent l-set already suffices. The complementary argument handles the second set. Thus
R(k,l)≤R(k−1,l)+R(k,l−1).
(9)
The boundary values are R(k,2)=k and R(2,l)=l. Induction on k+l, using Pascal's identity, therefore gives
R(k,l)≤(k−2k+l−3​)+(k−1k+l−3​)=(k−1k+l−2​)​.
(10)

The graph Ramsey number R(G,H) is the smallest N such that every red-blue coloring of the edges of KN​ has a red copy of G or a blue copy of H. These copies are subgraphs, not necessarily induced. Equivalently every graph on N vertices contains G or its complement contains H.

For s,t≥2, split (s−1)(t−1) vertices into s−1 classes of size t−1, coloring edges between classes red and edges inside classes blue. The red graph has no Ks​, and every blue component is too small for the connected tree. This proves the lower bound.
For the upper bound let N=(s−1)(t−1)+1 and suppose the blue graph contains no copy of the tree. By the permitted chromatic-number statement, its chromatic number is at most t−1. A graph of larger chromatic number would have a subgraph of chromatic number exactly t, obtained by deleting vertices until that number is reached. A proper blue coloring with at most t−1 classes puts no blue edges within any class, so each class is a red clique. If there is no red Ks​, each class has at most s−1 vertices, contradicting N>(s−1)(t−1). Therefore
R(Ks​,T)=(s−1)(t−1)+1​.
(11)
The one-vertex boundary cases give one directly.

d OurBigBook.com
A vertex with at least R(t,t) neighbours has either a t-clique among them, producing Kt+1​ with the vertex, or an independent t-set among them, producing an induced star with that centre.
For the second assertion put D=R(t,t)−1. If either of those two outcomes is absent, the preceding result forces maximum degree at most D. In a connected graph of this maximum degree, a ball of radius t−1 has at most 1+D+⋯+Dt−1 vertices, using the rough bound on walks at each distance. If the graph has more vertices, some vertex lies at distance at least t from a chosen root. A shortest path to it is induced, since a chord would shorten it; its first t edges give an induced path of the required length. Thus a sufficiently large connected graph has one of the three listed configurations.

The extremal number ex(n;H) is the maximum number of edges in a simple graph on n vertices containing no subgraph isomorphic to H. For a fixed graph with χ(H)=r≥2, the Erdős-Stone theorem gives
ex(n;H)=(1−r−11​+o(1))2n2​​.
(12)
Equivalently, the ratio to (2n​) tends to 1−1/(r−1). The bipartite case has limiting density zero.

For nonempty disjoint vertex sets define d(X,Y)=e(X,Y)/(∣X∣∣Y∣). The pair is ϵ-uniform, also called regular, if whenever X′⊆X and Y′⊆Y have sizes at least ϵ∣X∣ and ϵ∣Y∣,
∣d(X′,Y′)−d(X,Y)∣<ϵ.
(14)
Thus reasonably large relative subsets retain almost the same edge density.

Let S be the vertices of X with fewer than d∣Y∣/2 neighbours in Y. If ∣S∣>ϵn, it is a permissible subset in the regularity test, and averaging those degrees gives d(S,Y)<d/2. But d(X,Y)≥d, so

For events Ai​ with a dependency graph, assume each event is independent of the sigma-algebra generated by its nonneighbours. If numbers xi​∈[0,1) satisfy P(Ai​)≤xi​∏j∈Γ(i)​(1−xj​), the general Lovász local lemma gives
P(⋂i​Aic​)≥∏i​(1−xi​)>0.
(17)
The symmetric version says that if every event has probability at most q, the dependency graph has maximum degree at most d, and eq(d+1)≤1, then none occurs with positive probability. For d≥1, set xi​=1/(d+1). Since (1−1/(d+1))d≥e−1, the general condition follows from the symmetric numerical hypothesis. The logarithmic inequality log(1+1/d)≤1/d proves that estimate. When d=0, the events are mutually independent and each has probability below one, so the conclusion is immediate. This is the intended implication from the general to the symmetric form; the source repeats the word symmetric in its final clause.

b OurBigBook.com
Assign each vertex a color independently and uniformly from the five available residues. Let Av​ be the event that none of its neighbours has the successor color. Conditional on the color of v, its r neighbours all avoid that color with probability (4/5)r, so P(Av​)=(4/5)r.
The event only uses colors in the closed neighbourhood of v. Events based on disjoint such sets are jointly independent. A closed neighbourhood can intersect another only when the two centres are at distance at most two, giving dependency degree at most r+r(r−1)=r2. For sufficiently large r,
e(r2+1)(4/5)r≤1.
(18)
The symmetric local lemma therefore supplies a color assignment with no bad event, proving every vertex has a neighbour of the successor color. As defined in the question, this is a five-color assignment; it is not required to be a proper vertex coloring of every edge, which would be impossible for some large regular graphs.
The construction generalizes to cyclic successor colorings of regular graphs.

Solution

c OurBigBook.com
From every vertex choose one neighbour of the successor color and follow these choices. The finite directed graph with one chosen outgoing edge per vertex contains a directed cycle. Its vertices are distinct until the first repeated vertex. Each step increases the color by one modulo five, so returning to the starting vertex requires the cycle length to be divisible by five. It therefore has length at least five and is also a cycle in the underlying graph. This proves a cycle of length a positive multiple of five exists.

Let X count the four-vertex sets spanning a clique. Its expectation is μ=(4n​)p6. If p≪n−2/3, then μ→0 and Markov's inequality gives P(X>0)→0.
Now suppose p≫n−2/3. Then μ→∞. Two distinct clique indicators are independent unless their vertex sets share at least two vertices. An overlap of two vertices gives O(n6) ordered pairs requiring eleven distinct edges; an overlap of three gives O(n5) pairs requiring nine edges. Consequently
D=O(n6p11+n5p9),μ2D​=O(n−2p−1+n−3p−3)⟶0.
(22)
The preceding second-moment bound therefore gives P(X=0)→0. Thus
p∗​(n)=n−2/3​
(23)
is a threshold for the appearance of a four-clique. Sharing just one vertex creates no dependence because the two edge sets are disjoint.

d OurBigBook.com
Realize the random graph process by giving every edge an independent uniform arrival time. For a fixed four-set S, only its six internal edges and its 4(n−4) outgoing edges affect whether it is an isolated clique. It is an isolated clique at some time exactly when all six internal edges arrive before every outgoing edge.
The relative ordering of these 4n−10 edges is uniform. The chance that the first six are exactly the six internal edges is (64n−10​)−1. A union bound over the possible vertex sets gives
P(an isolated four-clique ever appears)≤(64n−10​)(4n​)​=O(n−2)⟶0​.
(24)
Edges entirely outside S are irrelevant. Counting the whole arrival-order event avoids an invalid union over continuously many times.
This is an example of isolated clique appearance in a uniform random graph process.

a OurBigBook.com
Explore the component of one vertex by processing discovered vertices in order. At each step, the number of newly discovered neighbours is a binomial variable with at most n trials and success probability 1/(2n), on previously unexposed independent edges. Pad each step with independent dummy trials. This couples the exploration below independent offspring variables ξi​∼Bin(n,1/(2n)), each of mean one half.
If the component has at least k≥2 vertices, after k−1 vertices have been processed at least k−1 offspring must have been found. Hence
P(∣C(v)∣≥k)≤P(∑i=1k−1​ξi​≥k−1).
(25)
The sum is binomial with mean μ=(k−1)/2. The multiplicative Chernoff inequality P(B≥2μ)≤(e/4)μ therefore gives
P(∣C(v)∣≥k)≤e−c(k−1),c=21​(loge​4−1)>0.
(26)
A union bound over all vertices bounds the probability of any component of size at least k by ne−c(k−1). Taking k=⌈αloge​n⌉ with any α>1/c, for example ten, makes this tend to zero. Thus the largest component is at most a constant times logn with high probability.

Every vertex in the component can be reached from v by a simple path. The number of potential length-ℓ simple paths beginning at v is at most nℓ, and each has probability (1/(2n))ℓ of being present. Counting such paths may count a vertex more than once, so it gives an upper bound on the expected number of reached vertices:
E∣C(v)∣≤∑ℓ=0n−1​nℓ(1/(2n))ℓ≤∑ℓ≥0​2−ℓ=2.
(27)
Thus K=2​ works for every n. Equivalently the dominating branching process has mean offspring one half and expected total progeny two.

Azuma's inequality states that if (Mi​)i=0m​ is a martingale with ∣Mi​−Mi−1​∣≤1 almost surely, then for t≥0,
P(∣Mm​−M0​∣≥t)≤2exp(−t2/(2m)).
(28)
For independent product coordinates Z1​,…,Zm​, expose them successively and set Mi​=E[f(Z)∣Z1​,…,Zi​]. If changing one coordinate changes f by at most one, the possible conditional means at that exposure have diameter at most one, by averaging the same remaining independent coordinates. Therefore the martingale increments have absolute value at most one. It follows that
P(∣f(Z)−Ef(Z)∣≥t)≤2exp(−t2/(2m))​.
(29)
For coordinate bounds ci​, the same argument gives denominator 2∑i​ci2​. The argument extends to arbitrary independent measurable coordinates for an integrable function with these bounds, rather than requiring finite state sets.

The union area is a bounded measurable function of the centres, with 0≤X≤nπ. Fix all centres except one, and let B be the union of the fixed disks. Replacing the remaining disk D changes the area to ∣B∣+∣D∖B∣. The second term always lies in [0,π], so the area changes by at most π, not merely by the weaker bound 2π.
Thus X/π has coordinate differences at most one for the n independent centres. The preceding martingale bound gives
P(∣X−EX∣≥t)≤2exp(−2π2nt2​)​.
(30)
One may take c=1/(2π2), independently of all centre distributions. Boundedness of the area ensures the conditional expectations exist even when the centre locations have no finite moments.

For i=1,…,n−1, make one block from the edge weights {Xij​:j>i}. These n−1 blocks are independent because they contain disjoint edges. Changing one whole block only changes weights of edges incident with vertex i. Every matching contains at most one such edge, so its total weight changes by at most one. Comparing an old optimal matching in the new weights and a new optimal matching in the old weights proves that the maximum matching weight also changes by at most one.
Apply the bounded-differences martingale argument to these n−1 blocks, giving
P(∣W−EW∣≥t)≤2exp(−2(n−1)t2​)​.
(31)
Exposing each edge separately would give a weaker quadratic-in-n denominator. The grouped exposure is what supplies the requested linear denominator. The proof needs only independent weights in [0,1], not identical uniform laws. For n=1, the matching weight is deterministically zero; the divided formula is intended for n≥2.
The general grouping argument is vertex-block concentration of maximum matching weight.
