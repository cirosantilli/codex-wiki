Hall's marriage theorem says that a finite bipartite graph with parts L,R has a matching covering L if and only if ∣N(S)∣≥∣S∣ for every S⊆L. Necessity follows because matching edges send the vertices of S to distinct neighbours.
For sufficiency, choose a maximum matching M. If it misses a∈L, explore all alternating paths from a, taking unmatched edges from left to right and matched edges from right to left. Let S⊆L and T⊆R be the reached vertices. No vertex of T can be unmatched, since flipping the edges along the resulting augmenting path would enlarge M.
Every neighbour of a reached left vertex is reached on the right: unmatched edges are exploration steps, and a matched edge leads back to an already reached matching partner. Thus T=N(S). Each right vertex in T is matched to a reached left vertex other than a, and every reached left vertex other than a arose this way. These matching partners give a bijection T↔S∖{a}. Hence ∣N(S)∣=∣S∣−1, contradicting the assumed inequality. Therefore the maximum matching covers L.

For a finite simple loopless graph, pG​(q) counts proper vertex colorings with q labelled available colors, initially for nonnegative integer q. For an edge e=uv, proper colorings of G−e split into those with different endpoint colors, counted by pG​(q), and those with equal endpoint colors, counted by the contracted graph. Hence the deletion-contraction recurrence for the chromatic polynomial is
pG​(q)=pG−e​(q)−pG/e​(q).
(1)
Parallel edges created by contraction can be suppressed since they impose the same coloring constraint. A graph with no edges has pG​(q)=q∣V(G)∣. Induction by this recurrence therefore gives a polynomial agreeing with the counting function on all nonnegative integers. That polynomial is unique because two distinct polynomials cannot agree at infinitely many points.

Induct using the same deletion-contraction recurrence for the chromatic polynomial. The edgeless graph has polynomial xn, so its leading coefficient is one and the others are zero. All nonempty-vertex chromatic polynomials have zero constant term, since no coloring uses zero colors.
For an existing edge, write the alternating coefficient lists of the deletion and simplified contraction as ai′​ and ai′′​. The contraction has n−1 vertices, so subtracting its polynomial changes its leading sign by one place. Thus
ai​(G)=ai′​+ai−1′′​,
(2)
with nonexistent indices understood as zero. Both summands are nonnegative by induction. The leading coefficient is a0​=1, and the next is
a1​=(m−1)+1=m.
(3)
This proves the alternating expansion and nonnegativity of every ai​. The coefficient assertion for a1​ is relevant when n≥2; the one-vertex graph simply has polynomial x.

For the complete graph, pKn​​(x)=x(x−1)⋯(x−n+1)>0 whenever x>n−1.
Use induction on the number of vertices, and for fixed n on the number of missing edges. If G is not complete, choose a missing edge e and put H=G+e. The recurrence rearranges as
pG​(x)=pH​(x)+pH/e​(x).
(6)
The first term is positive by the missing-edge induction. The second graph has n−1 vertices, so the vertex induction makes its polynomial positive for x>n−2, in particular for the present range. The one-vertex base case is x>0. Thus pG​(x)>0 for all x>n−1, including noninteger x; counting colorings alone would not establish that real-variable claim.

For disjoint nonempty vertex sets, their edge density is d(X,Y)=e(X,Y)/(∣X∣∣Y∣). The pair is epsilon-regular or epsilon-uniform if for all X′⊆X, Y′⊆Y with ∣X′∣≥ϵ∣X∣ and ∣Y′∣≥ϵ∣Y∣,
∣d(X′,Y′)−d(X,Y)∣<ϵ.
(7)
It controls density on every sufficiently large subpair, rather than merely on average over chosen subpairs.

An epsilon-uniform partition has disjoint classes V0​,V1​,…,Vk​ covering the vertex set, with ∣V0​∣≤ϵ∣V(G)∣, equal nonexceptional class sizes ∣V1​∣=⋯=∣Vk​∣, and at most ϵk2 unordered pairs of distinct nonexceptional classes failing epsilon-uniformity. The exceptional set need not have the common size.
Szemerédi regularity lemma states that for every ϵ>0 and positive integer m0​, there is M=M(ϵ,m0​) such that every sufficiently large finite graph, for example every graph of order at least M after enlarging M if needed, has such a partition with m0​≤k≤M. The bound depends only on the accuracy and minimum class count, not on the graph's order.

Write Xk​=∑i​Ii​. For two disjoint blocks, their relative orders are independent and each is increasing with probability 1/k!, giving joint probability 1/(k!)2. If their cyclic starting positions differ by s, with 1≤s<k, and k≤n/2, they overlap in just one interval. Both blocks increasing is then equivalent to their entire length-k+s union increasing, so the joint probability is 1/(k+s)!.
For each s there are 2n ordered overlapping pairs, while there are n(n−2k+1) ordered disjoint pairs and n diagonal pairs. Thus for k≥2,
EXk2​=k!n​+2n∑s=1k−1​(k+s)!1​+(k!)2n(n−2k+1)​.

Let Ii​=1Ai​​ and X=∑i​Ii​. Define
μ=∑i​P(Ai​),Δ=∑i=jEi​∩Ej​=∅​​P(Ai​∩Aj​).
(21)
The dependency sum is over ordered pairs, so each unordered overlapping pair is counted twice. Disjoint prescribed edge sets give independence.
Order the events. For each i, let Bi​ be the event that all earlier events fail. Split it as Bi​=Ci​∩Di​, where Ci​ excludes earlier events with edge sets disjoint from Ei​, and Di​ excludes the overlapping earlier events. These are down-sets. The event Ai​ is a principal up-set, and it is independent of Ci​ since their determining edge sets are disjoint.
If some Bi​ has probability zero the desired zero-event bound is immediate. Otherwise the allowed conditional correlation inequality gives
P(Ci​∩Di​∣Ai​)≥P(Ci​∣Ai​)P(Di​∣Ai​)=P(Ci​)P(Di​∣Ai​).
(22)
Since P(Bi​)≤P(Ci​), it follows that
qi​:=P(Ai​∣Bi​)≥P(Ai​)P(Di​∣Ai​)≥P(Ai​)−∑j<iEj​∩Ei​=∅​​P(Ai​∩Aj​).
(23)
The last step is the union bound under conditioning on Ai​. Zero-probability events may simply be omitted, and a certain event makes the target probability zero. Finally the sequential product and 1−u≤e−u give
P(X=0)=∏i​(1−qi​)≤exp(−∑i​qi​)≤e−μ+Δ/2​.
(24)
This proves the first Janson inequality with the dependency-sum convention explicit.

Independently retain each event with probability t, using a fresh Bernoulli mark for every index. Its retained occurrence is the original principal up-set together with its mark being one, so the first proof applies in the enlarged independent product space. The new parameters are μt​=tμ and Δt​=t2Δ: distinct retained events have independent marks, and pairs of disjoint original supports remain independent.
If no original event occurs, no retained event occurs. Thus
P(X=0)≤exp(−tμ+t2Δ/2),0≤t≤1.
(25)
When Δ≥μ>0, choose t=μ/Δ to minimize this quadratic exponent. It gives
P(X=0)≤exp(−2Δμ2​)​.
(26)
If μ=0, the appropriate trivial bound is one, rather than an undefined zero-over-zero exponent. The argument is the Janson lower-tail bound by independent thinning.

Count one copy for each four-element vertex set. It requires six independent edges, so
μ=EX=(4n​)2−6​.
(27)
Distinct copies share an edge exactly when their vertex intersection has size two or three. For each first copy, their numbers are 6(2n−4​) and 4(n−4), respectively. The required union then has eleven or nine edges. Hence the ordered Janson dependency sum is
Δ=(4n​)(6(2n−4​)2−11+4(n−4)2−9).
(28)
For large n, μ=Θ(n4) and Δ=Θ(n6), so Δ≥μ and μ2/(2Δ)≥an2 for some constant a>0. The second Janson inequality therefore gives
P(X=0)≤e−an2​.
(29)
This is the asserted asymptotic bound. It can also be made valid for every n≥4 by decreasing a to cover the finitely many smaller values, since each then has probability strictly below one of containing no copy. For n<4 the event has probability one and no positive exponential bound of this form can hold.

A dependency graph of events, written here as a loopless directed graph D on the event indices, must satisfy a joint independence condition: for each i, Ai​ is independent of the sigma-algebra generated by all Aj​ with j=i and no arc i→j. In particular this means independence from every Boolean combination of those non-neighbour events, not merely pairwise independence. The outgoing neighbours list the events for which that independence is not required.

The asymmetric Lovász local lemma states that if there are numbers 0≤xi​<1 with
P(Ai​)≤xi​∏j∈Γ+(i)​(1−xj​)
(30)
for every index, then
P(⋂i​Aic​)≥∏i​(1−xi​)>0.
(31)
For maximum out-degree d≥1, take every xi​=1/(d+1). Since
(1−d+11​)d=(1+d1​)−d≥e−1,
(32)
one has xi​(1−xi​)d≥1/[e(d+1)]≥p. Fewer than d outgoing neighbours only increases the product, so the hypothesis is satisfied whenever ep(d+1)≤1.
If d=0, take xi​=p<1; the neighbour products are empty and equal one. This separately covers the case in which 1/(d+1) would equal the forbidden value one. Thus the symmetric local lemma follows in every case.

Color every edge independently and uniformly with one of k colors. For each unoriented simple four-edge path P, let AP​ be its repetition event. Its four distinct edges must satisfy two prescribed color equalities, so P(AP​)=k−2.
Events with edge-disjoint paths depend on disjoint random variables. A fixed edge lies on at most 4Δ3 unoriented four-edge paths: count its four possible positions and two orientations, bound the remaining choices by Δ3, then divide the oriented path count by two. Thus the union of all paths sharing any of P's four edges has size at most 16Δ3. This union includes P itself, so a dependency graph has d+1≤16Δ3.
For k≥8Δ3/2,
ek−2(d+1)≤64Δ316eΔ3​=4e​<1.
(33)
The symmetric Lovász local lemma yields positive probability of avoiding every event. Therefore such an edge coloring exists. If there are no four-edge paths the conclusion is immediate.

Use both families of bad events in one asymmetric Lovász local lemma application. Give every four-edge event parameter x4​ and every six-edge event parameter x6​. A path of length ℓ shares edges with at most 4ℓΔ3 four-edge paths and 6ℓΔ5 six-edge paths. It therefore suffices to verify
k−2≤x4​(1−x4​)16Δ3(1−x6​)24Δ5,
(35)
k−3≤x6​(1−x4​)24Δ3(1−x6​)36Δ5,0<x4​,x6​<1.
(36)
These slightly excessive exponents are safe even though they may count the event itself. Choose x4​=2/k2 and x6​=2/k3. For k≥CΔ5/3, the sums of exponents times their parameters are bounded by constants times C−2Δ−1/3+C−3, uniformly in Δ≥1. Taking C sufficiently large makes each product of factors at least 1/2, for example using (1−x)r≥1−rx and multiplying the two factors. Both displayed inequalities then hold. Positive probability of no bad event gives simultaneous avoidance of both lengths. Keeping two event parameters avoids replacing the rarer six-edge events by the larger four-edge probability.
