The Ramsey number R(s,t) is the least integer N such that every graph on N vertices contains either a clique of size s or an independent set of size t. Equivalently, every red-blue colouring of the edges of KN​ contains a red Ks​ or a blue Kt​.
Put a=R(s−1,t), b=R(s,t−1) and consider a graph on a+b vertices. Fix a vertex v. If it has at least a neighbours, their induced graph contains either a clique of size s−1, which together with v gives a clique of size s, or an independent set of size t. Otherwise v has at least b nonneighbours. Their induced graph contains either a clique of size s, or an independent set of size t−1, which can be enlarged by v. Thus
R(s,t)≤R(s−1,t)+R(s,t−1).​
(1)
Since R(2,3)=R(3,2)=3, this gives R(3,3)≤6. For the reverse bound, the five-cycle has no triangle and no independent set of size three: its complement is again a five-cycle, so it too is triangle-free. Hence the property fails on five vertices and
R(3,3)=6.​
(2)

The vertex version of Menger's theorem states that for two distinct nonadjacent vertices s,t of a finite graph, the maximum number of internally vertex-disjoint s-t graph paths equals the minimum size of a set S⊆V∖{s,t} whose deletion separates s from t.
max{internally disjoint s-t paths}=min{∣S∣:s,t disconnected in G−S}.​
(3)
Internally disjoint graph paths may share their endpoints but no other vertex. The nonadjacency hypothesis is needed in this separator formulation, since an edge st cannot be destroyed by deleting other vertices.

Let the bipartition be L∪R. Form an auxiliary graph by adding a new vertex s adjacent to every vertex of L and a new vertex t adjacent to every vertex of R. The vertices s,t are nonadjacent, so Menger's theorem applies.
A matching of size m gives m internally disjoint graph paths sℓrt, one for each matching edge ℓr. Conversely, every s-t graph path must use an edge of the original bipartite graph. Choose one such edge from each member of an internally disjoint family. Their endpoints are all distinct, so the chosen edges form a matching. Thus the maximum number of those graph paths is the maximum matching size.
An s-t separator contained in L∪R is exactly a vertex cover of the original graph. Indeed, an uncovered edge ℓr would leave the graph path sℓrt, while removing a vertex cover removes every edge between L and R and hence every s-t graph path. The minimum separator size is therefore the minimum vertex cover size. Menger gives
ν(G)=τ(G)for bipartite G.​
(4)
This is Kőnig's theorem for bipartite matching. The auxiliary construction gives the required deduction from vertex-disjoint paths rather than assuming a matching theorem in advance.

Hall's marriage theorem says that a bipartite graph with parts L,R has a matching saturating L if and only if
∣N(S)∣≥∣S∣for every S⊆L.​
(5)
Necessity follows because the matched neighbours of vertices of S are distinct and belong to N(S).
For sufficiency, assume these inequalities. Let K=U∪W be any vertex cover, with U⊆L and W⊆R. Every neighbour of S=L∖U must belong to W, since otherwise its edge from S is uncovered. Therefore ∣W∣≥∣N(S)∣≥∣S∣, and ∣K∣≥∣U∣+∣S∣=∣L∣. The whole part L is itself a vertex cover of size ∣L∣. Part (ii) now gives a matching of size exactly ∣L∣, so it saturates L.

Fix a proper colouring with exactly k nonempty colour classes. For any class Ci​, suppose every vertex in it misses at least one of the other k−1 colours in its neighbourhood. Recolour each of these vertices by one of its missing colours. No edge joins two vertices of Ci​, so these simultaneous recolourings cannot conflict with one another, and the chosen colours do not conflict with vertices outside Ci​.
This would eliminate colour i and produce a (k−1)-colouring, contradicting optimality. Therefore each colour class contains a vertex adjacent to at least one vertex of every other colour. That vertex has degree at least k−1. Choosing one from each class gives k distinct vertices, proving
​{v:deg(v)≥k−1}​≥k.​
(11)
For k=1, a nonempty graph already has a vertex of degree at least zero, so the conclusion holds as well.

For disjoint nonempty sets X,Y, let e(X,Y) count edges between them. Their edge density is
d(X,Y)=∣X∣∣Y∣e(X,Y)​.​
(12)
The pair is ε-regular if every A⊆X and B⊆Y with ∣A∣≥ε∣X∣, ∣B∣≥ε∣Y∣ satisfies ∣d(A,B)−d(X,Y)∣≤ε.
An ε-regular partition has disjoint classes V0​,V1​,…,Vk​ covering V(G), with ∣V0​∣≤ε∣V(G)∣, equal positive sizes ∣V1​∣=⋯=∣Vk​∣, and at most εk2 unordered pairs (Vi​,Vj​) with 1≤i<j≤k that are not ε-regular. The set V0​ is exceptional and its pairs need not satisfy regularity.
Szemerédi regularity lemma states that for every ε>0 and integer m0​≥1, there are integers M,n0​ such that every graph with n≥n0​ vertices has such a partition with
m0​≤k≤M.​
(13)
The bounds depend on ε,m0​, not on the graph order. Using a strict regularity inequality or a slightly different constant in the allowed pair count gives an equivalent conventional version; the displayed convention will be used below.

Markov's inequality states that for Y≥0 and t>0, P(Y≥t)≤EY/t. It follows by taking expectations in Y≥t1{Y≥t}​.
If X has finite mean μ and variance σ2, apply Markov to (X−μ)2 to obtain Chebyshev's inequality:
P(∣X−μ∣≥t)≤t2σ2​(t>0).​
(21)
For the first moment method, assume μn​=EXn​→0. Nonnegative integer values give {Xn​>0}={Xn​≥1}, so
P(Xn​>0)≤EXn​⟶0.​
(22)
For a standard second moment method, suppose μn​>0 eventually, the second moments are finite, and EXn2​/μn2​→1. Equivalently, Var(Xn​)/μn2​→0. The zero event is contained in {∣Xn​−μn​∣≥μn​}, so
P(Xn​=0)≤μn2​Var(Xn​)​⟶0.
(23)
Hence P(Xn​>0)→1​. The second-moment condition controls the fluctuations relative to the mean; a large mean alone is insufficient.

A dependency digraph has vertices 1,…,n and no loops. For each i, the event Ai​ must be independent of the sigma-algebra generated jointly by all Aj​ with j=i for which there is no edge i→j. Joint independence from that collection is required; pairwise independence alone does not suffice.
Write Γ+(i) for the outneighbours of i, and BS​=⋂j∈S​Ajc​. We prove by induction on ∣S∣ that BS​ has positive probability and, when i∈/S,
P(Ai​∣BS​)≤xi​.
(30)
For S=∅, the asserted conditional bound follows from the hypothesis, since its product factors are at most one. For the induction step, positivity for a set of size r follows by writing its avoidance probability as a chain product: all preceding conditioning sets have size below r, so each factor is at least 1−xj​>0.
Now take ∣S∣=r, split S1​=S∩Γ+(i) and S2​=S∖Γ+(i), and condition first on BS2​​. Independence gives P(Ai​∣BS2​​)=P(Ai​). Moreover, order the elements of S1​ and use the inductive conditional bounds on the successive avoidance factors. Their conditioning sets have size at most r−1, so
P(BS1​​∣BS2​​)≥∏j∈S1​​(1−xj​).
(31)
This remains true when S1​ is empty, with both sides one. Therefore
P(Ai​∣BS​)​=P(BS1​​∣BS2​​)P(Ai​∩BS1​​∣BS2​​)​≤∏j∈S1​​(1−xj​)P(Ai​)​≤xi​j∈Γ+(i)∖S1​∏​(1−xj​)≤xi​.​
(32)
The induction is complete. Finally expand the probability of avoiding all events in the order 1,…,n:
P(i=1⋂n​Aic​)=i=1∏n​(1−P(Ai​​j<i⋂​Ajc​))≥i=1∏n​(1−xi​)>0.​
(33)
This proves the asymmetric Lovász local lemma, including the positivity needed to make every conditioning step legitimate.

The symmetric Lovász local lemma states: if each bad event has probability at most p, each event is independent of the collection of all but at most d other events, and
ep(d+1)≤1,​
(34)
then the probability that none of the bad events occurs is positive. Independence is from the sigma-algebra generated by the indicated nonneighbours, as in part (a). A sufficient sharper parameter condition is p≤x(1−x)d for some x∈(0,1); taking x=1/(d+1) gives the displayed standard bound when d≥1. When d=0, mutual independence directly gives the conclusion under the displayed bound.

A Galton-Watson process starts with one ancestor, X0​=1. Given generation size Xr​, define
Xr+1​=∑j=1Xr​​Zr,j​,
(38)
where all offspring variables are independent with common distribution Z. The empty sum is zero, so zero is absorbing. Its extinction probability is
η=P(∃r:Xr​=0)=limr→∞​P(Xr​=0).
(39)
For the probability generating function f(s)=E[sZ], the allowed fixed-point characterization is
η is the smallest solution in [0,1] of f(s)=s.​
(40)
For binomial offspring, f(s)=(1−p+ps)k, with f(1)=1, f′(1)=kp, and f convex on [0,1].
First assume 0<p<1. If k=0, extinction occurs immediately. For k=1, f(s)−s=(1−p)(1−s)>0 for s<1, so the only fixed point is one. For k≥2, strict convexity gives
f(s)>f(1)+f′(1)(s−1)=1+kp(s−1)(0≤s<1).
(41)
If kp≤1, the right-hand side is at least s, so no fixed point below one exists and η=1. If kp>1, f(s)−s<0 for s just below one, whereas f(0)>0. Continuity supplies a fixed point in (0,1); hence η<1. Thus in the nondegenerate parameter range,
0<p<1:η<1⟺kp>1.​
(42)
For completeness, if binomial endpoint parameters are allowed, p=0 or k=0 gives η=1, while p=1 and k≥1 gives deterministic positive offspring and η=0. In particular (k,p)=(1,1) survives forever despite mean one. Over k∈{0,1,…} and p∈[0,1], the full condition is
η<1⟺kp>1 or (k,p)=(1,1).​
(43)

Explore the family tree one individual at a time, maintaining a queue of individuals not yet explored. Initially its size is Y0​=1. Exploring individual t removes it and adds its independent offspring Zt​, so before the queue empties,
Yt​=1+∑j=1t​(Zj​−1).
(44)
One may define the independent increments indefinitely, but the tree exploration stops at T=inf{t:Yt​=0}. This hitting time is the total progeny, including the ancestor. Since each increment is at least −1, a queue cannot jump below zero before hitting zero. If it never hits zero, the family has infinitely many individuals.
Suppose kp<1. A Chernoff bound in moment-generating-function form says that for independent Wj​ and θ>0,
P(∑j=1n​Wj​≥0)≤∏j=1n​EeθWj​.

1−η≤P(T>n)=(1−η)+ηP(T>n)≤1−η+ηe−cn.​
(51)
Thus the unconditional tail tends to the positive survival probability 1−η, with an exponentially small finite-extinction contribution. It does not decay to zero.
If p=1 and kp>1, extinction has probability zero and conditioning on it does not specify a probability law. In that deterministic case total progeny is infinite and P(T>n)=1 for every finite n.
The nondegenerate law above is the binomial branching process conditioned on extinction.
