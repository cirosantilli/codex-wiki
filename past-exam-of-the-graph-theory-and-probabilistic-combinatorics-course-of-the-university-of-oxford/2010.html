For a nonnegative integer q, the chromatic polynomial pG​(q) counts the proper vertex colourings of G using a fixed set of q colours. In a proper colouring, the endpoints of every edge have different colours. The counting function extends uniquely to a polynomial, as follows either from the recurrence below with the edgeless base case pG​(x)=x∣V(G)∣ or from inclusion-exclusion.
Let e=uv. Every proper colouring of G−e falls into exactly one of two classes. If u and v have different colours, it is a proper colouring of G. If they have the same colour, identifying u and v gives a proper colouring of the contracted graph G/e, and this correspondence is bijective. Parallel edges produced by contraction impose identical conditions and can be suppressed. Hence, at every nonnegative integer q,
pG−e​(q)=pG​(q)+pG/e​(q).
(1)
Since polynomials agreeing at infinitely many values agree identically, the deletion-contraction recurrence for the chromatic polynomial is
pG​(x)=pG−e​(x)−pG/e​(x).​
(2)
Throughout, the graphs are finite and simple, as in the usual convention for these statements.

Induct on the number of edges, allowing coefficients outside the displayed degree range to be zero. An edgeless graph has pG​(x)=xn, so its leading coefficient is 1 and all others are zero. For an edge e, write the alternating coefficients of G−e as bi​ and those of the simplified contraction G/e as ci​. The deletion-contraction recurrence for the chromatic polynomial gives
pG​(x)=∑i​(−1)ibi​xn−i−∑j​(−1)jcj​xn−1−j=∑i​(−1)i(bi​+ci−1​)xn−i,
(3)
with c−1​=0. Both smaller graphs have fewer edges, so induction gives nonnegative coefficients bi​,ci​. Thus ai​=bi​+ci−1​≥0. Moreover a0​=b0​=1 and
a1​=b1​+c0​=(m−1)+1=m.
(4)
For a graph with at least one vertex, pG​(0)=0, so the constant term vanishes. Therefore
pG​(x)=i=0∑n−1​(−1)iai​xn−i,a0​=1,a1​=m,ai​≥0.​
(5)
In the one-vertex case, the coefficient of xn−1 is simply zero; this is the same a1​=m=0 convention beyond the written summation range.

An inclusion-exclusion proof gives the coefficients directly. For an edge set F⊆E(G), let c(F) be the number of connected components of the spanning graph (V(G),F), including isolated vertices. Requiring the two endpoints of every edge of F to have the same colour leaves exactly xc(F) colourings. Thus the chromatic polynomial inclusion-exclusion formula is
pG​(x)=∑F⊆E(G)​(−1)∣F∣xc(F).
(6)
Consider a term with n−c(F)=i≤k−2. If (V(G),F) contained a cycle, that cycle would have length at least k. The component containing it would have at least k vertices and hence contribute at least k−1 to n−c(F), a contradiction. Therefore F is a forest. For a forest, n−c(F)=∣F∣, so necessarily ∣F∣=i.
Conversely, every i-element edge set with i≤k−2 is a forest, since a cycle would require at least k edges. Thus exactly (im​) edge sets contribute to the coefficient of xn−i, and they all have sign (−1)i. Consequently
ai​=(im​)(0≤i≤min(k−2,n−1)).​
(7)
This proves girth determines initial chromatic coefficients without requiring the suggested double induction.

The Ramsey number R(s,t) is the smallest integer N such that every red-blue colouring of the edges of KN​ has a red Ks​ or a blue Kt​. Equivalently, every graph on N vertices contains a clique of size s or an independent set of size t.
Set R(1,t)=R(s,1)=1. For s,t≥2, let N=R(s−1,t)+R(s,t−1) and fix a vertex v in a red-blue colouring of KN​. Among the remaining N−1 vertices, either at least R(s−1,t) are red neighbors of v, or at least R(s,t−1) are blue neighbors. In the first case their induced colouring gives a red Ks−1​, which extends with v to a red Ks​, or a blue Kt​. The second case is symmetric. This proves the Ramsey number recursion
R(s,t)≤R(s−1,t)+R(s,t−1).
(8)
Induction on s+t, using the boundary values and Pascal's identity, now gives the binomial upper bound for a Ramsey number:
R(s,t)​≤(t−1s+t−3​)+(t−2s+t−3​)=(t−1s+t−2​)​.​
(9)
In particular this induction proves that the required Ramsey number exists.

Colour the edge ij of the complete graph on {1,…,6} by the class containing ∣j−i∣. Part (a) gives R(3,3)≤6, so there is a monochromatic triangle with vertices i<j<k. Its three edge differences are
x=j−i,y=k−j,z=k−i=x+y.
(10)
They are positive integers in one class. Hence every two-colouring of the positive integers has a monochromatic solution of x+y=z. This is the two-colour Schur theorem. The two summands need not be distinct.

But every infinite arithmetic progression of positive step d meets every sufficiently long dyadic block, because a block of at least d consecutive integers contains a representative of the progression's residue class. Both colours contain arbitrarily long blocks, so neither colour contains an infinite arithmetic progression.

Hall's marriage theorem states that a finite bipartite graph with vertex classes X,Y has a matching covering X if and only if
∣N(S)∣≥∣S∣for every S⊆X.
(12)
Necessity follows because the matched neighbors of the vertices in S are distinct members of N(S).
For sufficiency, induct on ∣X∣. The empty case is immediate. First suppose there is a nonempty proper subset S⊂X with ∣N(S)∣=∣S∣. The induced graph between S and N(S) satisfies Hall's condition, so induction matches S. In the remaining graph between X∖S and Y∖N(S), every T⊆X∖S satisfies
∣N(T)∖N(S)∣=∣N(T∪S)∣−∣N(S)∣≥∣T∣.
(13)
Induction matches the rest, and the two matchings are disjoint.
Otherwise every nonempty proper S⊂X has ∣N(S)∣≥∣S∣+1. Choose any edge xy, which exists by Hall's condition if X is nonempty. Delete x and y. Every nonempty subset of X∖{x} loses at most one neighbor and therefore still satisfies Hall's condition. Induction provides a matching of this smaller graph; append xy. If ∣X∣=1, choosing its neighbor already completes the argument. This proves the theorem.

For disjoint nonempty vertex sets X,Y, let e(X,Y) count edges with one endpoint in each. Their density is
d(X,Y)=∣X∣∣Y∣e(X,Y)​.​
(14)
For 0<ϵ<1, the pair is a regular pair of vertex sets if all X′⊆X and Y′⊆Y with ∣X′∣≥ϵ∣X∣ and ∣Y′∣≥ϵ∣Y∣ satisfy
∣d(X′,Y′)−d(X,Y)∣≤ϵ.​
(15)
Using the convention with a strict bound less than ϵ instead gives the same argument below; the assumed density is strictly larger than ϵ.

Verify Hall's marriage theorem. Let S⊆X; the empty case is immediate. If 0<∣S∣≤ϵn, any x∈S has at least ϵn neighbors in Y, so ∣N(S)∣≥∣S∣.
Next suppose ϵn<∣S∣≤(1−ϵ)n. If ∣N(S)∣<∣S∣, then T=Y∖N(S) has ∣T∣>n−∣S∣≥ϵn. There are no edges between S and T, so d(S,T)=0. But the regularity assumption implies
d(S,T)≥d(X,Y)−ϵ>0,
(16)
a contradiction.
Finally, if ∣S∣>(1−ϵ)n, then ∣X∖S∣<ϵn. Every y∈Y has at least ϵn neighbors in X, so at least one of them lies in S. Thus N(S)=Y and ∣N(S)∣=n≥∣S∣.
All possible sizes of S are covered, even if the middle range is empty. Hall's condition holds, so there is a perfect matching between X and Y. This is the perfect matching criterion for a regular pair with minimum degree.

For an indicator Ii​, Var(Ii​)=P(Ai​)(1−P(Ai​))≤P(Ai​). Covariances vanish for independent pairs. For dependent distinct pairs,
Cov(Ii​,Ij​)=P(Ai​∩Aj​)−P(Ai​)P(Aj​)≤P(Ai​∩Aj​).
(20)
Expanding the variance, with ordered pairs as in the definition of Δ, gives
Var(X)=i∑​Var(Ii​)+i∑​j∼i∑​Cov(Ii​,Ij​)≤EX+Δ.​
(21)
For sufficiently large n, μn​>0. By Chebyshev inequality,
P(Xn​=0)≤P(∣Xn​−μn​∣≥μn​)≤μn2​Var(Xn​)​≤μn​1​+μn2​Δn​​⟶0.
(22)
This is the second moment method: a vanishing relative variance makes a zero count unlikely.

A threshold function p∗(n) has the following meaning: if p/p∗→0, the probability of a K5​ tends to zero; if p/p∗→∞, that probability tends to one. Behavior at a fixed constant multiple is not specified by this definition.
Let X count the five-vertex subsets spanning K5​ in the Erdős-Rényi model G(n,p). Then
μ=EX=(5n​)p10.
(23)
If p≪n−1/2, then μ→0, so Markov inequality gives P(X>0)≤μ→0.
Now let p=n−1/2w with w→∞. Then μ∼w10/120→∞. Distinct copies have dependent indicators only if their vertex sets share at least two vertices. If they share j∈{2,3,4} vertices, they share (2j​) edges; there are O(n10−j) ordered such pairs, and their joint probability is p20−(2j​). Consequently
μ2Δ​=O(n2p1​+n3p31​+n4p61​)=O(n3/2w1​+n3/2w31​+nw61​)⟶0.
(24)
Part (b) gives P(X=0)→0. Therefore
p∗(n)=n−1/2.​
(25)
This is the five-vertex instance of the fixed-size clique appearance threshold.

Start the Galton-Watson process with one ancestor, Z0​=1, and let every individual independently have a number of offspring distributed as Z. With independent copies Zt,j​,
Zt+1​=∑j=1Zt​​Zt,j​,
(26)
where an empty sum is zero. Extinction is the event that some generation is empty; zero is then absorbing. Write q for its probability and 1−q for the survival probability.
Set qt​=P(Zt​=0), with q0​=0. Conditional on the ancestor's number of children, extinction by generation t+1 requires independent extinction by generation t in every child subtree. Thus
qt+1​=fZ​(qt​),fZ​(s)=E(sZ).
(27)
The events {Zt​=0} increase, so qt​↑q. The probability generating function is continuous on [0,1], by domination of sZ by 1, giving q=fZ​(q). If r∈[0,1] is any fixed point, then q0​=0≤r and monotonicity of fZ​ inductively gives qt​≤r. Taking limits proves the Galton-Watson extinction fixed point characterization:
q=min{s∈[0,1]:fZ​(s)=s}.​
(28)
In particular it is the smallest nonnegative fixed point wherever the generating series is defined, since it already lies in [0,1].
Let m=EZ=fZ′​(1−), allowing m=∞. The probability generating function is increasing and convex, with fZ​(1)=1. If m<1, its tangent inequality gives fZ​(s)≥1+m(s−1)>s for s<1, so q=1. If m=1 and P(Z=1)=1, there is positive mass at both zero and some integer at least two; strict convexity gives fZ​(s)>s for s<1, again q=1. The exceptional case Z=1 almost surely has one individual forever, so q=0 despite m=1.
If m>1, the left derivative at 1 gives fZ​(s)−s<0 for s<1 sufficiently close to 1. Since fZ​(0)≥0, continuity supplies a fixed point below 1, possibly 0. Thus survival has positive probability precisely when m>1, apart from deterministic one-child reproduction.
For the random bipartite graph, early exploration of a component alternates between the two vertex classes. Before many vertices are exposed, each discovered vertex has approximately Poisson(c) new neighbors in the other class, since Bin(n−O(1),c/n) tends to that distribution. The two-type mean offspring matrix is
M=(0c​c0​),
(29)
with spectral radius c; equivalently, the mean growth over two generations is c2. The branching-process comparison therefore predicts a giant component exactly when
c>1.​
(30)
For c≤1, the local process dies out almost surely, so no linear-sized component is expected; c=1 is the critical case, not a positive-survival exception. For c>1, the symmetric survival probability is 1−q>0, where q=ec(q−1), predicting approximately 2n(1−q) vertices in the giant. This last passage from local survival to a unique giant is the usual random-graph exploration argument; the branching-process reasoning here supplies the requested threshold explanation rather than a proof of that global theorem.

First take 0<p<1. For X∼Bin(n,p), independence of the Bernoulli trials gives
E(etX)=(1−p+pet)n.
(31)
For x≥p and t>0, Markov inequality yields
P(X≥nx)≤e−tnx(1−p+pet)n.
(32)
Differentiate the logarithm of the right-hand side to minimize it. The stationary point satisfies
et∗​=p(1−x)x(1−p)​,
(33)
which is at least 1 when x≥p. Substitution gives
P(X≥nx)≤[(xp​)x(1−x1−p​)1−x]n.​
(34)
When x=p, use the limit t∗​→0, giving the trivial bound 1.
For x≤p, take t<0. On {X≤nx} we have etX≥etnx, so the same Markov inequality gives the same expression. Its minimizing t∗​ is now nonpositive, yielding the stated lower-tail bound. No rounding of nx is needed, because the exponential event inclusion holds for real thresholds. The cases p=0 and p=1 are deterministic and follow directly, or by the corresponding limits.

Write the bound in part (a) as e−nD(x∥p), where the Bernoulli Kullback-Leibler divergence with natural logarithms is
D(x∥p)=xlogpx​+(1−x)log1−p1−x​.

The sum of the first n/2 variables has distribution Bin(n/2,1/2). For any fixed half-circle interval I, put
DI​=∑i∈I​Xi​−∑i∈/I​Xi​,ZI​=∑i∈I​Xi​+∑i∈/I​(1−Xi​).
(38)
The n terms defining ZI​ are independent Bernoulli variables of parameter 1/2, so ZI​∼Bin(n,1/2) and DI​=ZI​−n/2. Part (b), with h=w/n, gives
P(∣DI​∣≥w)≤2e−2w2/n.
(39)
There are at most n cyclic intervals of length n/2; this also covers the non-wrapping interpretation in the question. They need not be independent, since the union bound always applies. Hence
P(some half-circle has imbalance at least w)≤2ne−2w2/n.
(40)
With w=cnlogn​ and natural logarithms, this becomes 2n1−2c2, which tends to zero for every c>1/2​. In particular,
c=1 suffices.​
(41)
The argument proves uniform half-circle balance for independent Bernoulli variables. It improves on separately bounding the two half sums by recognizing the difference as one centered binomial variable.
