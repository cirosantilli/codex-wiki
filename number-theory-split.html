Number theory

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics
OurBigBook.com Words: 60k Articles: 939
Number theory studies integers, their divisibility, and the patterns created by prime numbers. Its questions often have short statements but rigid answers: which integers divide a given one, when does an equation have integer solutions, and when can arithmetic with congruence classes be reversed? The Euclidean algorithm and modular multiplicative inverses connect these questions through explicit calculations.
For example, 252=22⋅32⋅7 and 198=2⋅32⋅11. Their shared prime factors give greatest common divisor 18. The Euclidean algorithm reaches that answer without finding either complete prime factorization. It also produces 18=4⋅252−5⋅198, an instance of Bézout's identity. This relation certifies every common divisor at once and solves an associated linear Diophantine equation.
Modular arithmetic retains only remainders, but the lost information can be surprisingly controlled. Multiplication by 5 modulo 17 is a permutation of all residue classes, because 5 has modular inverse 7. Multiplication by 5 modulo 15 collapses fifteen residue classes to three. Thus an apparently small change in the modulus changes whether a calculation can be reversed. The Chinese remainder theorem explains another kind of compression: remainders modulo coprime integers can be combined into one remainder modulo their product.
Simple restrictions help reveal the structure without telling the whole story. Every prime number greater than three is congruent to 1 or 5 modulo 6: the other four residue classes force divisibility by 2 or 3. The converse fails because 25 and 35 lie in those classes but are composite numbers. Necessary conditions are useful filters, not substitutes for primality testing.
The subject's appeal lies partly in this tension: prime factorization gives a clean description of every positive integer, while identifying factors and predicting their distribution lead to substantial further questions. Analytic number theory uses tools from analysis to study global patterns, and algebraic number theory extends arithmetic into number fields. Applications include cryptography and error-correcting codes; MIT's discrete applied mathematics course develops modular structures for both purposes. [ref]
Euclid's Elements already gives a greatest-common-measure construction in Book VII, Proposition 2, and the argument that a finite list cannot exhaust the prime numbers in Book IX, Proposition 20. These are documented ancient treatments, rather than evidence that all of number theory began with one author. [ref] [ref]
