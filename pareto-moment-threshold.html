Pareto moment threshold (E[Xq]=αβq/(α−q),0≤q<α)

A Pareto distribution is a proper probability distribution for every α,β>0, even when its mean is infinite. To see both facts, integrate the probability density function against xq. For real q≥0,
E[Xq]=αβα∫β∞​xq−α−1dx=⎩⎨⎧​α−qαβq​,+∞,​0≤q<α,q≥α.​
(22)
The improper integral converges exactly when q−α−1<−1. At q=α it diverges logarithmically; above that it diverges as a positive power of the upper endpoint. Setting q=0 gives one, proving normalization regardless of whether higher moments exist.
Taking q=1,2 and using Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2 when both terms are finite gives
E[X]=α−1αβ​(α>1),Var(X)=(α−1)2(α−2)αβ2​(α>2).
(23)
For 0<α≤1, the nonnegative random variable has expected value +∞; a centered variance about a finite mean is unavailable. For 1<α≤2, the mean is finite and the variance is infinite.[ref] A finite total probability and a finite expected value answer different questions.
At the chosen minimum β=10, α=3 gives E[X]=15, E[X2]=300 and variance 300−152=75, but an infinite third moment. At α=3/2, the mean is 30 and the variance is infinite; at α=3/4, even the mean is infinite, while the median remains the finite number 1024/3≃25.198. At the boundary α=2, the earlier example has mean 20 but infinite variance. A finite sample contains finite values almost surely; that fact cannot establish that the underlying mean or variance is finite.
