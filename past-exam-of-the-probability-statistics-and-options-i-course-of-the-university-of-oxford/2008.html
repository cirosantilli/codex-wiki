A basis of a topology is a collection B of open sets such that every open set is a union of members of B. Equivalently, for every open U and y∈U there is B∈B with y∈B⊆U.
A map f:X→Y is continuous exactly when f−1(B) is open in X for every basic set B of Y. Necessity follows from the definition of a continuous map. For sufficiency, express an arbitrary open set as a union of basic sets and use preservation of unions by inverse images.

A connected set is one whose relative topology admits no decomposition into two disjoint nonempty open sets. A path-connected set permits, between any two points, a continuous map γ:[0,1]→A with the prescribed endpoints.
Path connectedness implies connectedness, because a continuous image of an interval is connected. The converse can fail, for example for the topologist's sine curve. On open subsets of Euclidean spaces Rn, the two properties are equivalent; more generally this holds in locally path-connected spaces.

In a Prüfer code, a vertex label occurs exactly its degree minus one times. A rooted binary tree with 2m+1 vertices has one root of degree two, m−1 other interior vertices of degree three, and m+1 leaves. Its code of length 2m−1 therefore has one label occurring once, m−1 labels occurring twice, and all other labels absent.
Conversely, these multiplicities force exactly those degrees in the tree decoded from the sequence. The unique once-occurring label is its root. This is the complete characterization.
