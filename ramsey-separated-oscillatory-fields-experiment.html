Ramsey separated oscillatory fields experiment

OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 7
A moving chemical atom or molecule can experience the two pulses of Ramsey interferometry by flying through two short oscillatory-field regions. Their common oscillator supplies the reference quantum phase; the flight between them supplies the interrogation time. The long interval need not contain a driving field. This turns a spatial apparatus into a coherent pulse sequence and makes narrow spectral lines possible without filling the entire flight path with a uniform oscillatory field.
Norman Foster Ramsey, Jr.'s 1950 paper calculates the two-state transition probability and averages it over molecular velocities. It is a theoretical investigation, rather than a report of an atomic clock's measured accuracy.[ref]
His 1989 lecture contrasts an electric-resonance apparatus photograph with a magnetic-resonance schematic. Coherent drive regions bracket a static-field flight region; state-dependent deflection converts transitions into detector changes, while differing flight times suppress side fringes.[ref]
The readout infers a change of internal quantum state through state-dependent transmission, as in the Rabi molecular beam magnetic resonance experiment. A useful control is to block one drive region: a narrow fringe requiring both interactions must disappear. Changing the second region's oscillation phase should move the fringe, whereas an incoherent background need not move. These are consequences of the model below, not additional historical measurements. A detector dip alone neither proves perfect preparation nor determines all apparatus-induced frequency shifts; cesium-beam clock procedure adds the controls needed for a practical atomic clock.
The publication sequence begins with Norman Foster Ramsey, Jr.'s 1949 article A New Molecular Beam Resonance Method, Physical Review 76, p. 996, published 1 October; the fuller theoretical paper followed in 1950.[ref]
NIST-7 supplied a practical realization: optical pumping moved caesium populations into the F=3 manifold, without isolating mF​=0. Two separated microwave interactions probed the clock transition; fluorescence detected transferred F=4 atoms. A synthesized reference, tied to a hydrogen maser, sampled opposite sides of resonance for feedback control. Beam reversal tested cavity quantum phase bias; independent velocity determinations checked motion corrections. The observed central fringe resolved the transition, but power-dependent velocity weighting and light shifts still required evaluation.[ref]
