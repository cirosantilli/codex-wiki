Amino acid

 Home Chemistry Chemical substance Organic acid Carboxylic acid
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 19
An amino acid combines carboxylic acid and amine functionality in the same molecule; the name alone does not specify their separation. An alpha-amino acid has its amino nitrogen attached to the carbon immediately adjacent to the carboxyl carbon, the amino acid alpha carbon. For example, H2​N−CH2​−COOH is glycine, whereas H2​N−CH2​−CH2​−COOH has an extra intervening carbon and is not an alpha-amino acid. These chemical formulas describe connectivity, not the predominant protonation state in water.
Most common proteinogenic amino acids share the skeleton H2​N−CH(R)−COOH, with R the amino acid side chain. In glycine, R is hydrogen; in alanine, R=CH3​. Proline is a useful exception to the displayed primary-amine skeleton: its amino nitrogen is part of a ring and has a second carbon substituent. The shared backbone permits assembly into polypeptides, while different amino acid side chains give the resulting proteins diverse chemistry. [ref]
In a chemical solution, protonation changes the electric charges without changing this backbone. Free glycine near neutral pH is predominantly H3​N+−CH2​−COO−, a zwitterion. Its positive and negative groups are joined within one molecule; zero net electric charge does not mean absence of local electric charges, nor does it mean the uncharged drawing H2​N−CH2​−COOH predominates. Glycine protonation explains how lowering or raising pH changes the balance. [ref]
Building a protein joins the carboxyl carbon of one amino acid to the amino nitrogen of another through a peptide bond, an amide linkage. The formal net condensation loses the elements of water; cellular translation uses activated substrates at a ribosome, rather than simply mixing free amino acids in water. Incorporated units are amino acid residues. The interior amide groups no longer have the same acid/base behavior as the two free terminal groups of glycine, so its free-molecule glycine titration cannot be copied independently at every chain position. [ref]
Chirality distinguishes most common proteinogenic amino acids from glycine. The other nineteen members of the conventional twenty have a stereocenter at their amino acid alpha carbon; ordinary translation incorporates their L forms. Glycine's two identical hydrogen substituents make it achiral. Thus being an amino acid, or even an alpha-amino acid, does not by itself imply chirality. [ref]
