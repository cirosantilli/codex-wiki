Ribonucleic acid

 Home Chemistry Polymer Nucleic acid
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 48
Ribonucleic acid, or RNA, is a nucleotide polymer whose nucleotide sequence can both carry information and organize a working molecular shape. Its backbone contains alternating ribose sugars and phosphate groups connected through covalent bonds. Its usual nucleobases are adenine A, uracil U, guanine G, and cytosine C. Thus RNA uses U where DNA uses T; its ribose also differs chemically from DNA's deoxyribose. See NHGRI: RNA.
In transcription, an enzyme uses one DNA strand as a template for RNA. For example, the DNA template 3′-TACGG-5′ specifies 5′-AUGCC-3′ in RNA. The base pairing rules now include A–U rather than A–T, while G–C remains. This RNA matches the other DNA strand's 5′-to-3′ lettering after T is replaced by U. Identifying the template strand matters: replacing T by U in an arbitrarily chosen DNA strand is not a general method for deriving the RNA product. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: from DNA to RNA.
RNA is often a single strand, but single-stranded does not mean straight or entirely unpaired. Complementary regions within one RNA molecule can undergo base pairing, bringing distant positions together. Consider the illustrative twelve-nucleotide nucleotide sequence 5′-GCAUUUUUAUGC-3′. Pair positions 1 with 12, 2 with 11, 3 with 10, and 4 with 9. The resulting stem-loop has four base pairs: G–C, C–G, A–U, U–A. Positions 5–8 remain unpaired in the loop. Both arms belong to the same continuous backbone, and the paired arms have the directions of antiparallel nucleic acid strands.
The diagram proposes a possible pairing pattern, not a prediction that this short RNA must adopt that shape under every condition. Actual RNA folding depends on additional interactions, temperature, ions, and associated proteins; the full three-dimensional arrangement contains more information than the drawn base pairs. The example's purpose is to show how a linear nucleotide sequence can specify contacts within itself.
Different RNA roles make this flexibility useful. Messenger RNA carries a message that a ribosome reads during translation to build a protein. Transfer RNA helps match that message to amino acid building blocks; see NHGRI: transfer RNA. Ribosomal RNA forms structural and catalytic parts of the ribosome. Other RNA participates in gene regulatory networks, changing how strongly genes are expressed. Many RNA molecules therefore function without being translated into proteins; for some genes, functional RNA is the product itself.
Most strikingly, some folded RNA acts as an enzyme: these catalytic RNA molecules are ribozymes. The ribosome's bond-forming active site is built from RNA, although the complete ribosome also contains proteins. Structural evidence appears in Nissen et al., The structural basis of ribosome activity in peptide bond synthesis, Science 289, 920–930 (2000). RNA therefore connects a readable record with physical recognition, regulation, and catalysis: the same kind of polymer can be a message, an adaptor, or part of molecular machinery.
