A sequentially compact space is one in which every sequence has a subsequence converging to a point of the space. A limit point ℓ of S means every open ball around ℓ meets S∖{ℓ}.
In a compact metric space, a sequence with a finite set of values has a constant subsequence. If its value set is infinite and had no limit point, each point of the space would have a neighbourhood containing at most one point of that value set. A finite subcover would then make the value set finite, a contradiction. It therefore has a limit point in the space, and the permitted subsequence result gives a convergent subsequence. This proves sequential compactness.
For the remaining assertions assume the compact space is nonempty, as required for a fixed-point claim. The displacement function q(x)=d(f(x),x) is continuous by the stated continuity of the metric and of f. The extreme value theorem gives a minimum δ=q(p). If δ>0, then p=f(p), and strict decrease of distances implies
q(f(p))=d(f2(p),f(p))<d(f(p),p)=δ,
(38)
contradicting minimality. Thus δ=0 and p is a fixed point. Two distinct fixed points would satisfy d(p,q)=d(f(p),f(q))<d(p,q), also impossible. Hence the fixed point is unique.
On the noncompact real line, take
f(x)=log(1+ex).​
(39)
Its derivative ex/(1+ex) is strictly between zero and one. The mean value theorem therefore gives ∣f(x)−f(y)∣<∣x−y∣ for each distinct pair. Yet f(x)−x=log(1+e−x)>0, so it has no fixed point. Compactness, rather than merely pointwise strict shrinkage, supplies attainment of a zero displacement in the preceding proof.
