In any topological space a closed subset of a compact set is compact: add its open complement to an open cover and take a finite subcover of the compact set. However, compact subsets need not be closed in a general space. In the indiscrete two-point space a singleton is compact but not closed. Conversely a closed subset need not be compact: R itself is closed in R but the cover {(−n,n):n≥1} has no finite subcover.
In a Hausdorff space every compact subset K is closed. Given x∈/K, separate x and each k∈K by disjoint neighborhoods; compactness selects finitely many neighborhoods covering K, and the intersection of their companion neighborhoods of x misses K. Thus the complement is open. Closedness still need not imply compactness, as the same real-line example shows. It does imply compactness if the ambient space is compact.
In a metric space compactness implies boundedness: finitely many radius-one balls cover the set, so their centers have a finite maximal distance from any fixed center and the triangle inequality gives a global bound. The converse is false. An infinite set with discrete metric d(x,y)=1 for x=y is bounded, but its singleton cover has no finite subcover. It is even closed and complete, so closedness plus boundedness alone does not suffice in general metric spaces.
