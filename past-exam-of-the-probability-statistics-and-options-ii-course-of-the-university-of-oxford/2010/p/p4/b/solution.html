An arbitrary orthonormal basis does not have the printed property. For example on [−1,1] with w=1, take the usual normalized constant and linear Legendre polynomials and swap their labels p0​,p1​. The resulting family is still an orthonormal basis but p0​ has one zero, rather than zero. The necessary intended hypothesis is that pk​ has degree k, or equivalently that the basis is obtained by orthogonalizing 1,x,x2,… in that order.
Under this hypothesis, pk​ is orthogonal to every polynomial of degree less than k. Let t1​,…,ts​ be its sign-changing zeros in (a,b) and put q(x)=∏j=1s​(x−tj​). If s<k, then pk​q has a constant nonzero sign except at finitely many points: each interior sign change has been canceled, and all remaining zeros have even multiplicity or occur at endpoints. Since w>0, its integral cannot vanish. But orthogonality to q, of degree s<k, says that integral is zero, a contradiction. Thus s≥k. Since the polynomial has degree k, it follows that
pk​ has exactly k distinct simple zeros, all in (a,b).​
(67)
This proves the requested conclusion in its standard intended setting and explains why it does not follow from the literal basis statement alone.
