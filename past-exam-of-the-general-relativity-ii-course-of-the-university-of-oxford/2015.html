A Killing vector field K generates local isometries: its flow of a vector field Φs​ satisfies Φs∗​g=g. Equivalently, the Lie derivative of the metric tensor vanishes. For the Levi-Civita connection, metric compatibility converts the coordinate expression into the Killing equation:
LK​g=0,∇μ​Kν​+∇ν​Kμ​=0.​
(1)
The Levi-Civita connection is an implicit extra assumption in this question. The printed assumption of vanishing torsion tensor alone does not imply metric compatibility. For example, on Minkowski spacetime, take the affine connection whose only nonzero Christoffel symbol is Γ000​=1. The vector field K=∂0​ preserves the Minkowski metric, but K0​=−1 gives ∇0​K0​=1, contradicting the Killing equation. The following calculation, and the covariant formulas in part (b), use the Levi-Civita connection; this is the role of metric compatibility in Killing identities.
For the stated curvature sign convention, the Ricci identity on a covector has a minus sign:
[∇μ​,∇ν​]Kρ​=−Rσρμν​Kσ​.
(2)
Write Aμνρ​=∇μ​∇ν​Kρ​. Differentiating the Killing equation gives Aμνρ​=−Aμρν​. Alternately using this relation and the Ricci identity gives
Aμνρ​​=−Aμρν​=−Aρμν​+Rσνμρ​Kσ​=Aρνμ​+Rσνμρ​Kσ​=Aνρμ​−Rσμρν​Kσ​+Rσνμρ​Kσ​=−Aμνρ​−Rσρμν​Kσ​−Rσμρν​Kσ​+Rσνμρ​Kσ​.​
(3)
The first Bianchi identity and antisymmetry of the last two indices of the Riemann curvature tensor reduce the three curvature terms to 2Rσμνρ​Kσ​. Consequently the second covariant derivative of a Killing vector is
∇μ​∇ν​Kρ​=Rσμνρ​Kσ​.​
(4)

Use an affine parameter s on the geodesic, so its tangent vector U obeys ∇U​U=0. The product rule, metric compatibility, and the Conformal Killing equation give
dsd​(Xμ​Uμ)​=UνUμ∇ν​Xμ​+Xμ​(∇U​U)μ=UνUμ∇(ν​Xμ)​=λgμν​UμUν.​
(7)
Hence
dsd​(X⋅U)=λg(U,U).​
(8)
It is conserved on every null geodesic with an affine parameter, but generally not on other geodesics. On a timelike geodesic parametrized by proper time, g(U,U)=−1, so the derivative is −λ. A Killing vector field, or a geodesic on which λ vanishes, supplies the exceptional conservation. This is the conformal Killing geodesic identity.
For an arbitrary parameter with ∇U​U=fU, the right-hand side acquires f(X⋅U). Thus even for a null geodesic, the simple conserved expression presupposes an affine parameter.

In Cartesian coordinates on Minkowski spacetime, the Conformal Killing equation becomes
∂μ​Xν​+∂ν​Xμ​=2λημν​.
(9)
Differentiate the equations with index pairs (ν,ρ), (μ,ρ) and (μ,ν) by ∂μ​, ∂ν​ and ∂ρ​, respectively. Adding the first two and subtracting the third, Clairaut's theorem yields the flat conformal Killing integrability formula
∂μ​∂ν​Xρ​=ηνρ​∂μ​λ+ημρ​∂ν​λ−ημν​∂ρ​λ.
(10)
Apply ∂ρ and use ∂ρXρ​=4λ. With the d'Alembert operator □=ηαβ∂α​∂β​, this gives
4∂μ​∂ν​λ=2∂μ​∂ν​λ−ημν​□λ,2∂μ​∂ν​λ=−ημν​□λ.
(11)
A tensor contraction with ημν gives 2□λ=−4□λ, so □λ=0. Returning to the preceding equation proves the stronger statement
∂μ​∂ν​λ=0.​
(12)
Thus the conformal Killing factor is an affine function, not an arbitrary solution of the wave equation.

Write the affine function obtained in part (iii) as λ=c+2bμ​xμ, with constant c and bμ​. A Conformal Killing vector field with precisely this conformal Killing factor is
Xpartμ​=cxμ+2(b⋅x)xμ−x2bμ,x2=ηαβ​xαxβ.
(13)
Direct partial differentiation verifies its Conformal Killing equation. Subtracting it from any other solution leaves a Killing vector field Y. The formula in part (iii), now with λ=0, says ∂μ​∂ν​Yρ​=0. Therefore Y is affine, and the Killing equation makes its linear coefficient antisymmetric after lowering an index. Every solution is consequently
Xμ=aμ+ωμν​xν+cxμ+2(b⋅x)xμ−x2bμ,ωμν​=−ωνμ​.​
(14)
The four constant aμ generate translations, the six independent ωμν​ generate Lorentz transformations, c generates a dilation, and the four bμ generate special conformal transformations. Their constant, linear and quadratic terms are independent; within the linear terms, the antisymmetric coefficient cannot equal a nonzero multiple of the identity. Hence the conformal Killing algebra of Minkowski spacetime has
dimconf(R1,3)=4+6+1+4=15.​
(15)
For λ=1, the canonical choice is the dilation generator D=xμ∂μ​. Its flow of a vector field solves dxμ/ds=xμ, giving
Φs​(x)=esx,Φs∗​η=e2sη,Φs​∘Φt​=Φs+t​.​
(16)
One may add any Killing vector field without changing λ=1, so this choice is not unique. The displayed dilation gives a global one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms. The count of fifteen concerns independent Conformal Killing vector fields; some special conformal transformations have only local flows on all of affine Minkowski spacetime.

A nonzero vector field has hypersurface orthogonality when it is locally normal to a family of hypersurfaces. In terms of its associated differential one-form K♭=gμν​Kνdxμ, the Frobenius theorem gives the equivalent conditions
K♭=fdh,K♭∧dK♭=0,K[μ​∇ν​Kρ]​=0,
(17)
locally with nonzero f and dh; square brackets denote tensor index antisymmetrization.
All coefficients of the Reissner-Nordstrom metric are independent of t, and K=∂t​ has constant coordinate components. Thus the Lie derivative obeys LK​g=∂t​g=0: K is a Killing vector field. Moreover,
K♭=−F(r)dt,dK♭=−F′(r)dr∧dt,K♭∧dK♭=0.​
(18)
Thus K has hypersurface orthogonality, with the t=constant hypersurfaces providing the orthogonal family wherever this chart is regular. It is timelike where F>0, since g(K,K)=−F. Its hypersurface orthogonality also holds in a regular extension across a horizon; singular static coordinates do not invalidate the Frobenius theorem test.

For the three cases, distinguish a curvature singularity from a coordinate singularity. The Reissner-Nordstrom Kretschmann scalar is
K=Rαβγδ​Rαβγδ=r648M2​−r796MQ2​+r856Q4​.
(19)
It diverges at r=0 when either M or Q is nonzero. Any positive root of F instead admits Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates: set v=t+∫dr/F to obtain
ds2=−Fdv2+2dvdr+r2(dθ2+sin2θdϕ2).
(20)
The determinant of its (v,r) block is −1, including at F=0. Hence positive roots of F are removable coordinate singularities of the static chart. The apparent degeneracy at the poles of the angular chart is also a coordinate singularity.

For 0<Q2<M2 there are two distinct positive roots:
r±​=M±M2−Q2​,F=r2(r−r+​)(r−r−​)​.​
(23)
Both roots are coordinate singularities of the static chart. In the standard extension, r+​ is the outer event horizon and r−​ is an inner Cauchy horizon. The curvature singularity is r=0. Between the horizons F<0, so the radial direction is timelike; for 0<r<r−​, F>0 again and the central curvature singularity is timelike. These are the three radial regions of the global structure of a charged spherical black hole.[ref]
For completeness, if Q=0 and M>0, the Schwarzschild spacetime limit has just the positive horizon r=2M and a spacelike curvature singularity at r=0; the formal root r−​=0 of r2F is not a regular inner horizon. If M=Q=0, the metric tensor is flat and the radial origin is regular in Cartesian coordinates.

A regular hypersurface h=0, with dh=0, is a null hypersurface if its normal is null:
gμν∂μ​h∂ν​h=0on h=0.​
(24)
The raised normal is then also tangent to the hypersurface, and its induced metric is degenerate. This is a local geometric property.
In an asymptotically flat spacetime, choose its future null infinity I+. The black hole region and future event horizon are
B=M∖J−(I+),H+=∂J−(I+).​
(25)
Here J−(I+) is the causal past of future null infinity: the region from which a future-directed causal curve can escape to arbitrarily distant observers. An event horizon therefore depends on the global future causal structure, and an arbitrary null hypersurface need not be an event horizon.

The scale factor a(t)>0 specifies the common time dependence of proper distance in cosmology between comoving observers. Their comoving coordinates remain fixed, while their separation is multiplied by a(t). Thus a˙>0 means expansion and a˙<0 means contraction. The coordinate t is cosmic time, measured by the proper time of a comoving observer.
The parameter k fixes the sign of the spatial curvature of an FLRW universe. More precisely, the spatial sectional curvature and three-dimensional scalar curvature are
Kspatial​=a2k​,(3)R=a26k​.​
(37)
The cases k=1,0,−1 have locally spherical geometry, Euclidean geometry, and hyperbolic geometry, respectively. For k=±1, a is the curvature radius of a constant-curvature space in the displayed normalization. Local curvature alone does not fix the global topology.

The Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric implements the cosmological principle: on the scales being modelled, the spatial slices have spatial homogeneity and cosmological isotropy. There is a family of comoving observers whose orthogonal slices share one cosmic time and one scale factor. Each spatial slice is a locally maximally symmetric space, with time-independent normalized k.
The matter is represented by a perfect fluid in general relativity, at rest relative to the comoving observers, with energy density ϱ(t) and pressure p(t) depending only on cosmic time. Its stress-energy tensor is
Tμν​=(ϱ+p)uμ​uν​+pgμν​,u=∂t​.​
(38)
There is no preferred spatial direction, so there is no anisotropic stress or spatial heat flux in that rest frame. Gravity obeys the Einstein field equations; the equations in this paper contain no separate cosmological constant, although a component with p=−ϱ can represent one. An equation of state relating p and ϱ closes the evolution equations. Spatial homogeneity and cosmological isotropy are idealizations of the cosmological background; they do not assert that individual local structures are uniform.

For a>0, the assumed inequality and the Friedmann acceleration equation imply a¨≤0. Thus a is a concave function. Let a0​=a(t0​)>0 and v0​=a˙(t0​)>0. At every earlier time in the same regular expanding interval, a˙(t)≥v0​. Integration gives the concavity bound on past FLRW evolution:
a0​−a(t)=∫tt0​​a˙(s)ds≥v0​(t0​−t),a(t)≤a0​−v0​(t0​−t).​
(44)
The right-hand side reaches zero at t=t0​−a0​/v0​. Hence an everywhere regular positive scale factor cannot extend arbitrarily far into the past. With the usual implicit continuation assumption that the cosmological solution remains regular while a>0, it reaches a(tB​)=0 at a finite past boundary satisfying
0<t0​−tB​≤v0​a0​​=H(t0​)1​.​
(45)
Past expansion lasts at most the present inverse Hubble parameter. The inequality is the essential argument, and equality is attained by a linearly growing scale factor.
There is a literal qualification to the claim that a must reach zero: the printed hypothesis alone does not exclude an earlier curvature singularity with positive limiting a. In arbitrary fixed units, take k=0 and a(t)=1+t​ for t>0, and define ϱ,p using the two Friedmann equations and the Friedmann acceleration equation. Then a˙>0, a¨<0, and ϱ+3p=−3a¨/(4πGa)>0, but a→1 while the energy density and pressure diverge as t↓0. Thus the rigorous unconditional conclusion is the finite bound on a regular past interval; reaching zero additionally requires the stated continuation assumption. Nor does a zero of a alone prove a curvature singularity, as the Milne model illustrates.
