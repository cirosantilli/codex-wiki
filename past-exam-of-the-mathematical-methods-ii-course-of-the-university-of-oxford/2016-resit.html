For a dispersion relation ω(k), phase velocity is vp​=ω/k and group velocity is vg​=dω/dk=vp​+kdvp​/dk. Their local equality of phase and group velocity holds throughout an interval for a nondispersive branch ω=ck. Equality at one particular k requires only that vp​ have zero derivative there, so it does not imply a globally nondispersive medium.
Use k=2π/λ in the prescribed gravity-capillary wave relation. Then ω2=gk+(T/ρ)k3, and differentiation gives
vg​=2gλ/(2π)+2πT/(λρ)​gλ/(2π)+6πT/(λρ)​.​
(8)
In the gravity-dominated limit, vg​=vp​/2; in the surface-tension-dominated limit, vg​=3vp​/2. For g,T,ρ>0, the two are equal at λ=2πT/(ρg)​, the minimum of the phase velocity for this dispersion relation.

For a change of variables in a double integral, first solve for x=x(u,v) and y=y(u,v) and transform every boundary inequality to identify the new integration region S. Compute the Jacobian determinant, replace the integrand by its expression in u,v, and use
∬R​f(x,y)dxdy=∬S​f(x(u,v),y(u,v))​∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​​dudv.​
(16)
A sufficient standard hypothesis is a continuously differentiable one-to-one transformation with nonzero Jacobian determinant in the interior, with harmless measure-zero boundaries. If one coordinate chart does not cover the region injectively, split into suitable pieces rather than silently counting points multiple times. The absolute value is essential for an ordinary area integral.

The notation (∂f/∂x)y​ means the derivative with respect to x while y is held fixed:
(∂x∂f​)y​=limh→0​hf(x+h,y)−f(x,y)​.
(33)
For a differentiable function, its first-order height change is df=fx​dx+fy​dy. If both coefficients vanish, the tangent plane is horizontal and every directional derivative vanishes: the surface is flat to first order at that point. It need not be constant in a neighbourhood; f=x2+y2 at the origin is a counterexample. Existence of coordinate partials alone, without differentiability, would not justify this tangent-plane conclusion.

At a stationary point, the linear terms vanish, so Δf≃21​(Ah2+2Bhk+Ck2), with A=fxx​, B=fxy​, C=fyy​. Let D=AC−B2 be the Hessian determinant. For A=0, completing the square gives
2Δf≃A(h+AB​k)2+AD​k2.
(34)
The second-derivative test therefore yields
D>0, A>0: strict local minimum;D>0, A<0: strict local maximum;D<0: saddle point.​
(35)
When D<0 the quadratic form is indefinite, including the case A=0, B=0. When D=0, the quadratic approximation is degenerate and the test is inconclusive: higher-order terms may produce a minimum, maximum or saddle. For nondegenerate cases the quadratic term dominates the Taylor remainder sufficiently close to the point.

The gradient components are fx​=2x(1+6x2) and fy​=2y(1−6y2). Their real simultaneous zeros are (0,0) and (0,±1/6​). The Hessian matrix is diagonal:
H=diag(2+36x2,2−36y2).
(36)
At the origin it is positive definite; at each other critical point it is diag(2,−4) and indefinite. Hence
(0,0): strict local minimum, f=0;(0,±1/6​): saddle points, f=1/12.​
(37)
There are no local maxima. The contours are symmetric under either coordinate reflection. Small positive levels have a closed component surrounding the minimum; these meet the saddle-level separatrices at level 1/12. That critical contour has the useful exact form 3x4+x2=3(y2−1/6)2. Other components extend toward large ∣y∣, where the −3y4 term makes the function unbounded below.
