A normal mode of a stable undamped linear coupled oscillator has a single angular frequency and a fixed ratio of component displacements. All components share a sinusoidal time factor, with relative oscillation phases of zero or π for real mode shapes. A general free motion is a superposition of the normal modes. For this system the symmetric stiffness matrix gives real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors; positive spring constants make the squared angular frequencies positive.
Write m=m1​=m2​>0 and κ=k12​. Assume ideal massless linear springs, negligible damping and positive k1​,k2​,κ. By Hooke's law and Newton's second law,
mx¨1​=−(k1​+κ)x1​+κx2​,mx¨2​=κx1​−(k2​+κ)x2​.​
(28)
The coupling spring extends by x2​−x1​, giving equal and opposite coupling forces.
For a normal mode, take x=Re(aeiωt). Nonzero eigenvectors require
det(k1​+κ−mω2−κ​−κk2​+κ−mω2​)=0.
(29)
Solving this quadratic equation in mω2 gives
ω±2​=2mk1​+k2​+2κ ±(k1​−k2​)2+4κ2​​.​
(30)
A corresponding mode shape has ratio
a1​a2​​=κk1​+κ−mω±2​​,
(31)
and the general real solution is x=C−​a−​cos(ω−​t+ϕ−​)+C+​a+​cos(ω+​t+ϕ+​). The arbitrary amplitudes and oscillation phases account for four independent initial data.
Weak coupling alone does not guarantee large beats. A useful representative sketch assumes nearly equal uncoupled angular frequencies. In the resonant case k1​=k2​=k with κ≪k, the mode shapes are (1,1) and (1,−1), and
ω−2​=k/m,ω+2​=(k+2κ)/m.
(32)
For x1​(0)=A, x2​(0)=x˙1​(0)=x˙2​(0)=0, both normal modes have equal coefficients. With ωˉ=(ω+​+ω−​)/2 and Δω=ω+​−ω−​,
x1​=Acos(ωˉt)cos(Δωt/2),x2​=Asin(ωˉt)sin(Δωt/2).​
(33)
These rapid oscillations have slowly varying complementary wave envelopes. The initial displacement shifts almost entirely to the other mass at t=π/Δω, and returns after 2π/Δω. Arbitrary initial data give a different superposition; if ∣k1​−k2​∣≫κ, the normal modes are localized mainly on individual masses, and strong exchange is suppressed.
Holding one mass fixed leaves the coupling spring attached and stretched by the other mass. Therefore the two constrained angular frequencies are
ω1​=(k1​+κ)/m​,ω2​=(k2​+κ)/m​.​
(34)
These differ from the angular frequencies obtained by physically removing the coupling spring. Ordinary frequencies in hertz are each corresponding ω/(2π).
Finally, for k1​>k2​ and κ>0, put D=(k1​−k2​)2+4κ2​>k1​−k2​. Then
ω−2​−ω22​=2mk1​−k2​−D​<0,ω+2​−ω12​=2mD−(k1​−k2​)​>0.
(35)
Since ω1​>ω2​, taking positive square roots proves
ω−​<ω2​<ω1​<ω+​.​
(36)
At κ=0, the outer inequalities become equalities.

Let S be a compact piecewise smooth oriented surface, n its chosen unit normal vector, and ∂S its entire oriented boundary. Let A be a continuously differentiable vector field on a neighborhood of S. Stokes theorem states
∬S​(∇×A)⋅ndS=∮∂S​A⋅dr.​
(37)
Here ∇×A is the curl, dS is the surface area element, and dr is the directed line integral element. The boundary follows the right-hand rule relative to n; equivalently the surface lies to the left when walking along its boundary with one's head toward n.
The topological requirement is orientability, together with the regularity and boundary assumptions above. Simple connectivity is unnecessary. An annulus or cylindrical side is allowed, provided every boundary component is included with its induced direction. For a concrete counterexample to a disk-only restriction, take the planar annulus 1≤x2+y2≤4 and A=(−y/2,x/2,0). The curl is ez​, the surface integral is 3π, and the outer counterclockwise and inner clockwise line integrals sum to 4π−π=3π.
For S in the xy plane with upward normal vector and A=(P,Q,0), its curl has vertical component Qx​−Py​. Thus Stokes theorem becomes Green's theorem:
∮∂D​(Pdx+Qdy)=∬D​(∂x∂Q​−∂y∂P​)dxdy.​
(38)
The outer boundary is counterclockwise and any inner boundaries are clockwise.

Let F=(Fx​,Fy​,Fz​) be continuously differentiable near P. Take a small axis-aligned rectangular volume around P, with side lengths Δx,Δy,Δz shrinking with bounded aspect ratios. The outward flux through its two x faces is, to leading order,
[Fx​(x+Δx/2,y,z)−Fx​(x−Δx/2,y,z)]ΔyΔz=∂x∂Fx​​(P)ΔxΔyΔz+o(ΔV),
(44)
where ΔV=ΔxΔyΔz. The analogous face pairs give ∂Fy​/∂y and ∂Fz​/∂z. Dividing total flux by ΔV and taking the limit gives
∇⋅F=∂x∂Fx​​+∂y∂Fy​​+∂z∂Fz​​.​
(45)
The divergence is thus the local outward flux per unit volume; the symbols on the right are Cartesian partial derivatives.
For a bounded volume V with piecewise smooth closed boundary S, subdivide into small cells. Each internal face occurs twice with opposite normal vectors, so its flux cancels. The remaining sum is the exterior surface integral; the cell sums of (∇⋅F)ΔV converge to the volume integral. Assuming continuous first partial derivatives on a neighborhood of the closure of V, this proves the divergence theorem:
∬​S​F⋅ndS=∭V​∇⋅FdV.​
(46)
The unit normal vector n points out of V. Multiple boundary components are allowed; at a cavity wall it points into the cavity.

Wave dispersion means that different frequencies propagate with different phase velocities, so a wave packet generally changes shape. Equivalently the dispersion relation is not proportional to k over the relevant band. Three natural examples are the colour separation in a rainbow, where the refractive index of water depends on wavelength; deep-water surface gravity waves, for which ω2=gk and long waves have larger phase velocity; and capillary waves, whose short-wave limit is ω2=(γ/ρ)k3 and whose shorter waves have larger phase velocity. Here g is gravitational acceleration, γ is surface tension, and ρ is fluid mass density, with positive k and negligible viscosity.
A distinct application is an optical prism spectrometer: the wavelength-dependent refractive index produces different refraction angles, separating spectral components for measurement. This exploits wave dispersion deliberately.

The result depends on the component amplitudes and wave phases; adding many frequencies does not by itself guarantee one localized wave packet. For a definite sketch, take many equally spaced wavenumbers kj​=k0​+jΔk in the same fixed band, with equal, aligned complex amplitudes B/N. Linearize the dispersion relation as ω(kj​)≃ωc​+vg​(kj​−k0​), where ωc​=k02​/m. Then with ξ=x−vg​t the wave envelope is
NB​∑j=−(N−1)/2(N−1)/2​eijΔkξ=NB​sin(Δkξ/2)sin(NΔkξ/2)​
(58)
for odd N. This is a normalized Dirichlet kernel: constructive interference occurs near ξ=0, while most other positions undergo partial cancellation. At finite N there are repeated groups separated by 2π/Δk.
As N grows with the half-bandwidth K fixed, Δk decreases and the repetition spacing grows. The continuous flat-band limit is
y(x,t)≃Bei(k0​x−ωc​t)Kξsin(Kξ)​,ξ=x−vg​t.​
(59)
Thus a sinc function envelope has a central group between its first zeros at ξ=±π/K, smaller side lobes and rapidly oscillating crests. At fixed bandwidth the limiting central width is 2π/K; it does not shrink indefinitely just because more components are added. Smoother spectral weights can reduce the side lobes, and random wave phases would produce a different shape.
