For an oriented piecewise smooth surface S with boundary ∂S, and a vector field F continuously differentiable on a neighborhood of S, Stokes theorem states
∫S​(∇×F)⋅ndS=∮∂S​F⋅dr.​
(22)
Here n is the chosen unit normal vector, dS is the unsigned surface area element, and dr is the tangent displacement along the boundary. The right-hand rule pairs the boundary orientation with n. If there are several boundary components, the line integral includes all of them with their induced orientations.
A simply connected surface is connected and every closed loop on it can be continuously contracted to a point while remaining on the surface. A disc is an example. An annulus is not simply connected: a loop winding once around its hole cannot contract within it.
Stokes' theorem is valid for both types of surface, under the smoothness and orientation hypotheses above. Simple connectivity is not a requirement of the theorem. For a concrete annular example, take F=(−y/2,x/2,0) and a≤r≤R in the horizontal plane. Its curl is (0,0,1), so the left side is π(R2−a2). The right side is the counterclockwise outer-circle integral πR2 minus the counterclockwise inner-circle integral πa2, giving exactly the same answer. Omitting the inner boundary would be an incorrect application of the theorem.

A dispersive medium has frequency-dependent phase velocity, so different Fourier components generally propagate with different phases. A dispersion relation specifies ω as a function of the wave number k, or an equivalent relation between them. For a monochromatic factor ei(kx−ωt), a point of fixed phase moves at
vp​=kω​.​
(33)
For a narrow wave packet centered at k∗​, expand ω(k)≃ω(k∗​)+(k−k∗​)ω′(k∗​). The envelope then translates at the group velocity
vg​=dkdω​.​
(34)
The phase velocity tracks crests of a monochromatic wave. The group velocity tracks a slowly varying envelope when the narrow-band approximation is valid; in the ideal lossless guide here it also equals the longitudinal energy-transport speed. It is not a universal formula for signal-front speed in every possible dispersive medium.
For the propagating branch of the supplied electromagnetic waveguide relation, take ω0​>0, k>0 and ω=ω02​+c2k2​. Differentiation gives
vp​=kω02​+c2k2​​>c,vg​=ω02​+c2k2​c2k​<c,vp​vg​=c2.​
(35)
The product alone would also allow vp​=vg​=c; the strictly positive cutoff is what makes the inequalities strict. At k=0 the phase-speed expression is undefined, and these formulas are interpreted through the propagating limit.
A moving monochromatic crest does not carry a new message. An information-bearing modulation is made from a range of frequencies; here its packet and energy move below c, and the high-frequency limit approaches c. Causal propagation of a newly switched-on disturbance is governed by the electromagnetic equations, so the superluminal phase velocity does not permit faster-than-light signalling. These are the waveguide phase and group velocities for a mode with nonzero cutoff, rather than a zero-cutoff mode with both velocities equal to c.

A normal mode is a motion of a linear oscillatory system in which every coordinate has the same angular frequency and a fixed relative amplitude and phase. For a conservative small-displacement system it has the form
x(t)=qcos(ωt+ϕ),Kq=ω2Mq,
(43)
where q is the mode shape, K the stiffness matrix, and M the mass matrix. Superposing normal modes gives the general small-oscillation motion when their eigenvectors form a complete basis.
Two common examples are standing transverse oscillations of a string fixed at both ends, and oscillations of coupled masses and springs. The string's mode shapes are sinusoidal along its length; the coupled system's mode shapes are fixed patterns of particle displacements. The following parts determine those patterns for the three-mass system in the source figure.

To specify “initial motion” unambiguously, choose each mode to pass through equilibrium at t=0 with x˙(0) proportional to its listed mode shape. The arrows in the figure then describe relative initial velocities:
  • In mode 1, all three masses move in the same direction; the central mass has 2​ times either outer velocity.
  • In mode 2, the two outer masses move with equal and opposite velocities, while the central mass remains stationary.
  • In mode 3, the outer masses move together while the central mass moves oppositely with 2​ times their speed.
Reversing every arrow changes only the common phase. A quarter-cycle later these same ratios describe the extremal displacements, with zero instantaneous velocity.
Now replace the central mass by M, leaving the outer masses equal to m. The antisymmetric mode shape q=(1,0,−1)T gives zero net force on the central mass, since kx1​+kx3​=0. Each outer mass still obeys mx¨=−2kx. Consequently
ω2​=m2k​​ is unchanged for every M>0.​
(48)
This middle-mass-independent mode of a fixed three-mass spring chain is exact, not merely a large-M approximation. The other two modes move the central mass and generally change. For example, their symmetric-sector frequencies satisfy
(2k−mω2)(2k−Mω2)−2k2=0,
(49)
which explicitly depends on M.
