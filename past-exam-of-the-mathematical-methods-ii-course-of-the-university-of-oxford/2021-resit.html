a OurBigBook.com
For a continuously differentiable vector field F on a neighborhood of an oriented, piecewise smooth surface S, Stokes theorem states
∮∂S​F⋅dr=∬S​(∇×F)⋅n^dS.​
(23)
Here ∂S is the boundary curve, dr is its directed tangent element, n^ is the chosen unit normal vector, dS is the surface area element, and ∇×F is the curl. The right-hand rule relates the boundary direction to n^. Thus circulation around the edge equals the flux of curl through the spanning surface. Reversing both orientations changes both signs. One can replace a difficult line integral by an easier surface integral, or replace one spanning surface by another within the region of regularity.
For a bounded volume V with piecewise smooth closed boundary and a continuously differentiable vector field on a neighborhood of its closure, the divergence theorem states
∬∂V​F⋅n^out​dS=∭V​∇⋅FdV.​
(24)
Here n^out​ points out of V, including into any holes, dV is the volume element, and ∇⋅F is the divergence. It equates total outward flux to integrated source strength. It converts a closed surface integral into a volume integral, and explains why internal fluxes cancel in a subdivided region. Singularities cannot be included without checking the hypotheses.
For a conservative force, the work around every closed path is zero. Stokes theorem gives zero flux of curl through every sufficiently small patch. Dividing by its area and shrinking the patch shows that every component of the continuous curl vanishes:
∮C​F⋅dr=0,∇×F=0,F=−∇U.​
(25)
Here U is potential energy; conversely, zero curl implies a conservative vector field on a simply connected domain. The divergence theorem then gives the compatible relation ∬∂V​F⋅n^dS=−∭V​∇2UdV. There is no general zero-divergence condition on a conservative force: F=−r=−∇(r2/2) is a concrete counterexample, with ∇⋅F=−3.

For the reflection of a string wave from a freely moving endpoint mass, let c=T/μ​, k=ω/c>0 and use the complex time-harmonic wave convention
y(x,t)=Re{Ai​(eikx+Re−ikx)e−iωt},x≤0.

The string pulls its endpoint toward negative x, so its transverse force on the attached mass is −Tyx​(0,t). Newton's second law gives the boundary condition mytt​(0,t)=−Tyx​(0,t). Substitution yields
mω2(1+R)=iTk(1−R),R=iTk+mω2iTk−mω2​=Z−imωZ+imω​,Z=cT​=Tμ​.​

Here Z is the characteristic impedance of a stretched string. Since the incident and reflected waves share the same string, their mean power ratio is the squared modulus of this displacement reflection coefficient:
R=∣R∣2=1.​
(49)

Define a common displacement coordinate X=(x1​+qx2​)/(1+q) and a relative displacement δ=x1​−x2​. Adding and subtracting the equations of motion gives
X¨=0,δ¨+ω2δ=0.
(56)
The general real motions of the two normal modes are therefore
zero mode:oscillatory mode:​(x1​,x2​)=(C+Dt)(1,1),(x1​,x2​)=(Acosωt+Bsinωt)(1,−1/q).​
(57)
Here C,D,A,B are independent real constants. The zero mode includes uniform drift as well as a static common displacement; writing only a constant times cos0t would miss the independent linear-in-time solution. Their superposition is
x1​=C+Dt+Acosωt+Bsinωt,x2​=C+Dt−q1​(Acosωt+Bsinωt).​

The lighter particle then oscillates against two effectively anchored springs with combined spring constant 2k. A common uniform rotation remains an allowed zero mode; the heavy particle is stationary in the limit only if the initial common drift is zero or tends to zero, as it does for bounded total angular momentum.
