Both masses in the source's absorber diagram are free: there is no additional spring attaching M to a wall. Let their displacements be X and x. The equations are
MX¨=F0​cosωt+k(x−X),mx¨=−k(x−X).
(35)
For a periodic particular response X=Acosωt, x=Bcosωt, the amplitude equations are (k−Mω2)A−kB=F0​ and −kA+(k−mω2)B=0. Their solution is
A=ω2[Mmω2−k(M+m)]F0​(k−mω2)​,B=ω2[Mmω2−k(M+m)]F0​k​​.
(36)
At k=mω2​, A=0 and B=−F0​/k: the spring force on the larger mass cancels the applied force at every instant. This is antiresonance, a frequency-selective cancellation, rather than dissipation of energy.
There is a nearby undamped resonance at kres​=Mmω2/(M+m). For m≪M it lies close to the cancellation setting. More quantitatively, write k=mω2(1+ϵ). Then
A=−ω2[m+ϵ(M+m)]F0​ϵ​.
(37)
For ∣ϵ∣≪m/(M+m), the amplitude relative to the unconnected mass's amplitude F0​/(Mω2) is approximately (M/m)∣ϵ∣. A light absorber therefore needs very accurate tuning and loses suppression when the driving frequency changes. Its own travel ∣B∣=F0​/(mω2) can also become large, limiting linearity and available stroke.
Without damping, resonance growth and persistent free oscillations remain; special initial conditions select the displayed periodic particular response. The free pair also has rigid-translation motion. These limitations prevent interpreting the cancellation as broadband damping or as a unique attracting late-time state.
