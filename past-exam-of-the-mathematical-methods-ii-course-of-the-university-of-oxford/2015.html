For r=x2+y2+z2​>0, the radial vector field has components Fi​=xi​f(r). The product rule gives
∇⋅F=∑i​[f(r)+xi​f′(r)rxi​​]=3f(r)+rf′(r).
(10)
Thus for f(r)=rn−1,
∇⋅F=(n+2)rn−1,r>0​.
(11)
When n=−2, F=r^/r2 and the ordinary divergence is zero at every nonzero radius. However, the field is singular at the origin. Its outward flux through a radius-R sphere is (1/R2)(4πR2)=4π, so one cannot apply the smooth-field divergence theorem across the origin and conclude that this flux vanishes.
In the language of distributions, the complete distributional divergence identity is ∇⋅(r^/r2)=4πδ(3)(r)​. Here δ(3) is the three-dimensional Dirac delta function. Without that generalized interpretation, the correct statement is simply zero divergence away from the origin and undefined ordinary divergence at the singular point. Removing a small ball around the origin restores the ordinary theorem: the inner boundary has inward radial orientation and cancels the outer flux.

Take the constant speed parameter v=0, so r=x+vt and s=x−vt are independent characteristic coordinates. The chain rule gives
∂x​=∂r​+∂s​,∂t​=v(∂r​−∂s​).
(17)

Fxx​=Frr​+2Frs​+Fss​,Ftt​=v2(Frr​−2Frs​+Fss​).
(18)

At a stationary point of a twice continuously differentiable function, write the Hessian matrix as H=(AB​BC​) and its determinant as D=AC−B2. The quadratic term in the Taylor expansion determines the local nature whenever H is nonsingular.
If D>0, its two eigenvalues have the same sign. With A>0 the quadratic form is positive definite, giving a strict local minimum; with A<0 it is negative definite, giving a strict local maximum. If D<0, the eigenvalues have opposite signs and the point is a saddle, with nearby increasing and decreasing directions. Thus
D>0, A>0: min;D>0, A<0: max;D<0: saddle​.
(25)
If D=0, the test is inconclusive. For example, x4+y4, −x4−y4 and x4−y4 all have zero gradient and zero Hessian matrix at the origin, yet give a minimum, a maximum and a saddle respectively. One must inspect higher-order terms or use another argument in such a case.

Both masses in the source's absorber diagram are free: there is no additional spring attaching M to a wall. Let their displacements be X and x. The equations are
MX¨=F0​cosωt+k(x−X),mx¨=−k(x−X).
(35)
For a periodic particular response X=Acosωt, x=Bcosωt, the amplitude equations are (k−Mω2)A−kB=F0​ and −kA+(k−mω2)B=0. Their solution is
A=ω2[Mmω2−k(M+m)]F0​(k−mω2)​,B=ω2[Mmω2−k(M+m)]F0​k​​.
(36)
At k=mω2​, A=0 and B=−F0​/k: the spring force on the larger mass cancels the applied force at every instant. This is antiresonance, a frequency-selective cancellation, rather than dissipation of energy.
There is a nearby undamped resonance at kres​=Mmω2/(M+m). For m≪M it lies close to the cancellation setting. More quantitatively, write k=mω2(1+ϵ). Then
A=−ω2[m+ϵ(M+m)]F0​ϵ​.
(37)
For ∣ϵ∣≪m/(M+m), the amplitude relative to the unconnected mass's amplitude F0​/(Mω2) is approximately (M/m)∣ϵ∣. A light absorber therefore needs very accurate tuning and loses suppression when the driving frequency changes. Its own travel ∣B∣=F0​/(mω2) can also become large, limiting linearity and available stroke.
Without damping, resonance growth and persistent free oscillations remain; special initial conditions select the displayed periodic particular response. The free pair also has rigid-translation motion. These limitations prevent interpreting the cancellation as broadband damping or as a unique attracting late-time state.

Interpret the axis-directed route as successive coordinate-axis-parallel segments
(0,0,0)⟶(1,0,0)⟶(1,2,0)⟶(1,2,1).
(51)
On the first segment, y=z=0, so the field vanishes. On the second, z=0, so it again vanishes. On the last, x=1, y=2 and only dz is nonzero, giving F⋅dr=8zdz. Thus
∫C​F⋅dr=0+0+∫01​8zdz=4​.
(52)
The second and third segments are parallel to the corresponding global axes; they do not return to the global y- or z-axis before reaching the endpoint.

(53)
This is the fundamental theorem for line integrals: an exact one-form integrates to the difference of its single-valued potential at the endpoints. The agreement is guaranteed for every piecewise smooth connecting path, rather than being an accident of these two examples. In particular the integral around a closed path assembled from one route and the reverse of the other is zero.

Stokes theorem states
∮C​A⋅dr=∬S​(∇×A)⋅n^dS​,
(54)
with boundary orientation induced by the chosen surface normal and with sufficient smoothness of the field and surface. Choose the outward normal on the upper unit hemisphere; its equatorial boundary runs counterclockwise as viewed from +z.
For the stated field, the curl is
∇×A=(−2yz−(−2yz), 0−0, 0−(−1))=z^.
(55)
On the unit sphere, the outward normal is n^=(x,y,z). In spherical coordinates, the surface integral over the upper hemisphere is
∫02π​∫0π/2​cosθsinθdθdφ=π.
(56)
