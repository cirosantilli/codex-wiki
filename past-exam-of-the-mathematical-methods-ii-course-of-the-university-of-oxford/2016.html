For the real-valued expression, first consider the one-sided limit x→0+. Its natural logarithm is xln(ln(1+x)). Since ln(1+x)/x→1,
xln(ln(1+x))=xlnx+xln(xln(1+x)​)⟶0,
(3)
using xlnx→0. The continuity of the exponential function therefore gives right-hand limit 1.
The printed two-sided notation needs qualification: ln(1+x)<0 for −1<x<0, so raising it to an arbitrary real power does not define a real-valued function throughout a left neighbourhood of zero. There is consequently no ordinary two-sided real limit on an open punctured neighbourhood with the expression as written. The value 1 is the natural right-hand interpretation.

The gradient vanishes when 3x2−2=3y2−2=0. Put s=2/3​. The Hessian matrix is diag(6x,6y), so the second-derivative test gives:
  • (s,s): a strict local minimum, with f=−8s/3;
  • (−s,−s): a strict local maximum, with f=8s/3;
  • (s,−s) and (−s,s): saddle points, both with f=0.
The positive-definite and negative definite cases give small closed level curves around the minimum and maximum. At each saddle, the zero contour crosses itself. In fact,
f=(x+y)(x2−xy+y2−2),
(12)
so the entire zero level curve is the union of the line y=−x and the ellipse x2−xy+y2=2. This factorization fixes the topology of a rough contour sketch. Neither local extremum is global, since the cubic is unbounded above and below.

For a continuously differentiable vector field F near a piecewise smooth oriented surface S, Stokes theorem states
∮∂S​F⋅dr=∬S​(∇×F)⋅n^dS.​
(13)
The circulation around the whole boundary equals the flux of the curl through the surface. The unit normal vector and boundary direction must be compatible with the right-hand rule: curling the right-hand fingers in the boundary direction points the thumb along the normal. Reversing both orientations reverses both integrals.

Apply Stokes theorem to F=ψB, with B an arbitrary constant vector. The curl product rule for a scalar field reduces to ∇×(ψB)=∇ψ×B. Using the scalar triple product,
B⋅∮C​ψdr=∬S​n^⋅(∇ψ×B)dS=B⋅∬S​(n^×∇ψ)dS.

∮C​ψdr=∬S​(n^×∇ψ)dS.​
(15)
In particular, the ordering of the cross product is normal crossed with gradient; reversing it would change the sign.

A normal mode has a fixed pattern of relative displacements, with every coordinate proportional to one common time-dependent factor. There are three independent normal modes in this three-coordinate model: two internal vibrations and one zero-frequency rigid-body translation. The latter moves all three atoms together without stretching either spring. This count concerns the specified one-dimensional model, rather than a molecule with unrestricted three-dimensional motion.

For the zero normal mode, x1​=x2​=x3​=A+Bt. The molecule translates uniformly; a common constant displacement is the special case B=0. For the lower vibrational normal mode, put ω=k/m​:
x(t)=A(1,0,−1)cos(ωt+ϕ).

Substituting y(x,t)=f(x)g(t) into the wave equation on a string gives f′′g=fg′′/c2. Wherever both factors are nonzero,
f(x)f′′(x)​=c2g(t)g′′(t)​.
(32)

f′′=αf,g′′=αc2g.​
(33)
The resulting differential equations extend through zeros of f or g by continuity; division at a node is unnecessary.
