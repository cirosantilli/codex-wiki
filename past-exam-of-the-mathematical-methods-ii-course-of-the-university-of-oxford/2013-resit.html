ln(1+x)=x−2x2​+3x3​+O(x4),1+2x​=1+x−2x2​+2x3​+O(x4).

For one sine mode, yxx​=−kn2​An​sin(kn​x)cos(ωn​t) and ytt​=−ωn2​An​sin(kn​x)cos(ωn​t). The wave equation on a string therefore holds when
ωn2​=μT​kn2​,ωn​=vkn​,v=T/μ​.​
(4)
The superposition principle gives the same equation for the sum, assuming enough regularity to differentiate termwise, or interpreting finite-energy data in the usual modal sense. Fixed endpoints require sin(kn​L)=0, so kn​=nπ/L. Every cosine time factor has zero time derivative at t=0, giving the held-at-rest initial condition.
The coefficients must represent the actual held profile f(x) through its Fourier sine series, An​=(2/L)∫0L​f(x)sin(nπx/L)dx for n≥1. Thus the series accommodates the displacement initial condition once these coefficients are chosen; it does not select an unspecified initial shape by itself. The printed n=0 term is identically zero because sin0=0, so the nontrivial fixed-end modes start at n=1.

Apply the ordinary product rule to the components of the curl. For example, the first component is
[∇×(ϕF)]x​=∂y​(ϕFz​)−∂z​(ϕFy​)=ϕ(∂y​Fz​−∂z​Fy​)+ϕy​Fz​−ϕz​Fy​.

(15)

After transients have decayed, seek the driven response x=asinωt. At the given ω2=k/m, substitution gives
(2−2​−20​)a=(F0​).
(26)
The second row forces a1​=0, and the first gives a2​=−F/2. Hence, in the negligible-damping steady-response limit,
x1​(t)=0,x2​(t)=−2F​sinωt.​

For a separated solution y=X(x)Q(t), the wave equation gives X′′/X=Q¨​/(v2Q). Choose the separation constant −k2. Then
X=Acoskx+Bsinkx,Q=Ccos(vkt)+Dsin(vkt).
(28)
These are the oscillatory separated building blocks. Since no endpoint conditions have yet been imposed, the general solution is a superposition over the allowed continuum of k, rather than one product with four constants. Equivalently, combining the left- and right-travelling separated components gives the D'Alembert's formula
y(x,t)=F(x−vt)+G(x+vt),​
(29)
with arbitrary twice differentiable functions F,G on the relevant characteristic intervals. Indeed, in characteristic coordinates ξ=x−vt, η=x+vt, the equation becomes yξη​=0, whose general solution is exactly this sum. This also includes zero-frequency and polynomial limits that would be omitted by restricting to one nonzero-k sine mode.

For a twice continuously differentiable function, a finite second-order Taylor expansion about an interior point is
f(x+a,y+b)=f(x,y)+fx​a+fy​b+21​(Aa2+2Bab+Cb2)+o(a2+b2),
(34)
where A=fxx​, B=fxy​, C=fyy​ at that point. A differentiable interior extremum requires fx​=fy​=0. For a strict minimum guaranteed by the quadratic term, the Hessian matrix must be positive definite. Completing the square gives Aa2+2Bab+Cb2=A(a+Bb/A)2+(AC−B2)b2/A. Thus sufficient nondegenerate minimum conditions are
fx​=fy​=0,A>0,D=AC−B2>0.​
(35)
If D=0, the second-order test is inconclusive; for example x4+y4 has a strict minimum at zero despite a zero Hessian. The finite Taylor argument only needs C2 regularity. The source's infinite Taylor representation additionally needs suitable analyticity: a smooth flat function, such as e−1/(x2+y2) extended by zero at the origin, has every Taylor coefficient zero there but is positive nearby.

At an interior stationary point, let A=fxx​, B=fxy​, C=fyy​ and D=AC−B2. The quadratic change in the Taylor expansion is negative in every nonzero displacement when the Hessian matrix is negative definite. Applying the minimum test to −f gives the sufficient nondegenerate maximum conditions
fx​=fy​=0,A<0,D>0.​
(36)
The quadratic term then dominates the smaller Taylor remainder in a sufficiently small neighborhood, proving a strict local maximum. A vanishing determinant requires higher-order analysis instead.

At an interior stationary point, a negative determinant of the symmetric Hessian matrix means its two eigenvalues have opposite signs. Along their eigenvectors the leading quadratic change has opposite signs, and the higher-order remainder cannot reverse these signs sufficiently close to the point. Hence the nondegenerate saddle condition is
fx​=fy​=0,fxx​fyy​−fxy2​<0.​
(37)
When the determinant vanishes, a saddle is still possible but is not determined by second derivatives alone: x4−y4 is a simple example. Thus these tests are decisive for nondegenerate stationary points, not an exhaustive classification from first and second derivatives of every smooth function.

The stationarity equations are 4x3−16y=0 and 4y3−16x=0. Thus y=x3/4 and x=y3/4, so x(x8−256)=0. The real solutions are (0,0), (2,2) and (−2,−2). The Hessian matrix is
H=(12x2−16​−1612y2​).
(38)
At the origin its determinant is −256, giving a saddle. At either nonzero stationary point it has eigenvalues 32,64, so both are strict minima, with value −32:
(0,0):saddle,f=0;(2,2),(−2,−2):minima,f=−32.​
(39)
They are also global minima, because f=(x2−y2)2+2(xy−4)2−32≥−32, with equality exactly at those two points. This is the special coefficient choice in a quartic double well with bilinear coupling.

The contours have exchange symmetry f(x,y)=f(y,x) and inversion symmetry f(−x,−y)=f(x,y). Negative levels between −32 and zero form separate loops around the two minima. The zero contour meets itself at the origin, reflecting the saddle there; positive levels form a single outer closed contour. At large radius the positive quartic terms dominate, so the contours remain bounded. There are no contours below −32.
The diagram marks the two minima and central saddle. The restrictions are classified separately below: a minimum or maximum along a single line need not be a two-dimensional extremum.
