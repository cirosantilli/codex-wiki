Convex hull (conv(S))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Convex set
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 8
The convex hull of a set consists of every finite convex combination of its points. It collects all choices obtainable by mixing the original choices with nonnegative weights summing to one. For S in a real vector space,
conv(S)={∑i=1m​λi​si​:si​∈S, m≥1, λi​≥0, ∑i=1m​λi​=1}.
(24)
Equivalently this is the smallest convex set containing S. Mixing two already mixed points produces another finite convex combination, so the displayed set is convex; every convex set containing S must contain all these mixtures.[ref]
In an original planar example, take four points A=(0,0), B=(4,0), C=(3,3), D=(0,2) and four additional samples (1,1), (2,1.7), (3,0.5), (1,0.5). Their convex hull is the filled convex polygon ABCD, including its boundary. None of the samples is p=(2,1), but
p=125​A+41​B+31​C.
(25)
The coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one, providing an explicit membership certificate. Geometrically, first form U=(3/2,0)=85​A+83​B on the line segment AB, then mix p=32​U+31​C. The additional interior samples do not enlarge the convex hull.
For a finite nonempty S⊂Rd, the convex hull is the image of a probability simplex of weights under the linear map λ↦∑i​λi​si​. The probability simplex is a compact set, and the map is continuous, so this convex hull is compact and closed. A one-point input stays one point; a collinear input stays in a line segment. Filling the convex hull does not necessarily produce a full-dimensional region.
The construction is useful whenever mixtures preserve the intended meaning of a choice. Mixing resource-allocation vectors, for example, interpolates their coordinate requirements. The convex hull describes the resulting feasible region. A coordinate-wise box can admit combinations that no mixture produces, as the outside-point certificate in linear optimization on a convex hull shows.
