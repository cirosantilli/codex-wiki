The closed 1s2 and 2s2 subshells contribute zero total orbital angular momentum and spin, so only the two 2p electrons matter. The first of Hund's rules maximizes their total spin, giving S=1. The Pauli exclusion principle then requires an antisymmetric spatial wavefunction. Two parallel-spin electrons cannot occupy the same ml​ orbital: the greatest possible orbital projection is 1+0=1, so the maximal allowed L within this triplet is L=1.
The ground term is therefore 3P, in term symbol notation 2S+1LJ​. Since the p2 subshell contains fewer than half of its six possible electrons, the third of Hund's rules places the smallest J lowest. Addition of angular momentum gives J=0,1,2, in that energy order within the ground term. Thus
ground level: 1s22s22p23P0​(L=1,S=1,J=0),​
(1)
first two excited levels: 3P1​ (J=1),3P2​ (J=2).​
(2)
These are the fine structure partners of the same electronic term, with magnetic multiplicities 1,3,5, rather than different principal-shell excitations. The ordering agrees with the tabulated lowest neutral-carbon levels.[ref]

Let A21​ be the spontaneous emission coefficient, and let B12​ρ and B21​ρ be the absorption and stimulated emission rates per atom under the radiation spectral energy density ρ at resonance. Because the two levels are nondegenerate, the Einstein coefficients satisfy B12​=B21​=B.
The radiative balance in a nondegenerate two-level atom is
dtdN2​​=N1​Bρ−N2​Bρ−N2​A21​=0.
(3)
The total spontaneous and stimulated emission rates are Rsp​=N2​A21​ and Rst​=N2​Bρ. Dividing the balance equation by N2​Bρ gives
Rst​Rsp​​=BρA21​​=N2​N1​−N2​​=N2​N1​​−1.​
(4)
This presumes nonzero drive and upper-level population, and no other population-transfer mechanism. It does not require a thermal Boltzmann distribution. In this closed driven two-level system, spontaneous decay keeps N1​>N2​; arbitrarily intense driving approaches equal populations, rather than producing a population inversion.

Use the electronic weak-field Zeeman splitting model, neglecting hyperfine structure. The Landé g-factor is gg​=2 for 3S1/2​ and ge​=2/3 for 3P1/2​. The shift of an absorption frequency is therefore
δν=hμB​Bz​​(ge​me​−gg​mg​),mg​,me​=±21​.
(5)
Here the magnetic field is weak relative to spin-orbit coupling, so J remains a useful quantum number, but the spectral line widths are small enough to resolve its magnetic substates.
For linear polarization parallel to z, the optical electric field has only the field-axis π component. The electric-dipole selection rule is Δm=0. Its two allowed transitions are mg​=me​=±1/2, giving
E∥B:ν=ν0​±3h2μB​Bz​​.​
(6)
For propagation perpendicular to z with linear polarization also perpendicular to z, the optical electric field is an equal-amplitude combination of the two field-axis circular components. It drives σ± transitions with Δm=±1. These connect −1/2→+1/2 and +1/2→−1/2, giving
E⊥B:ν=ν0​±3h4μB​Bz​​.​
(7)
Thus each orientation selects two lines, with twice the separation in the perpendicular-polarization case. Neither pair has an unshifted central component. This is polarization-selective Zeeman splitting of an alkali D1 line, an anomalous Zeeman effect rather than a normal orbital triplet.

For a self-adjoint Hamiltonian operator bounded below with ground-state energy E0​, every admissible nonzero trial wavefunction has a Rayleigh quotient at least E0​. To see this explicitly in a complete orthonormal eigenbasis, write ∣ψ⟩=∑n​cn​∣n⟩ with H∣n⟩=En​∣n⟩. Then
⟨ψ∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣H∣ψ⟩​−E0​=∑n​∣cn​∣2∑n​∣cn​∣2(En​−E0​)​≥0,
(8)
so
E0​≤⟨ψ∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣H∣ψ⟩​.​
(9)
For continuous spectral components, the spectral theorem replaces the sum by an integral with the same nonnegative integrand. Equality requires the trial quantum state to lie entirely in the ground-state subspace. The variational method chooses a tractable family, evaluates the quotient and minimizes over its parameters. This gives an upper bound; enlarging the family can only improve that bound. The trial must belong to the operator domain for the operator expression above, or have finite energy in the corresponding quadratic-form version of the quantum variational principle.
For the oscillator, use the standard kinetic operator T=−ℏ2d2/(2mdx2): the original PDF omits the superscript 2 on the derivative in its numerator. Take a>0, and normalize the scaled exact ground state as
ψλ​(x)=λ​ϕ0​(λx),λ>0.
(10)
The factor λ​ is optional in a quotient but makes the calculation transparent. Changing variable to u=λx and differentiating the scaled wavefunction gives
⟨T⟩λ​=λ2T0​,⟨V⟩λ​=λ−2V0​,T0​=⟨ϕ0​∣T∣ϕ0​⟩,V0​=⟨ϕ0​∣ax2∣ϕ0​⟩.
(11)
At λ=1 this trial is the exact minimizing state, so its energy E(λ)=λ2T0​+λ−2V0​ has zero derivative there. Hence 2T0​−2V0​=0. Since E0​=T0​+V0​,
⟨T⟩=⟨V⟩=E0​/2.​
(12)
This is the quantum virial identity from scaling for a quadratic potential, and hence the oscillator form of the virial theorem.
For the inverse-power attraction, take a>0 and suppose, for contradiction, that an exact ground state exists with separately finite kinetic energy T0​>0 and potential mean U0​<0, admitting the same dilation variations. Homogeneity now gives
E(λ)=λ2T0​+λpU0​.
(13)
Stationarity at λ=1 would require 2T0​+pU0​=0. Its second derivative would then be
E′′(1)=2T0​+p(p−1)U0​=2(2−p)T0​.
(14)
For p>2 this is negative, contradicting a ground-state minimum. More strongly, one does not need a presumed exact state: choose any normalized smooth test function supported away from the singular origin. Its T and U are finite, and its scaled quotient satisfies
E(λ)=λ2T+λpU⟶−∞(λ→∞, p>2).​
(15)
The unregularized attraction therefore exhibits fall to the centre for a supercritical inverse-power potential and has no stable ground state.
There is a necessary qualification to the PDF's broader wording about “no bound state”. Under the usual finite-energy virial theorem assumptions, a putative negative-energy bound state would satisfy
E=T+U=(1−p2​)T>0,
(16)
which contradicts being below the continuum threshold at zero. Thus no negative-energy bound state satisfying those regularity assumptions is possible. But this singular differential expression needs a specified domain at x=0; separately divergent kinetic and potential means or boundary conditions not preserved by dilation invalidate that ordinary calculation. The domain assumptions in singular-potential virial arguments must therefore be stated: the scaling proof establishes absence of a semibounded ground-state problem, rather than a domain-independent theorem about every formal square-integrable solution. A short-distance regularization would be a different potential.

The single-electron isotropic quantum harmonic oscillator has energies
ϵnx​ny​nz​​=ℏω0​(nx​+ny​+nz​+23​).
(17)
Its lowest spatial orbital, denoted s, is unique, with energy 3ℏω0​/2. Including spin one-half gives two spin orbitals. Two identical electrons can occupy both only with opposite spin in the unique singlet state. Thus the two-electron ground state has energy 3ℏω0​ and multiplicity one.
The first excited spatial shell consists of px​,py​,pz​, each of energy 5ℏω0​/2, giving six spin orbitals. A first-excited two-electron Slater determinant occupies one of the two ground-shell spin orbitals and one of these six excited-shell spin orbitals. Hence
Eground​=3ℏω0​,dground​=1;Efirst​=4ℏω0​,dfirst​=2×6=12.​
(18)
No further factor of two is inserted for interchanging the electrons, since the fermionic exchange symmetry is already built into a Slater determinant.
The spatial Hamiltonian operator is invariant under simultaneous rotations of both positions, so [H0​,Lk​]=0 for each component of the total orbital angular momentum. It is also spin independent, so [H0​,Sk​]=0 for each total spin component. Therefore
[H0​,L2]=[H0​,S2]=0,​
(19)
expressing conservation of angular momentum and conservation of the total spin magnitude for this model. The Pauli exclusion principle restricts which states occur but does not break either symmetry.
To change the first-shell basis, take normalized real Cartesian orbitals ϕx​,ϕy​,ϕz​. Using spherical coordinates, z=rcosθ and x±iy=rsinθe±iφ. Comparison with the spherical harmonics gives the Cartesian and spherical bases of the first oscillator shell:
ϕ+1​=−2​ϕx​+iϕy​​,ϕ0​=ϕz​,ϕ−1​=2​ϕx​−iϕy​​.​
(20)
Each is a common radial factor Rp​(r) times Y1m​(θ,φ), so l=1 and m=1,0,−1. Their normalizations and mutual orthogonality follow because this is a unitary change of basis. The ground spatial orbital has l=0.
For two ground-shell electrons, addition of angular momentum gives L=0, and their symmetric spatial state requires S=0. For one electron in each shell, 0⊗1 gives only L=1. For each m=−1,0,1, form the normalized spatial combinations
Φm±​(1,2)=2​s(1)ϕm​(2)±ϕm​(1)s(2)​.
(21)
The exchange symmetry of two identical spin-one-half fermions permits Φm+​ with the singlet state χ00​ and Φm−​ with each triplet state χ1M​. In term symbol notation the manifolds are therefore
energy3ℏω0​4ℏω0​4ℏω0​​(L,S)(0,0)(1,0)(1,1)​term1S1P3P​multiplicity139​​​
(22)
The count (2L+1)(2S+1) reproduces 1 and 3+9=12, as required. Both exchange symmetries are possible for the distinct occupied orbitals; dropping either spin sector would undercount the two-electron states in the first two shells of an isotropic oscillator.
For the specified spin-orbit interaction, S and L are the total two-electron operators. Set J=L+S. The spin-dot-product eigenvalue identity gives
ΔELSJ​=2λℏ2​[J(J+1)−L(L+1)−S(S+1)].
(23)
For the L=1,S=0 singlet, J=1 and the shift is zero. For the L=S=1 triplet, J=0,1,2. The spin-orbit splitting of the first excited two-electron oscillator level is consequently
level3P0​3P1​1P1​3P2​​ΔE−2λℏ2−λℏ20+λℏ2​multiplicity1335​​​
(24)
Add each shift to 4ℏω0​. The multiplicities sum to twelve and their weighted shift sums to zero. For positive λ the table is ordered in energy; for negative λ the ordering reverses. Weak coupling means ∣λ∣ℏ2≪ℏω0​. Since this total-spin interaction commutes with H0​, L2 and S2, the shifts within this invariant manifold are in fact exact for the specified model, although only first order was requested.

Expand the time-dependent quantum state in unperturbed energy eigenstates, factoring out free dynamical phases:
∣ψ(t)⟩=∑n​cn​(t)e−iEn​t/ℏ∣n⟩.
(25)
The Schrödinger equation gives the exact interaction picture coefficient equations
iℏc˙j​(t)=∑n​ei(Ej​−En​)t/ℏ⟨j∣ΔV(t)∣n⟩cn​(t).
(26)
Initially cn​(−∞)=δni​. To first order in time-dependent perturbation theory, replace the coefficients on the right by these zeroth-order values and integrate. Thus
Sji​=δji​+γji(1)​+O(ΔV2),γji(1)​=iℏ1​∫−∞∞​eiωji​t⟨j∣ΔV(t)∣i⟩dt.​
(27)
For a transition j=i, this is the desired leading probability amplitude, and Pji​=∣γji(1)​∣2 to leading order. The formula is understood with free asymptotic phases removed. For j=i, the full survival amplitude also contains the zeroth-order 1; the displayed integral alone is its first-order correction, not the whole amplitude. This specifies the usual scattering operator meaning of the paper's notation at infinite times.
For the ion pulse, write xji​=⟨j∣x∣i⟩ and yji​=⟨j∣y∣i⟩. Then
γji(1)​=iℏZe2​(xji​Ix​+yji​Iy​),
(28)
Ix​=∫−∞∞​(b2+v2t2)3/2beiωji​t​dt,Iy​=∫−∞∞​(b2+v2t2)3/2vteiωji​t​dt.
(29)
Consider an excitation with ωji​>0 and put β=ωji​b/v, u=ωji​t. The first real pulse is even in time and the second is odd. Their respective surviving Fourier components are therefore cosine and imaginary sine:
Ix​=v32bωji2​​∫0∞​(β2+u2)3/2cosu​du≃v3β22bωji2​​=bv2​,
(30)
Iy​=v22iωji​​∫0∞​(β2+u2)3/2usinu​du≃bv2i​β(c−logβ).
(31)
Since βlog(1/β)→0, the longitudinal term is smaller for comparable position matrix elements. Hence the electric-dipole excitation by a passing ion has
γji(1)​≃iℏbv2Ze2​xji​,Pji​≃(ℏbv2Ze2​)2∣xji​∣2.​
(32)
This leading limit extends to either sign of ωji​ using ∣ωji​∣b/v≪1. If xji​=0 by a selection rule, the retained leading term vanishes and the smaller longitudinal term may give the first nonzero probability. The conditions also include a small total transition probability, approximately straight constant-velocity motion, and b large compared with atomic size for the electric-dipole approximation. The paper uses a Coulomb-coupling convention in which the factor is written Ze2; in SI units replace e2 throughout by e2/(4πϵ0​).
The collision duration is of order b/v. Excitations with angular frequency ωji​ receive an unsuppressed impulse when this is short relative to 1/ωji​. In contrast, for ωji​b/v≫1, cancellations in the Fourier transform suppress the transition: a slowly varying distant field approximately preserves the atomic energy eigenstate. The exact dipole-pulse transforms make this explicit:
Ix​=bv2βK1​(β)​,Iy​=bv2iβK0​(β)​(ωji​>0),
(33)
where Kν​ is a Modified Bessel function of the second kind. Their large-argument exponential decrease explains the slow-passage cutoff. These transforms are optional refinements of the small-β derivation above, not a separate assumption about the target.
To connect the microscopic transition probability to energy loss, suppose the atom begins in a low-energy state. Exciting bound electronic states and causing ionization transfer positive energies Ej​−Ei​ from the ion to the material. For target atomic number density nat​ and path length d, a schematic independent-encounter sum is
δE≃nat​d∑j​(Ej​−Ei​)∫2πbdbPji​(b,v),
(34)
with a continuum integral as well as a sum for ionized final states. The short-collision formula gives Pji​∝Z2/(b2v2), so the impact-parameter integral has logarithmic dependence on its cutoffs. Its upper effective cutoff is of order v/ωji​, where the transition ceases to be sudden; the lower cutoff must exclude regions where the dipole or weak-collision approximation fails. Thus the leading high-speed charge and isotope scaling of electronic stopping is
δE∝v2Z2​×a slowly varying excitation factor.​
(35)
Although a faster ion can access more energetic transitions and a wider range of distant encounters, its shorter interaction time reduces each ordinary excitation probability. In the fast-ion regime shown, the 1/v2 decrease dominates the slower variation of the excitation factor. The energy loss consequently falls with increasing incident kinetic energy. The original figure has δE increasing downward, so the curves' rise toward the top of the page means decreasing loss, not increasing loss. A low-speed extrapolation of the inverse-square law is invalid because excitation thresholds, slow-passage suppression, changing ion charge and nuclear recoil can then matter.
For nonrelativistic ion mass M, E=Mv2/2. At fixed incident energy the leading prediction becomes
δE∝EZ2M​up to the slower excitation dependence.​
(36)
The greater atomic number of nitrogen than carbon than boron therefore gives larger loss; at the same velocity the leading species factor is Z2. Within an isotopic family, the heavier ion moves more slowly at the same E and loses more energy. Equivalently, a heavier isotope needs proportionally greater kinetic energy to achieve the same velocity and approximately the same electronic loss. Comparing at fixed energy per nucleon, rather than fixed total E, approximately removes this leading isotope mass dependence. This accounts for the separate isotope bands in the original graph without assigning different electronic excitation spectra to isotopes of the same projectile element.
The charge in this scaling is the effective ion charge if screening by bound projectile electrons is important. The interpretation assumes the same traversed material and thickness and uses the paper's small-fractional-loss regime, δE/E≪1; points with loss comparable to incident energy cannot be described quantitatively by treating v as constant. The plotted curves support the qualitative ordering and high-energy decrease, rather than determining a universal normalization from the supplied single-atom model.
