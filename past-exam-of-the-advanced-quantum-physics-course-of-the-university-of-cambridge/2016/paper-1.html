The open shell contains one f electron, so L=3 and S=1/2. For a less-than-half-filled shell, Hund's rules select the smaller total angular momentum, J=L−S=5/2, giving the free-ion ground level 2F5/2​. The Landé g-factor is
gJ​=23​+2(5/2)(7/2)(1/2)(3/2)−3(4)​=76​​.
(1)
In a magnetic field weak compared with the spin-orbit splitting, the electronic Zeeman shifts are δEmJ​​=gJ​μB​BmJ​, for mJ​=−5/2,−3/2,…,5/2. Thus six equally spaced magnetic substates appear. At B=1T, the adjacent-level spacing is
ΔEadjacent​=76​μB​B=7.95×10−24J=4.96×10−5eV.​
(2)
The complete lowest-to-highest multiplet spread is five spacings,
ΔEfull​=5ΔEadjacent​=3.97×10−23J=2.48×10−4eV.​
(3)
Giving both quantities removes the ambiguity in the word splitting. This is the free-ion, weak-field result; no crystal-field doublet or hyperfine structure is specified.

Hermiticity supplies the reverse matrix elements, also equal to the real number t. In an orthonormal basis for the degenerate eigenspace, the perturbation is
H1​=t​011​101​110​​=t(J3​−I),

The first-order energies are therefore
E0​+2tonce,E0​−ttwice.​

Using a spherical Bohr-radius volume for the atom, the calculations below give a cutoff electromagnetic vacuum energy density of about 6.2×1015Jm−3, compared with a hydrogen binding-energy density of about 3.5×1012Jm−3. The cutoff vacuum estimate is about 1.8×103 times larger. Alternative reasonable definitions of atomic volume change this ratio by a factor of a few, not the main order-of-magnitude conclusion.
The cutoff result is a bare zero-point mode sum. It is not a classical radiation bath incident on the atom, nor a calculation of an observable absolute vacuum energy without a subtraction or renormalization prescription.

The WKB approximation describes a wave whose local wavelength changes slowly compared with one wavelength. For the one-dimensional stationary Schrödinger equation, put p(x)=2m[E−V(x)]​. In a classically allowed region,
ψ(x)≃p(x)​C+​​exp[ℏi​∫xp(s)ds]+p(x)​C−​​exp[−ℏi​∫xp(s)ds].
(10)
The phase is the classical action divided by ℏ. The amplitude varies as p−1/2 so that a single travelling component has constant probability current. A useful local validity condition is ∣ℏp′/p2∣≪1.
For V>E, write κ=2m(V−E)​/ℏ. The wave instead has growing and decaying components proportional to κ−1/2exp(±∫κdx). At a simple turning point, p vanishes and neither local WKB expression is valid. Linearizing the potential gives an Airy-function solution that connects the two sides and supplies the turning-point phase shift. It is not legitimate to carry the divergent WKB amplitude straight through the turning point.[ref]
A useful application is tunnelling through a smooth opaque barrier. With turning points x1​,x2​, the leading transmission probability is
T≃exp[−2∫x1​x2​​κ(x)dx].​
(11)
The exponent is twice the forbidden-region action in units of ℏ, because transmission is a probability rather than a wave amplitude. For an ideal rectangular barrier of height V0​>E and width d, the same leading exponential is exp[−2d2m(V0​−E)​/ℏ]; matching at the abrupt edges supplies an additional prefactor.
For a bound state between two smooth turning points, the related WKB quantization rule is ∫x1​x2​​pdx=(n+1/2)πℏ. In a harmonic potential the integral equals πE/ω, recovering En​=(n+1/2)ℏω. This exact agreement of energies for the harmonic oscillator does not make WKB wavefunctions uniformly exact near its turning points.

Stimulated emission adds a photon to an already occupied radiation mode; spontaneous emission can occur when that mode initially contains no photons. In the electric-dipole approximation the atom-field interaction is Hint​=−d⋅E. Quantizing the radiation field makes the emission matrix element into a mode with occupation n proportional to
⟨n+1∣a†∣n⟩=n+1​.
(12)
This photon occupation enhancement of emission makes the emission rate into that mode proportional to n+1: the part proportional to n is stimulated and the remaining part is spontaneous. Absorption is proportional to ∣⟨n−1∣a∣n⟩∣2=n. The photon energy is the atomic energy difference, up to any specified recoil or line broadening.
The stimulated contribution preferentially populates the stimulating mode, including its propagation direction and polarization; it provides optical gain. Spontaneous photons populate the available modes according to the transition's dipole matrix elements and radiation environment. They are not universally isotropic. An excited bare atom with the field vacuum is not an eigenstate of the fully coupled Hamiltonian, which resolves the apparent paradox of a nominally stationary excited state radiating.[ref]
For lower and upper levels 1 and 2, with radiation spectral energy density uν​ per unit ordinary frequency, the rates are
Rabs​=N1​B12​uν​,Rstim​=N2​B21​uν​,Rsp​=N2​A21​.
(13)
Thermal detailed balance with the Planck spectrum gives
g1​B12​=g2​B21​,B21​A21​​=c38πhν3​.​
(14)
The statistical weights gi​ here are level degeneracies, not Landé g-factors. A definition of B multiplying intensity or angular-frequency density would change the normalization factors.
For a specified free-space electric-dipole transition, summing radiation modes gives
A21​=3πϵ0​ℏc3ω213​∣d12​∣2​,ω21​=2πν.
(15)
Appropriate sums over final magnetic substates and averages over initial substates are needed for an unresolved degenerate level. The radiative lifetime is 1/A21​ only when this is the sole decay channel. In a laser, net stimulated gain requires N2​/g2​>N1​/g1​, as well as sufficient gain to overcome cavity loss; population inversion alone does not specify the lasing threshold.

Fermi's golden rule converts a weak transition matrix element and the available density of final states into a transition rate. For a static perturbation W and a continuum,
Γi→all​=ℏ2π​∫∣⟨f∣W∣i⟩∣2δ(Ef​−Ei​)dNf​.​
(16)
When the matrix element is nearly constant on the energy shell, this becomes (2π/ℏ)∣Wfi​∣2ρ(Ei​). The energy delta function is to be integrated, rather than interpreted as a finite rate to one exactly resonant discrete level. The observation time must resolve a smooth final-state continuum while remaining short enough that first-order depletion is small. A periodically driven perturbation instead gives the corresponding absorption and emission energy shells.
For elastic scattering from a weak localized potential W(r), use box-normalized free states ⟨r∣k⟩=V−1/2eik⋅r. Then
Wfi​=V1​W(q),W(q)=∫e−iq⋅rW(r)d3r,q=kf​−ki​.
(17)
The number of final states is Vkf2​dkf​dΩ/(2π)3. With Ef​=ℏ2kf2​/(2m), the density per energy and solid angle is Vmkf​/[(2π)3ℏ2]. Dividing the resulting golden-rule rate by the incident single-particle flux ji​=ℏki​/(mV) gives
dΩdσ​=4π2ℏ4m2​ki​kf​​∣W(q)∣2.
(18)
This is the golden-rule derivation of the Born cross-section. For the elastic problem kf​=ki​, so the first Born scattering amplitude and cross-section are
fB​(kf​,ki​)=−2πℏ2m​W(q),dΩdσ​=∣fB​∣2.​
(19)
The box volume cancels, as a physical cross-section must. The minus sign is fixed by the outgoing-wave scattering convention; the golden-rule rate alone determines only the modulus.
For example, W(r)=ge−κr/r has W=4πg/(q2+κ2), hence fB​=−2mg/[ℏ2(q2+κ2)], with q=2ksin(θ/2). Replacing the exact scattering state by the incident plane wave is the essential Born approximation. It can fail for strong scattering, low-energy resonances and situations requiring repeated scattering; a small perturbation and a suitable continuum are separate conditions.[ref]

Spin and spatial exchange symmetry. Write the normalized spin singlet and triplet states as
χ00​=2​∣↑↓⟩−∣↓↑⟩​,χ11​=∣↑↑⟩,χ10​=2​∣↑↓⟩+∣↓↑⟩​,χ1,−1​=∣↓↓⟩.
(20)
The singlet is antisymmetric under exchange and has total spin zero; the triplet is symmetric and has total spin one. For orthogonal orbitals define
C=2​∣ab⟩+∣ba⟩​,A=2​∣ab⟩−∣ba⟩​.
(21)
Identical spin one-half particles are fermions, so the complete state must be antisymmetric. Thus Cχ00​ is allowed, and Aχ1M​ is allowed for each triplet component. The spatially symmetric same-orbital states ∣aa⟩,∣bb⟩ also require the singlet. A same-orbital triplet vanishes by the Pauli exclusion principle.
These products give a spin-adapted basis. More generally the physical state can be written
∣Ψ⟩=(ca​∣aa⟩+cb​∣bb⟩+cC​C)χ00​+A∑M=−11​dM​χ1M​.
(22)
A superposition of the singlet and triplet sectors need not factor into one spatial function times one spin function. Factorization holds for a definite-spin sector, as used in the subsequent model. If the original normalized orbitals are not orthogonal, the corresponding ab±ba normalization is 2(1±∣⟨a∣b⟩∣2)​; the remainder of this calculation uses the stipulated orthogonal orbitals.
Integral matrix elements and positivity. Let a(r) and b(r) denote the localized spatial orbitals. With fixed proton positions, the one-electron electronic operator is
h=−2me​ℏ2​∇2−4πϵ0​e2​(∣r−Ra​∣1​+∣r−Rb​∣1​).
(23)
The proton-proton repulsion is a separate common nuclear energy. The requested one-electron and direct two-electron elements are
⟨a∣h∣a⟩=∫a∗(r)ha(r)d3r,
(24)
Jab​=⟨ab∣H1,2​∣ab⟩=4πϵ0​e2​∬∣r1​−r2​∣∣a(r1​)∣2∣b(r2​)∣2​d3r1​d3r2​.
(25)
Since H1​=h⊗I,
⟨aa∣H1​∣ab⟩=⟨a∣h∣a⟩⟨a∣b⟩=0.​
(26)
Likewise,
U=4πϵ0​e2​∬∣r1​−r2​∣∣a(r1​)∣2∣a(r2​)∣2​d3r1​d3r2​>0.​
(27)
The integrand is nonnegative and is positive on a set of nonzero measure. This is the repulsion cost of double occupancy; the coincidence singularity is integrable for ordinary smooth localized orbitals.
One electron on each center. Suppress the normalized singlet spin factor and use ∣ψ⟩=C. For each of the spatial products ab and ba, the one-body sum has expectation 2E0​. The cross terms vanish because a one-body hop leaves an orthogonal spectator orbital. The interaction contribution is the direct integral plus the exchange integral, both neglected in the model. Consequently
Eψ​=⟨C∣H∣C⟩=2E0​.​
(28)
This state has no same-center double occupancy, but remains an exchange-symmetric spatial superposition rather than assigning a distinguishable electron permanently to each proton.
Two electrons in the bonding orbital. The normalized one-electron bonding orbital is g=(a+b)/2​. The trial spatial state is ∣ϕ⟩=∣gg⟩, whose one-body expectation is
2⟨g∣h∣g⟩=2[2E0​+E0​+t+t​]=2E0​+2t.
(29)
Expanding the product gives ∣ϕ⟩=(∣aa⟩+∣ab⟩+∣ba⟩+∣bb⟩)/2. The total probability of double occupancy is therefore 1/2. Keeping only on-site Coulomb repulsion gives the intended interaction truncation,
H1,2​≃U(∣aa⟩⟨aa∣+∣bb⟩⟨bb∣),
(30)
and the interaction expectation is U/2. Hence
Eϕ​=2E0​+2t+2U​.​
(31)
This truncation must be stated. In particular, mixed Coulomb elements such as X=⟨aa∣H1,2​∣ab⟩ do not vanish merely by orbital orthogonality or inversion symmetry. The printed list does not explicitly name them. If they are retained and are equal and real by site symmetry, their contribution to Eϕ​ is 2X; the requested expression uses their neglect along with the other off-site interaction terms. No numerical value of X is supplied or invented.
Energy comparison and the bond. Put x=∣t∣/U with U>0 and t=−∣t∣. Then
UEϕ​−Eψ​​=21​−2x.​
(32)
The straight line starts at 1/2 and crosses zero at x=1/4. The purely bonding-orbital trial state is better for x>1/4, while the singly occupied covalent trial state is better for x<1/4.
For localized orbitals of approximately fixed atomic size, the hopping magnitude ∣t∣ decreases rapidly, typically exponentially times a polynomial, as the proton separation grows. The on-site integral U remains of atomic Coulomb scale and tends to the isolated-orbital value; it does not decay as the intersite repulsion 1/R. If the orbitals themselves are allowed to relax, U also changes, so no universal monotonic law for U(R) follows from the data.
At small ∣t∣/U, electron correlation suppresses the ionic configurations aa and bb, favoring a singlet with roughly one electron on each center. A single doubly occupied bonding orbital has an artificial finite ionic weight even as the centers separate. At large ∣t∣/U, delocalization gains enough kinetic energy that the bonding-orbital description becomes useful and charge fluctuations are substantial.
The crossing of two restricted trial energies is not a sharp transition of the actual two-site ground state. To see this without adding empirical data, include both C and the symmetric ionic state I+​=(∣aa⟩+∣bb⟩)/2​. This two-site Hubbard dimer reduces to
H−2E0​I=(02t​2tU​)C,I+​​,Eg​=2E0​+2U−U2+16t2​​.​
(33)
Its ground state is cosθC+sinθI+​, with tan2θ=4∣t∣/U and 0≤θ≤π/4. The ionic probability is
Pdouble​=21​(1−U2+16t2​U​).
(34)
It changes smoothly from zero to 1/2. At large separation, the electronic lowering is −4t2/U to leading order and vanishes as hopping vanishes. An absolute molecular binding curve would additionally require E0​(R), the nuclear repulsion and the neglected intersite terms; this simplified comparison alone does not determine an equilibrium bond length.

Uniform system and dispersion. A plane wave reduces the differential Hamiltonian to a two-by-two matrix acting on its spinor:
H(k)=(Vℏc(kx​+iky​)​ℏc(kx​−iky​)−V​)=d(k)⋅σ,d=(ℏckx​,ℏcky​,V).
(35)
The Pauli anticommutation relations imply H(k)2=∣d∣2I. Choosing a spinor in one of its eigenspaces therefore gives
E±​(k)=±ℏ2c2k2+V2​,k=∣k∣.​
(36)
An arbitrary constant spinor multiplying a plane wave is not necessarily an energy eigenstate; it must be an eigenvector of this matrix. For example, for the upper branch and ε=∣d∣, one can use
u+​(k)=2ε(ε+V)​1​(ε+Vℏc(kx​+iky​)​),
(37)
away from the coordinate singularity ε+V=0, where another normalized eigenvector chart is used.
For the usual c>0 convention, the massless branches are E±​=±ℏck. For nonzero V, the two branches have a gap 2∣V∣ and are quadratic near k=0:
E±​≃±∣V∣±2∣V∣ℏ2c2k2​.
(38)
Changing the sign of V does not change these energies. It reverses the out-of-plane spin polarization of a given band at the origin. The plots for equal positive and negative mass magnitudes therefore have identical dispersion curves, despite their different spinors.
Spin direction in the massless case. For k>0 put kx​+iky​=keiφ. The normalized eigenspinors can be chosen as
us​(k)=2​1​(1seiφ​),Es​=sℏck,s=±1.
(39)
Direct multiplication gives ⟨σx​⟩=scosφ, ⟨σy​⟩=ssinφ and ⟨σz​⟩=0. Since the Pauli matrices represent physical spin in this question,
⟨S⟩s​=s2ℏ​k(kx​,ky​,0)​.​
(40)
The spin is parallel to momentum on the positive-energy branch and antiparallel on the negative-energy branch. Thus the branch-independent statement is collinearity; the printed parallel claim needs the positive-branch qualification. At k=0,V=0, the Hamiltonian is zero and spin is not fixed by a wavevector direction.
Mass-domain-wall eigenstate. Assume c>0 for the stated normalizable wall state and write
F(y)=exp[−ℏc1​∫0y​V(s)ds],u−​=2​1​(1−1​).
(41)
Then F′=−VF/(ℏc), σx​u−​=−u−​, and (iσy​+σz​)u−​=0. Applying the full operator gives
H{eikx​xF(y)u−​}=eikx​xF(y){ℏckx​σx​+iV(y)σy​+V(y)σz​}u−​=−ℏckx​eikx​xF(y)u−​.
(42)
The transverse derivative and mass terms cancel pointwise. Hence the specified state is an eigenstate with E=−ℏckx​.
For a step mass V(y)=V+​>0 above the interface and V(y)=V−​<0 below it,
F(y)={e−y/ξ+​,ey/ξ−​,​y>0,y<0,​ξ+​=V+​ℏc​,ξ−​=∣V−​∣ℏc​.​
(43)
The Localization length at a step Dirac mass wall sets the two exponential tails. The amplitude is continuous, peaks at the interface and decays on both sides, with generally unequal decay lengths. The probability density decays with lengths ξ±​/2. A normalized transverse function is f(y)=2/(ξ+​+ξ−​)​F(y) when multiplying the unit spinor u−​. The longitudinal plane wave can independently be box-normalized. A step in V allows a step in F′; the first-order Dirac equation does not impose continuity of that derivative.
Propagation. The interface dispersion is the straight line
E(kx​)=−ℏckx​,vx​=ℏ1​dkx​dE​=−c.​
(44)
Every component on this branch travels in the negative x direction. A packet has the form
Ψ(x,y,t)=f(y)u−​∫A(kx​)eikx​(x+ct)dkx​=f(y)u−​g(x+ct).
(45)
This dispersionless chiral interface wavepacket therefore translates rigidly with speed c in that direction, without dispersion in this exactly linear model. It remains confined in y; there is no free transverse group velocity, because the bound-state family is not labelled by a transverse travelling-wave momentum.
The continuity equation gives the current components jx​=cΨ†σx​Ψ=−c∣Ψ∣2 and jy​=cΨ†σy​Ψ=0. This explicitly confirms absence of probability transport away from the interface. For the step-mass case the profile is normalizable because its tails have positive decay constants and c>0. A general sign-changing profile also requires F∈L2(R); nonzero asymptotic masses suffice, whereas signs alone do not guarantee confinement if their integrated magnitudes grow too slowly. If c<0, the supplied exponential instead grows at infinity; a normalizable state requires the opposite spinor and envelope sign. Reversing the wall orientation also reverses the propagation chirality.
