In a contractible region, the Maxwell equations ∇⋅B=0 and the Poincaré lemma imply the existence of a magnetic vector potential A such that B=∇×A. Faraday's law of induction then gives
∇×(E+∂t​A)=0.
(1)
The expression in parentheses is a gradient there, so a scalar electric potential ϕ can be chosen with
B=∇×A,E=−∇ϕ−∂t​A.​
(2)
These statements are local, or global when the domain has the required topology and the electromagnetic fields are regular. A nonzero flux through a topologically nontrivial closed surface can obstruct a single globally defined vector potential, even though local expressions remain valid. The electric potential and magnetic vector potential are not unique: A↦A+∇χ and ϕ↦ϕ−∂t​χ leave both electromagnetic fields unchanged.
For the Lorenz gauge condition ∇⋅A=−c−2∂t​ϕ, Gauss's law becomes
−∇2ϕ−∂t​(∇⋅A)=ϵ0​ρ​,(∇2−c21​∂t2​)ϕ=−ϵ0​ρ​.
(3)
The Ampere-Maxwell law gives
∇(∇⋅A)−∇2A=μ0​J−c21​∇∂t​ϕ−c21​∂t2​A.
(4)
Cancel the two gradient terms using the Lorenz gauge condition. Hence
(∇2−c21​∂t2​)ϕ=−ϵ0​ρ​,(∇2−c21​∂t2​)A=−μ0​J.​
(5)
Both potential equations have the same d'Alembert operator, with their respective charge density and current density sources. In a source-free region these are homogeneous wave equations with propagation speed c.

Let ℓ=2πr−d be the mean path length in the core. Neglect leakage and fringing across the narrow air gap, and assume a cross-section small compared with r. Continuity of normal magnetic flux density then gives the same tangential magnetic field magnitude B in the core and gap. Ampère's circuital law for the magnetic field strength H is
Hcore​ℓ+Hgap​d=nI.
(6)
For a linear core, Hcore​=B/(μ0​μr​) and Hgap​=B/μ0​, giving
Bcore​≃Bgap​≃d+ℓ/μr​μ0​nI​t.​
(7)
Here t follows the winding's right-hand rule. The gap has much larger H than the core when μr​≫1, although B is approximately continuous. Equivalently, the core and gap magnetic reluctances add in series.
On a magnetic hysteresis loop, remanence is the nonzero Br​ remaining at H=0 after magnetizing the sample. The normal magnetic coercivity HcB​ is the magnitude of reverse H at which B=0 on that branch. The intrinsic magnetic coercivity instead refers to zero magnetization; these two conventions should not be confused. A comparison of coercive H with remanent B requires a unit conversion or a specified constitutive scale, because their SI units differ. A soft ferromagnet is understood here to have a narrow loop and small coercive field.
Use the stated single-valued constitutive equation for the calculation. Inverting it gives Hcore​=Hm​artanh(B/Bm​), so
nI=ℓHm​artanh(B/Bm​)+μ0​d​B.
(8)
Since artanhz=z+O(z3), the small-signal response of a gapped magnetic core is
B≃κI,κ=ℓHm​/Bm​+d/μ0​n​=d+μ0​ℓHm​/Bm​μ0​n​.​
(9)
The small-field core magnetic permeability is Bm​/Hm​. Strictly, B=Bm​tanh(H/Hm​) is a reversible approximation: at H=0 it has B=0, so it cannot itself model nonzero remanence. The displayed loop and this constitutive approximation serve different purposes. Keeping a genuinely remanent branch would generally add a history-dependent offset; it would not imply B=κI through the origin.

Take d as the centre-to-centre separation and ϵ=ϵ0​ϵr​. Use the ideal lossless two-conductor transmission line model, with nonmagnetic surroundings and negligible internal-wire inductance. Opposite line charges +λq​ and −λq​ approximate the cylindrical conductors when d≫a. Integrating their electric fields, or adding their logarithmic electric potentials, gives the potential difference
V≃πϵλq​​lnad​,C′=Vλq​​≃ln(d/a)πϵ​.
(10)
This is the two-wire capacitance at large separation. For opposite electric currents +I and −I, the magnetic fields between the wires add. The linked magnetic flux per unit length is approximately
Φ′≃2πμ0​I​∫ad−a​(x1​+d−x1​)dx=πμ0​I​lnad−a​≃πμ0​I​lnad​.
(11)
Thus the external inductance of a thin two-wire line is L′≃μ0​ln(d/a)/π. The lossless telegrapher's equations give a forward wave with V/I=L′/C′​, so its characteristic impedance is
Z0​≃π1​ϵ0​ϵr​μ0​​​lnad​≃ϵr​​120Ω​lnad​.​
(12)
The product L′C′=μ0​ϵ also gives the propagation speed c/ϵr​​, an independent consistency check. The exact ideal external-field geometry replaces ln(d/a) by arcosh(d/(2a)). If the wire separation were specified between surfaces, its centre separation would be d+2a, with the same leading large-separation result. Finite electrical conductivity and internal-wire magnetic energy require a more detailed line model.

Snell's law is na​sinθa​=nb​sinθb​, with both angles measured from the interface normal vector. Let ψ be the meridional ray's angle to the optical axis inside the core and let n0​ be the incident medium's refractive index. At the entrance face,
n0​sinθ=ninner​sinψ.
(13)
At the cylindrical wall the angle of incidence is i=π/2−ψ. For ninner​>nouter​, the critical angle for total internal reflection satisfies sinic​=nouter​/ninner​. At the limiting accepted ray,
sinψmax​=cosic​=1−ninner2​nouter2​​​.
(14)
Consequently the optical fiber acceptance angle obeys
n0​sinθmax​=ninner2​−nouter2​​.​
(15)
The requested expression follows for incidence from air, n0​≃1, provided the right-hand side is at most one. Strict total internal reflection occurs inside this limiting cone. The derivation uses the standard meridional ray model; skew launches need additional geometry.
If ninner2​−nouter2​​>n0​, every forward entrance angle allowed by this ideal model meets the side-wall condition, and the limiting half-angle is π/2, rather than an undefined inverse sine. For example, ninner​=1.6, nouter​=1.1, n0​=1 gives 1.35​>1. Thus the unsaturated printed formula is not valid for every possible pair of refractive indices.

Use the thin lens sign convention in which a real object on the incident side has positive object distance, a real image on the outgoing side has positive image distance, and a converging lens has positive focal length. The first image distance is
v1​=d1​−f1​f1​d1​​.
(16)
For the image distance for two separated thin lenses, the second lens has object distance u2​=L−v1​, which can be negative when its intermediate object is virtual. Applying the thin lens equation again gives
d2​=u2​−f2​f2​u2​​=(L−f1​−f2​)d1​+f1​f2​−Lf1​f2​[(L−f1​)d1​−Lf1​]​.​
(17)
The image is d2​ beyond the second lens when d2​>0; d2​<0 denotes a virtual image on its incident side. A vanishing denominator with nonzero numerator denotes an image at infinity. The rational expression also supplies appropriate limits when the first image is at infinity; for example, d1​=f1​ gives d2​=f2​ for finite nonzero focal lengths.
As L→0, the effective focal length is F=f1​f2​/(f1​+f2​) and
d2​=d1​−FFd1​​.​
(18)
For a real incident object, d1​>0, and positive F, the real-image condition is exactly d1​>F, yielding the requested inequality. This includes two converging lenses. The positivity assumption matters: with f1​=f2​=−1m and d1​=1m, one has F=−0.5m and d1​>F, but d2​=−1/3m is virtual. If f1​+f2​=0, the touching pair has zero net optical power and produces no real image of this real object in the ideal thin lens model.

Put the plate behind the slit at transverse coordinate +d/2, and let positive screen coordinate x point toward that slit. The extra optical path length is
Δ=(n1​−1)t=0.52(200μm)=104μm.
(19)
In the paraxial approximation, the covered path minus the uncovered path has total optical path difference Δ−dx/L. Its zero determines the shifted central interference fringe:
x0​=dL(n1​−1)t​=1.04×10−2m=10.4mm,​
(20)
toward the slit covered by the plate. Moving toward that slit shortens its geometric path and compensates the extra delay in the plate.
More generally, the phase difference is (2π/λ)(Δ−dx/L), so every interference fringe translates by the same x0​ in this approximation. The spacing λL/d is unchanged, and the shift corresponds to Δ/λ fringe periods. For an ideal uniform transparent plate that changes only the wave phase, the envelope and interferometric visibility are unchanged. Reflection losses or unequal transmission through a real plate could additionally reduce the interferometric visibility; those are separate amplitude effects. No numerical wavelength is needed for the physical displacement of the zero-order fringe.

Write μ=μ0​μr​ and ϵ=ϵ0​ϵr​ for homogeneous scalar material constants, and use J=σE. Taking the curl of Faraday's law of induction and substituting the Ampere-Maxwell law gives
∇×(∇×E)=−μ∂t​(σE+ϵ∂t​E).
(21)
The vector calculus identity for a double curl therefore yields
∇2E−∇(∇⋅E)=μϵ∂t2​E+μσ∂t​E.​
(22)
The second term represents electric current response, rather than displacement current. A dispersive σ(ω) is used in the frequency domain; its general time-domain counterpart is a temporal response convolution.
For the convention ei(k⋅r−ωt), the plane wave equation is
[kkT+(μϵω2+iμσω−k2)I]E0​=0.
(23)
Decompose the electric field into components parallel and perpendicular to k. With S=μϵω2+iμσω, the longitudinal eigenvalue is S and each transverse eigenvalue is S−k2; thus the determinant is S(S−k2)2. For a transverse electromagnetic wave, k⋅E0​=0 and
k2=c2ω2​+iμ0​σω​
(24)
when μr​=1 and the background ϵr​≃1. A real dissipative electrical conductivity generally makes k complex, with the decaying branch chosen for a passive medium.
For a cold, collisionless, unmagnetized plasma, treat ions as stationary and write e>0 for the magnitude of the elementary charge. The electron equation of motion is −iωme​v=−eE, so
v=−me​ωie​E,J=−Ne​ev=σ(ω)E,σ(ω)=me​ωiNe​e2​.​
(25)
This reactive response includes no collision-induced Joule heating. Substitution into the transverse dispersion relation gives
k2c2=ω2−ωp2​,ωp​=me​ϵ0​Ne​e2​​,n=ωck​=1−ω2ωp2​​​.​
(26)
The quantity ωp​ is the electron plasma frequency. The refractive index is real above this cutoff; below it, the wave is evanescent. The electron response is represented here by σ(ω), so it must not also be counted a second time in the background permittivity.
For a nonzero longitudinal component, taking the dot product of the matrix equation with k instead gives S(k⋅E0​)=0. The same cold plasma response makes S=(ω2−ωp2​)/c2, hence
ω2=ωp2​,ω=ωp​for positive frequency.​
(27)
At nonzero k, the remaining equation forces E0​ to be parallel to k, and Faraday's law of induction gives B0​=0. These are electrostatic Langmuir waves, with no k dependence of angular frequency in the zero-temperature model. Equilibrium neutrality does not forbid their perturbed charge density; Gauss's law requires such a density when k⋅E0​=0.

Let ρq​=3q/(4πa3) be the uniform charge density and take ω=ωz. Rigid rotation produces current density J=ρq​ω×r. The magnetic dipole moment is
ms​=21​∫r×Jd3r=2ρq​​∫[r2ω−r(r⋅ω)]d3r.
(28)
Spherical symmetry gives ∫ri​rj​d3r=δij​∫r2d3r/3, while ∫r2d3r=4πa5/5. Therefore
ms​=5qa2​ω.​
(29)
It is parallel to the angular velocity for positive q and antiparallel for negative q. The fully signed magnitude statement is ∣ms​∣=∣q∣∣ω∣a2/5; the printed magnitude assumes positive q and positive angular speed.
Axisymmetry permits a purely azimuthal magnetic vector potential, A=f(r)sinθφ​, with Ar​=Aθ​=0. For the source's associated Legendre function convention, P11​(cosθ)=+sinθ, since its definition omits the Condon–Shortley phase. The given differential operator reduces to
F[f(r)sinθ]=(f′′+r2​f′−r22f​)sinθ.
(30)
Inside the sphere, Jφ​=ρq​ωrsinθ. Since the radial operator maps r3 to 10r, the particular coefficient is α0​=−μ0​ρq​ω/10. Regularity at the origin and decay at infinity give
fin​=Ar−10μ0​ρq​ω​r3,fout​=r2C​.
(31)
There is no surface sheet electric current, so both normal and tangential magnetic field components are continuous at r=a. Using
Br​=r2f(r)​cosθ,Bθ​=−rf(r)+rf′(r)​sinθ,
(32)
this is equivalent to continuity of f and f′. The two equations give A=μ0​ρq​ωa2/6 and C=μ0​ρq​ωa5/15. Thus
Ain​=30μ0​ρq​​(5a2−3r2)(ω×r),r<a,​
(33)
Aout​=4πμ0​​r3ms​×r​,r>a.​
(34)
In the homogeneous expansion, α1​=A, β1​=C in their respective interior and exterior regions; all higher angular orders vanish. Indeed, matching the regular rℓ and decaying r−ℓ−1 terms with zero source at ℓ>1 forces both coefficients to zero. The outside magnetic field is exactly the magnetic dipole field of ms​, not merely its leading far-field approximation. For a positive moment along z, Aφ​=μ0​ms​sinθ/(4πr2), which is the printed cross-product magnitude expression.
For the flux coupling of a rotating charged sphere to a circular loop, take a loop of radius b>a in the equatorial plane and use Stokes theorem on the regular full vector potential, evaluated around the rim. Its linked magnetic flux is
Φb​=∮A⋅dℓ=2πb4πb2μ0​ms​​=2bμ0​ms​​.
(35)
With ms​=πa2I0​, the mutual inductance defined using this effective electric current is
M=I0​Φb​​=2bμ0​πa2​.​
(36)
Integrating an ideal magnetic dipole field through the entire disk would incorrectly use its singular expression inside the actual sphere; the rim calculation avoids that error.
For the prescribed time dependence, the quasistatic Faraday's law of induction result and the purely resistive loop approximation give
Ib​(t)=−R1​dtdΦb​​=−10bRμ0​qa2ω0​ν​cos(νt).​
(37)
Positive loop electric current is defined to produce a positive z magnetic dipole moment, so the minus sign is Lenz's law. This assumes negligible retardation and νLb​≪R, where Lb​ is the loop's self-inductance. If that is not negligible, the governing equation is instead Lb​I˙b​+RIb​=−Φ˙b​; R alone is insufficient for the exact dynamic response.

Let R=∣r∣2 be the single-mirror power reflectivity and θg​ the propagation angle inside the gas. Define λ as the vacuum wavelength. Successive transmitted complex amplitudes differ by a round-trip factor Reiδ, where, for real dielectric reflection coefficients whose product has positive sign,
δ=λ4πndcosθg​​.
(38)
The cosine arises by comparing successive emerging rays on the same outgoing wavefront; it is not the literal zigzag distance 2d/cosθg​. Equivalently, the round-trip phase is 2kz​d. If the source's θ is the angle in external air, Snell's law gives nsinθg​=sinθ and
δ=λ4πd​n2−sin2θ​.
(39)
If θ denotes the internal angle, simply set θg​=θ.
For identical lossless mirrors, their power transmission is 1−R. In an energy-normalized amplitude convention this is ∣t∣2=1−R; more general interface amplitudes require the corresponding wave impedance factors. The geometric series of transmitted amplitudes gives
I0​It​​=∣1−Reiδ∣2∣t∣4​=(1−R)2+4Rsin2(δ/2)(1−R)2​.
(40)
Hence
It​=1+Qsin2(δ/2)I0​​,Q=(1−R)24R​=(1−∣r∣2)24∣r∣2​.​
(41)
This is Fabry–Pérot transmission from multiple reflections.[ref]
At normal incidence, for nondispersive n and zero net reflection phase modulo 2π, the resonance condition is δ=2πm. The resonance frequencies and free spectral range are
νm​=2ndmc​,ΔνFSR​=2ndc​.​
(42)
For finite R<1 the resonances have finite width, and transmission away from them is nonzero. Thus “only discrete frequencies” is an ideal high-reflectivity description of the selected resonances, not an exact statement about the support of the transmitted spectrum. For example, R=0.9 at δ=π still gives It​/I0​=(0.1/1.9)2≃0.00277.
At half the unit peak, Qsin2(δ/2)=1. Measuring the phase displacement from a resonance yields
ΔδFWHM​=4arcsinQ​1​,ΔνFWHM​=π2ΔνFSR​​arcsin(2R​1−R​)≃2ndc​πR​1−R​.​
(43)
The last expression is the high-reflectivity approximation, with finesse πR​/(1−R). At R=0.9, the full phase width is about 0.2109 radians and ΔνFWHM​/ΔνFSR​≃0.03357. At R=0.1, the minimum transmission is 0.6694, so the trace never reaches half the peak and this full width at half maximum is not defined. The exact half-maximum condition requires R≥3−22​.
For the absorbing Fabry–Pérot mirror phase and transmission, write r=R​eiϕr​ and let T be the power transmission of one mirror. Optical absorption makes R+T<1. The transmitted intensity becomes
I0​It​​=(1−R)2T2​1+(1−R)24R​sin2(δtot​/2)1​,δtot​=c4πνnd​+2ϕr​.​
(44)
Here the phase formula is at normal incidence. The peak is reduced below unity, while a change in R changes the width. The complex reflection phase also moves the resonances: if ϕr​ is approximately constant,
νm​=2ndc​(m−πϕr​​).​
(45)
If either n or ϕr​ depends on frequency, this is an implicit condition and the free spectral range need not be constant. No numerical shift is determined without the mirrors' optical response.

Place the two slits at transverse coordinates +h/2 and −h/2. Let the source point have coordinate s and the observation point coordinate x, both measured in the same transverse direction. The total lower-slit path minus upper-slit path is
Δℓ(s,x)=≃​R2+(s+h/2)2​−R2+(s−h/2)2​+D2+(x+h/2)2​−D2+(x−h/2)2​h(Rs​+Dx​).​
(46)
The last line uses the paraxial approximation at both source and screen. The source assumptions give w,h≪R; the screen expression also assumes h,∣x∣≪D. Reversing the source-coordinate orientation would change the sign of s/R, without changing the uniform-source result.
At the slits, the differential path delay from a source element is hs/R. Across the full filament its range is hw/R. Keeping all these phases nearly equal therefore requires
Rhw​≪λ.​
(47)
This is a sufficient near-unit-spatial coherence condition, rather than a necessary condition for any observable interference. That distinction follows quantitatively below.
Different small elements of a thermal filament are mutually incoherent, so their wave intensities add. The two paths from one element are coherent. If that element supplies the same intensity I0​B(s)ds through each slit, the superposition principle gives
dIx​=​I0​B(s)ds​eiφ+​+I0​B(s)ds​eiφ−​​2=2I0​B(s)[1+cosδ(s,x)]ds,
(48)
where
δ(s,x)=λ2πh​(Dx​+Rs​).​
(49)
Integrating intensities over the incoherent source gives
Ix​=2I0​∫−w/2w/2​B(s)[1+cos(λD2πhx​+λR2πhs​)]ds.​
(50)
Adding complex amplitudes across different source elements instead would incorrectly assume mutual optical coherence of the entire filament.
For B(s)=1/w, define α=2πh/(λD) and β=πh/(λR). The source's signed quantity is
Vprinted​(x)=w1​∫−w/2w/2​cos(αx+2βs)ds=cos(αx)βwsin(βw)​.
(51)
Thus
α=λD2πh​,β=λRπh​,Ix​=2I0​[1+cos(αx)βwsin(βw)​].​
(52)
The continuous limit at βw=0 uses sinz/z→1.
The printed V is a signed local modulation, not the usual fringe visibility. From the maximum and minimum of this scanned fringe pattern, the interferometric visibility is
V=Imax​+Imin​Imax​−Imin​​=​βwsin(βw)​​.​
(53)
It is independent of the screen's fringe phase and is nonnegative. For a concrete distinction, take βw=π/2 and αx=π/2: the printed V is zero at that observation point, while the pattern has contrast V=2/π. The same example has hw/R=λ/2, which is not much smaller than λ, yet substantial interference survives. When the signed coherence factor is negative, bright and dark fringe positions interchange; the contrast remains its absolute value. This is the distinction between signed coherence and fringe visibility.
