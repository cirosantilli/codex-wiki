Bernstein polynomial (Bn​f)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Functional analysis Stone-Weierstrass theorem Weierstrass approximation theorem
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 13
For a function f:[0,1]→R and an integer n≥1, its Bernstein polynomial is
Bn​f(x)=∑k=0n​f(k/n)(kn​)xk(1−x)n−k=E[f(K/n)],K∼Bin(n,x).
(10)
The Bernstein basis supplies nonnegative weights whose sum is one. Equivalently, the weights are the probabilities of a binomial distribution, and the result is an expected value. At a given x, this averages the sampled values f(k/n) with weights concentrated near k/n=x.[ref]
The endpoints are exact: Bn​f(0)=f(0) and Bn​f(1)=f(1). Interior sampled points need not be interpolated. For example, binomial distribution moments give Bn​(x)=x and Bn​(x2)=x2+x(1−x)/n. Thus every affine function is reproduced exactly, whereas x2 is generally changed. Bernstein polynomial degree preservation is a statement about degree of a polynomial, not equality of the entire polynomial.
For a continuous f, the uniform continuity of f and the variance Var(K/n)=x(1−x)/n≤1/(4n) explain uniform convergence. Split the expected value of the error into ∣K/n−x∣<δ, where uniform continuity makes the change small, and its complement, whose probability is at most 1/(4nδ2) by Chebyshev's inequality. The bounded function f bounds the remaining error. This proves convergence without assuming differentiability.
For f∈C([0,1]d), the tensor-product Bernstein polynomial is the expected value of f(X1​/n,…,Xd​/n) for independent random variables Xj​∼Bin(n,xj​). The same uniform continuity and coordinatewise variance estimates give uniform convergence for each fixed finite d.
