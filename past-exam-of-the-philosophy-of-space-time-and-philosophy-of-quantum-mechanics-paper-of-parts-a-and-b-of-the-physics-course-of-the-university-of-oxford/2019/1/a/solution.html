There is no unconditional logical dependence of Newton's first law on Newton's third law. There is, however, a dependence if one applies the first to an extended, isolated composite and interprets its motion as the motion of its centre of mass. The difference is between the behaviour of an elementary body with zero total force and a theorem about cancellation of internal forces. Isaac Newton's own first-law discussion includes projectiles and spinning bodies, so the composite-body issue is physically significant.[ref]
For a constant positive mass in an inertial frame, Newton's second law gives mx¨=F. Setting the total force to zero yields uniform rectilinear motion; it uses no action–reaction assumption. The first law additionally identifies a common class of inertial frames in which such a statement applies. One cannot establish that class by arbitrarily straightening a single trajectory, and one cannot test the claim by defining every observed acceleration to be an unspecified force.
A concrete countermodel separates these issues. Suppose two bodies obey the second-law equations with internal forces F12​=f and F21​=−2f, with nonzero constant f. Every elementary body with zero total force still has constant velocity, but the two-body momentum satisfies
dtd​(p1​+p2​)=−f.
(1)
Thus the elementary-body first law is compatible with failure of the third. Calling the pair isolated does not make its internal total force zero: that is precisely what has not been assumed. This countermodel establishes logical independence, not a proposal for a successful fundamental interaction.
Now strengthen the first-law claim: every isolated composite has an inertially moving centre of mass whenever the total external force vanishes. With M=∑i​mi​, Newton's second law gives
MR¨=∑i​Fiext​+∑i<j​(Fij​+Fji​).
(2)
For an isolated pair, the strengthened claim requires F12​+F21​=0. This is the weak, equal-and-opposite form of Newton's third law. It does not by itself require collinearity with the bodies' separation; the central-force condition is stronger and bears on conservation of angular momentum. For many bodies, cancellation of the sum does not alone imply pairwise cancellation without assumptions about the decomposition into interactions.
The right conclusion is therefore conditional. A first law for individual force-free bodies does not presuppose the third law. A first law for all isolated material composites, combined with the usual centre of mass and Newton's second law, imports an internal-momentum-balance condition. In theories with dynamical fields, material bodies can exchange momentum with those fields; conservation of momentum then concerns the combined system and need not imply instantaneous pairwise action–reaction between particles.
