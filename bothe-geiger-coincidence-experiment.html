Bothe-Geiger coincidence experiment

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Electromagnetic radiation X-ray
OurBigBook.com Words: 871 Articles: 1
In 1924–1925, Walther Bothe and Hans Geiger tested event-level Compton scattering correlations against the statistical conservation proposed by BKS theory.[ref]
Needle Geiger counters shared a wall. X-rays passed beside it through hydrogen; recoil electrons registered in one counter and scattered photons triggered electron release in the other. Photographic records resolved approximately 10−4s.[ref]
The coincidence excess matched paired-emission expectations. Bothe distinguishes a later directional test from this timing experiment.[ref]
The two channels have different physical roles. The first responds to the local recoil electron's ionization. The second converts a scattered photon into an electron response, as in the photoelectric effect. A large counter signal is an amplified indication of an interaction, not a direct measurement of the incoming photon's energy. The detection efficiency and accepted directions determine how many genuine partners can be seen. Consequently, failure to record every partner is compatible with paired production.
The relevant distinction is between an average balance and a balance in each event. For an initially stationary free electron, Compton recoil kinematics gives
E=E′+Te​,cE​n^=cE′​n^′+pe​.
(7)
Here E,E′ are incident and scattered photon energies, Te​ is recoil kinetic energy, and pe​ is electron momentum. These are paired constraints, not just equations between averages over unrelated detections. Summing correctly paired events preserves the equations; throwing away the pair identities loses information. Two samples can have identical separate energy distributions while differing in their joint probability distribution.
The experiment therefore supplied evidence for the event association required by photon recoil, rather than weighing both outgoing energies in every event. A statistically established excess above accidental coincidences matters; one near-simultaneous pair cannot establish the association. It also cannot by itself settle every question about wave-particle duality.
Preparation, readout and background checks address different failure modes. The direct beam must avoid the second counter, and the counter boundary must prevent a recoil electron from masquerading as its own photon partner. Separate-channel rates constrain accidental coincidences; common disturbances or electrical coupling could instead create a correlated instrumental background. A useful diagnostic is to displace one record in time: under a stationary process assumption, unrelated-pair expectations persist while prompt partners separate. This is the explanatory control modeled below, not an assertion that Bothe performed that particular record-shifting procedure. A background estimate and a statistical comparison are essential to the inference.
The existing coincidence circuit article explains electronic selection, and accidental coincidence derives the accepted-window background. Here pairing can be understood directly from the two recorded time sequences, without imposing a modern detector trigger on the historical readout.
