Beer-Lambert law (A=εlc)

 Home Chemistry Physical chemistry Absorbance
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 5
The Beer-Lambert law relates absorbance A to an absorbing species' concentration c and path length l by A=εlc in a homogeneous medium using sufficiently narrow-band light. The coefficient ε depends on the species, wavelength and chemical solution conditions. This makes Ellman's reagent useful for quantitative thiol assays once its released colored species has been calibrated. [ref]
Here c is molar concentration, l is the geometric length through the sample, and ε is the molar absorption coefficient. The ideal beam has parallel light rays, a single wavelength, and a medium with uniform composition and isotropy; finite spectral width must be small relative to features in the optical spectrum.[ref]
For a spectrophotometer, distinguish the source's output from the reference that reaches the photodetector. Let P0​ be the received radiant power through a reference blank and P the received radiant power through the sample, after subtracting the photodetector's dark response. Both refer to the same selected wavelength band and collection geometry. Define the blank-normalized transmittance and absorbance by
T=P0​P​,A=−log10​T,T=10−A.
(19)
The reference blank includes the solvent and other assay ingredients except the absorbing target. Matching cuvettes and conditions makes their common losses divide out. Thus this T is relative to the reference blank; it is not the absolute transmittance of the entire cuvette and its contents. Both radiant powers can be expressed in watts, while their ratio, T, and its logarithm, A, are dimensionless. An instrument displaying 40% transmission means T=0.40, not T=40.
Why take a logarithm? Consider two consecutive identical absorbing layers. Each transmits a fraction t of the radiant power entering it, so together they transmit t2. Their absorbances add because −log10​(t2)=2(−log10​t). Doubling the sample length doubles absorbance while squaring transmittance. This turns multiplicative loss into an additive scale and makes a straight line useful for inferring molar concentration.
A short model derivation makes the assumption explicit. Suppose a thin layer of thickness dx removes the fraction kcdx of its incoming radiant power, where k is a fixed constant of proportionality and c is a uniform molar concentration. The corresponding ordinary differential equation, initial condition, and solution are
dxdP​=−kcP,P(0)=P0​,P(l)=P0​e−kcl.
(20)
Taking the base-ten logarithm gives A=kcl/ln10, so ε=k/ln10. The exponential decay follows from equal layers removing equal fractions of what remains. Proportionality to molar concentration additionally assumes that the absorbing chemical species retains the same response as its molar concentration changes; the geometric argument alone does not establish that.
