A tied largest modulus can prevent even direction convergence. With D=diag(2,−2) and x0​=(1,1)T/2​,
xk​=2​1​(1(−1)k​),ρk​=0,∥rk​∥2​=2.
(34)
The two directions form a cycle; neither is an eigenvector. There is no dominant-modulus spectral gap. A tied modulus is not a universal failure: an initial eigenvector remains on its line, and A=2I preserves every direction. Subspace iteration addresses a separated cluster by targeting a dominant invariant subspace instead of one eigenvector.
An orthogonal start can fail despite a strict spectral gap. For A=(21​12​) and x0​=(1,−1)T/2​, the dominant component is zero, so Ax0​=x0​ and every iterate equals x0​. The Rayleigh quotient is exactly 1 and the eigenpair residual is zero, although the dominant eigenvalue is 3. Exact arithmetic never creates a missing component.[ref]
The third panel contrasts these failures with N=diag(−3,1), x0​=(1,1)T/2​, for which
xk​=1+3−2k​1​((−1)k3−k​).
(35)
Here the horizontal eigenvector direction converges with a strict spectral gap, while the normalized vectors alternate signs. This is the successful direction convergence described under sign and phase in power iteration.
For a nonorthogonal eigenbasis, even the word “orthogonal” must be used carefully. The diagonalizable matrix C=(30​21​) has right eigenvectors v1​=(1,0)T, v2​=(−1,1)T, and dominant left eigenvector w1​=(1,1)T. For x0​=(a,b)T, the dominant coefficient is c1​=w1T​x0​=a+b. The starting vector v2​ is nonorthogonal to v1​ but stays at the smaller eigenvalue 1; (0,1)T is orthogonal to v1​ but has c1​=1 and converges to the dominant direction. The left eigenvector test, not the right-eigenvector inner product, controls the outcome.
A random vector drawn from a continuous probability distribution with a probability density function on the full real or complex vector space has c1​=0 with probability one. This avoids the exact missing-component hyperplane, but does not guarantee a large component or fast convergence. Floating-point arithmetic may seed a missing component, yet a structurally preserved zero can remain zero, so accidental perturbations are not a convergence guarantee.
