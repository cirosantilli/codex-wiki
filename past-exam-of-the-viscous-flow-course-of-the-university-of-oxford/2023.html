Let V(t) have a boundary moving with velocity vb​, and let n be its outward unit normal vector. For a smooth scalar field q, the Reynolds transport theorem is
dtd​∫V(t)​qdV=∫V(t)​∂t​qdV+∫∂V(t)​qvb​⋅ndS=∫V(t)​[∂t​q+∇⋅(qvb​)]dV.
(1)
In the last expression, extend vb​ smoothly into V(t) and use the divergence theorem. For a material volume, its boundary moves with the fluid velocity u, so vb​=u. Equivalently, the Reynolds transport theorem for a material volume reads
dtd​∫V(t)​qdV=∫V(t)​(DtDq​+q∇⋅u)dV,DtD​=∂t​+u⋅∇,
(2)
where D/Dt is the material derivative.
For an arbitrary moving region, mass conservation includes the relative mass flow rate across its boundary:
dtd​∫V(t)​ρdV=−∫∂V(t)​ρ(u−vb​)⋅ndS.
(3)
Substitution of the Reynolds transport theorem and the divergence theorem gives
∫V(t)​[∂t​ρ+∇⋅(ρu)]dV=0.
(4)
Since the region is arbitrary, a continuous integrand must vanish: if it had a positive or negative value at a point, a sufficiently small surrounding region would have an integral of the same sign. This gives the continuity equation. With constant positive mass density ρ, it reduces to
∇⋅u=0.​
(5)
Thus the flow is incompressible. For a material volume, the same argument starts directly from conservation of its total mass, with zero relative boundary flux.

At an oriented internal surface with unit normal vector n, the traction t(x,t;n) is the limiting contact force per unit surface area exerted by the material on the positive-n side on the material on the negative-n side:
t(n)=limΔA→0​ΔAΔF​.
(6)
For a boundary of a chosen material volume, this convention gives the external traction when n is outward. The Cauchy stress tensor has components
σij​=ei​⋅t(ej​),
(7)
so the second index specifies the surface orientation and the first specifies the force component. The vectors ei​ form the standard Cartesian coordinate system basis of unit vectors.
Cauchy's stress theorem states that the dependence of traction on the normal vector is linear:
t(n)=σn,ti​=σij​nj​.​
(8)
Repeated indices are summed according to Einstein notation. In particular, reversing the normal vector reverses the traction, and knowing the tractions on three mutually perpendicular coordinate planes determines the traction on every plane. Symmetry of the Cauchy stress tensor, for an ordinary fluid without body couples, follows separately from angular momentum conservation.

For an arbitrary material volume V(t), Newton's second law equates the rate of change of linear momentum to the resultant contact force, since there is no body force:
dtd​∫V(t)​ρui​dV=∫∂V(t)​ti​(n)dS=∫∂V(t)​σij​nj​dS.
(9)
Use the Reynolds transport theorem on the left. Expanding the product and using the continuity equation leaves
∂t​(ρui​)+∂j​(ρui​uj​)=ρ(∂t​ui​+uj​∂j​ui​)+ui​[∂t​ρ+∂j​(ρuj​)]=ρDtDui​​.
(10)
On the right, the divergence theorem applied to each row of the Cauchy stress tensor gives
∫∂V(t)​σij​nj​dS=∫V(t)​∂j​σij​dV.
(11)
Consequently the volume integral of ρDui​/Dt−∂j​σij​ vanishes on every material volume. Localizing as in part (i) gives the Cauchy momentum equation:
ρ(∂t∂u​+(u⋅∇)u)=ei​∂xj​∂σij​​.​
(12)
The left side is mass density times material acceleration; the right side is the divergence of the Cauchy stress tensor.

For an isotropic Newtonian fluid in incompressible flow, the constitutive equation is
σij​=−pδij​+2μEij​,Eij​=21​(∂j​ui​+∂i​uj​).​
(13)
Here p is pressure, δij​ is the Kronecker delta, μ is the constant dynamic viscosity, and E is the strain-rate tensor. The more general compressible constitutive equation can include a term proportional to (∇⋅u)δij​; that term vanishes here.
Differentiate the Cauchy stress tensor, retaining the constant dynamic viscosity outside the derivatives:
∂j​σij​=−∂i​p+μ∂j​∂j​ui​+μ∂i​(∂j​uj​)=−∂i​p+μ∇2ui​.
(14)
The last equality uses incompressibility. Substitution into the Cauchy momentum equation gives the Navier-Stokes equation
ρ(∂t∂u​+(u⋅∇)u)=−∇p+μ∇2u.​
(15)
The pressure gradient and the Laplacian of the velocity field therefore give the two contact force densities in this model.

∫−bb​cos(kn​y)cos(km​y)dy={b,0,​m=n,m=n.​

In a thin boundary layer, take streamwise velocity and distance to be order one, set z=δζ, and use incompressibility to set w=δW. The streamwise convective acceleration is order one, whereas its dominant viscous contribution after division by the Reynolds number is Re−1δ−2uζζ​. Their dominant balance selects
δ=Re−1/2.​
(36)
With this choice, the exact rescaled Navier-Stokes equations are
ux​+Wζ​uux​+Wuζ​δ2(uWx​+WWζ​)​=0,=−px​+uζζ​+δ2uxx​,=−pζ​+δ2Wζζ​+δ4Wxx​.​
(37)
Thus the leading pressure is independent of ζ. Matching it to the outer solution from part (a), evaluated near z=0, gives p=p∗​−x2/2, hence −px​=x. The leading boundary-layer equation is therefore
ux​+Wζ​=0,uux​+Wuζ​=x+uζζ​,ζ<0.​
(38)
The exact surface shear stress condition rescales to uζ​+δ2Wx​=δβx=γx. Since W(x,0)=0 for all x, its tangential derivative also vanishes there. The leading surface boundary conditions are
W(x,0)=0,uζ​(x,0)=γx.​
(39)
Matching to the outer solution requires
u(x,ζ)⟶x(ζ→−∞),W(x,ζ)∼−ζ(ζ→−∞).
(40)
The second matching condition specifies the leading growth of W, allowing a constant displacement correction. Requiring W+ζ→0 would impose an extra condition not supplied by the leading outer solution. This is the stress-driven stagnation-point boundary layer.
