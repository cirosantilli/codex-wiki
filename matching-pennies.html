Matching pennies

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Game theory Bimatrix game
OurBigBook.com Words: 336
Both players choose heads H or tails T without seeing the other's choice. The row player receives 1 when the choices match and −1 when they differ; the column player receives the opposite payoff. Thus matching pennies is a zero-sum game, with ordered payoff pairs:
Row choice / column choiceHT
H(1,−1)(−1,1)
T(−1,1)(1,−1)
At (H,H) the column player improves by switching to T; at (H,T) the row player improves by switching to T; at (T,H) the row player improves by switching to H; at (T,T) the column player improves by switching to H. Every pure strategy strategy profile therefore has a profitable unilateral deviation, so none is a Nash equilibrium.
Let p be the row player's probability of heads and q the column player's probability of heads. Their random choices are independent. Against fixed q, the row player's pure-choice expected payoffs are
U1​(H,q)=2q−1,U1​(T,q)=1−2q.
(27)
Against fixed p, the column player's pure-choice expected payoffs are
U2​(p,H)=1−2p,U2​(p,T)=2p−1.
(28)
Consequently the best responses, expressed as allowed heads probabilities, are
BR1​(q)=⎩⎨⎧​{0}[0,1]{1}​q<1/2,q=1/2,q>1/2,​BR2​(p)=⎩⎨⎧​{1}[0,1]{0}​p<1/2,p=1/2,p>1/2.​
(29)
Here [0,1] means every mixture is a best response, including both pure strategies. If q>1/2, the row player must choose p=1, which makes the column player's best response q=0, a contradiction. If q<1/2, the row player must choose p=0, which forces q=1, also a contradiction. Hence q=1/2; for the column player to mix at this q, the two column payoffs must tie, forcing p=1/2. The unique Nash equilibrium is therefore
p∗=q∗=21​,U1​(p∗,q∗)=U2​(p∗,q∗)=0.
(30)
Indeed, for any p,q, U1​(p,q)=(2p−1)(2q−1) and U2​(p,q)=−U1​(p,q). Holding q=1/2 gives the row player zero for every deviation p′; holding p=1/2 gives the column player zero for every deviation q′. A player is indifferent against the equilibrium mixture, but choosing an arbitrary best response to it need not preserve the opponent's best response. This is why that indifference does not produce additional Nash equilibria.
For a teaching treatment with the same payoff convention, see MIT 17.810, lecture 3, slides 29–38, on mixed strategies and matching pennies.
