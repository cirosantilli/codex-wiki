Farkas certificate for linear inequalities (CTy=0,y≥0,dTy<0)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Convex optimization Conic optimization Farkas' lemma
OurBigBook.com Words: 737 Articles: 1
Let C∈Rm×n and d∈Rm. For unrestricted x∈Rn, the system of linear inequalities Cx≤d has no feasible point if and only if there is a vector y∈Rm with
y≥0,CTy=0,dTy<0.
(16)
The entries yi​ are nonnegative Lagrange multipliers for the rows. Multiplying inequalities by them preserves their direction; the condition CTy=0 cancels every variable, leaving the contradiction
0=(CTy)Tx=yTCx≤yTd<0.
(17)
This is a Farkas' lemma alternative for non-strict inequalities; no strict-feasibility assumption is needed. See Boyd and Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization (2004), §5.8.3, pp. 261–262.
Three individually possible requirements, jointly impossible. Suppose two unrestricted real coordinates x1​,x2​, drawn as x,y in the diagram, must satisfy
x1​≥1,x2​≥1,x1​+x2​≤1.
(18)
Write every row in the same ≤ direction:
C=​−101​0−11​​,d=​−1−11​​,y=​111​​.
(19)
Then CTy=(0,0)T and dTy=−1. The nonnegative weighted sum, with Lagrange multipliers (1,1,1), is visibly
≤−1−x1​​​+≤−1−x2​​​+≤1(x1​+x2​)​​⟹0≤−1.
(20)
Each individual requirement defines a half-space in the plane. Even every pair has a feasible point: (1,1) satisfies the first two, (1,0) the first and third, and (0,1) the second and third. Their common intersection is empty, because the first two force x1​+x2​≥2, while the third demands at most 1.
Why cancellation is necessary for unrestricted variables. A nonzero linear functional (CTy)Tx can be made arbitrarily positive or negative by varying unrestricted x, so CTy≥0 alone would not yield a contradiction. Equality is essential here. If x≥0 is also imposed, the sufficient certificate becomes y≥0, CTy≥0, dTy<0, because then yTCx≥0. This is the sign convention used in robust infeasibility under uniform constraint relaxation. One can instead append the rows −xj​≤0 and use the equality certificate on the enlarged system. Reversing all constraints to Cx≥d reverses the scalar sign to dTy>0, as in the normalized integer Farkas infeasibility gap.
Checking a solver's infeasibility claim. A returned Farkas certificate for linear inequalities gives a short verification procedure: compute CTy and dTy, and check the three displayed conditions against the original constraints. Any positive multiple is another certificate; division by −dTy normalizes dTy to −1. With rational numbers as data and certificate, the tests can be exact. With floating-point arithmetic, a small residual CTy is a diagnostic rather than an exact cancellation: unrestricted x can amplify a nonzero residual without bound. A rigorous certificate therefore needs exact cancellation or justified error bounds and additional bounds on the variables. The strict negative scalar is the margin of contradiction.
The same weighted sums give dual bounds. For the linear program maximizing cTx over unrestricted x with Cx≤d, any y≥0 satisfying CTy=c gives
cTx=yTCx≤yTd
(21)
for every feasible point. Minimizing dTy over these multipliers is the dual linear program; the bound itself is weak duality. For example, maximize x1​+x2​ subject to −x1​≤0, −x2​≤0, x1​+x2​≤1. The multipliers (0,0,1) give the upper bound 1, attained at (1,0). The infeasibility example above uses the same row directions, but lower bounds of 1 on both coordinates. Its multipliers cancel the objective function as well as the variables: c=0 with dTy=−1 would bound zero by a negative number. Thus impossibility and linear programming optimality certificates use the same arithmetic. See Boyd and Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization slides, §§5.32–5.35 for infeasibility as a dual alternative.
Why every inconsistent system has such a certificate. Introduce a slack variable vector s≥0 and write Cx+s=d. Split the unrestricted vector as x=u−v with u,v≥0. Applying Farkas' lemma to the column matrix [C −C Im​] gives CTy≥0, −CTy≥0, y≥0 and dTy<0. The first two conditions together are exactly CTy=0. This reduction explains both the equality and the nonnegative row multipliers.
