The repeated, similarly oriented rounded triangular outlines in panel (a) are characteristic of AFM tip convolution: a narrow raised object can image the shape of a blunt or contaminated AFM probe, rather than reveal its own lateral shape. A finite tip radius broadens lateral dimensions. Replace or clean the AFM probe, check it on an independently characterized sharp-feature standard, and repeat at low load; agreement after a tip change is stronger evidence than a visually pleasing image. Image processing cannot uniquely recover unmeasured sidewalls or prove that every particle with a similar outline is an artefact.
Panel (b) contains horizontal streaks associated with steep features. The natural instrumental diagnosis is AFM feedback artefact: the height controller does not track a rapid change faithfully, or rings after it. Reduce the scan speed and adjust proportional/integral gains below the oscillation threshold. Compare trace and retrace and rotate the fast-scan direction: a streak following the scan rather than the specimen is instrumental. Loose contamination can also produce scan-line streaks, so clean or change the AFM probe if tuning does not remove them. Tip-shape broadening in (a), and scan-direction tracking/streaking in (b), are the principal diagnoses; the images alone do not uniquely establish every underlying cause.

piezoelectric scanner creep is the continued slow displacement after a step in scanner drive, even when the commanded voltage has stopped changing. The scanner's strain continues to relax, and the motion can resemble a slowly decaying, approximately logarithmic drift. After changing scan size, position or offset, early lines can therefore have stretched or compressed coordinates. Allow settling, avoid large abrupt moves, use a position-sensor closed-loop control system, and compare repeated images. This time-dependent response differs from the direction-dependent piezoelectric hysteresis and from ordinary thermal drift.

A steady-current image is not supported by the electrical connection drawn. tunnelling atomic force microscopy requires a conducting AFM probe, an applied bias voltage and a continuous return path from the contacted region to the current amplifier. graphene is conducting, but a flake on insulating glass is not electrically grounded merely because the other side or edge of the glass is earthed. Conventional semiconductor scanning capacitance microscopy is not a reliable dopant-contrast image of this specimen as drawn. At position (1), the AFM probe is far from the surface, with approximately zero force. At (2), attractive interactions bend the AFM cantilever toward the surface; when the attractive force gradient exceeds the cantilever spring constant, the equilibrium becomes unstable and jump-to-contact occurs. Position (3) is on the mechanical-contact branch: changes in imposed height largely bend the AFM cantilever and, for a compliant specimen, indent the sample. This branch can include compressive contact and adhesive contact under tension; contact does not itself imply a positive force at every point. At (4), during withdrawal the tip remains attached and pulls the AFM cantilever downward until the pull-off force is reached and it detaches. Approach and retraction follow different paths because adhesion creates a hysteretic contact. The numbered points identify stages on the source curve, not four extra question parts.

In quantitative nanomechanical mapping, the operational adhesion is the magnitude of the most attractive force immediately before detachment on a calibrated retraction curve:
Fadh​=−minFretract​,F=kc​δc​.
(1)
Here kc​ is the cantilever spring constant and δc​ its deflection after baseline subtraction. The area between approach and retraction relates to dissipated work, whereas the minimum force is a force, not an energy. Chemical attraction, Van der Waals forces and ambient-water capillary bridges can all contribute. Three suitable approaches give complementary contrast. tapping-mode atomic force microscopy supplies surface topography and an oscillation-phase image. It reduces lateral drag and is often effective for soft block copolymer domains. Its phase contrast depends on elastic response, adhesion, dissipation and operating setpoint together: a bright phase domain is not automatically the stiffer polymer. Check contrast as amplitude and setpoint change and use a known reference.
quantitative nanomechanical mapping fits the repulsive portions of many AFM force curves and separately maps effective elastic modulus, adhesion and deformation. It directly addresses the stiffness difference and limits maximum force. Its quantitative result depends on tip radius, force calibration, contact model, loading rate and film thickness; domains narrower than the contact footprint are mixed and the substrate can dominate a thin film. Use small indentation, substrate controls and a suitable viscoelastic interpretation.
friction force microscopy can reveal domains with different frictional and adhesive response, using torsional deflection during contact scans. It is simple and sensitive to surface chemistry, but continuous shear may deform or displace soft domains, and slope/topography can contaminate the lateral signal. Acquire trace–retrace pairs at several low normal loads and check reproducibility. Use a mechanical-modulus map to test the stiffness assignment; phase or friction contrast alone does not uniquely identify stiffness or chemical composition.

An immersion liquid increases numerical aperture through NA=nsinα, if the optics can use the same ray angle α inside a transparent, chemically compatible liquid of refractive index n. With unchanged k1​ and vacuum wavelength, the width scales as Rimm​=Rair​/n at fixed angle. If the two empirical formulae printed in the question are used literally with fixed k2​, they give DOFimm​=300/n2 nm.
For a physical axial displacement inside the medium, however, the familiar paraxial defocus range contains an additional index factor: DOF∝nλ0​/NA2. That convention gives DOFimm​/DOFair​≃1/n at fixed ray angle, or an approximately n-fold larger depth of focus at fixed NA and resolution. Thus immersion can improve lateral resolution, while its focus benefit depends on what is held fixed and on the axial-focus convention. It cannot improve resolution merely by inserting n into NA while ignoring optical redesign, absorption or resist compatibility. No liquid index is supplied for a numerical prediction.

i OurBigBook.com
charge stabilization of colloids supplies repulsion when like-charged surfaces and their electric double layers approach. steric stabilization uses attached or adsorbed molecular layers whose overlap costs conformational and osmotic free energy. Combining both gives electrosteric stabilization; a solvated coating must remain attached and well solvated. A viscous medium slows encounters but does not by itself create an equilibrium repulsive barrier.
DLVO theory adds screened electrostatic repulsion to Van der Waals force. A representative close-gap form is
U(h)≃Be−κh−12hAH​a​,
(5)
for equal spheres of radius a, with Hamaker constant AH​ and inverse Debye–Hückel screening length κ. The formula is schematic near molecular contact, where hydration, surface roughness and finite ion size matter. A repulsive barrier many kB​T high produces kinetic stability even if the primary attractive minimum is deeper than the dispersed state. Entry into that primary minimum generally gives strongly bound aggregation; a shallow secondary minimum can give reversible flocculation.
Adding electrolyte increases κ, shortens the repulsion and can remove the barrier, enabling fast diffusion-limited aggregation. The critical coagulation concentration is the approximate salt concentration at which this rapid-coagulation regime begins under specified conditions. It is not a universal property of the salt alone. Test the prediction using time-resolved dynamic light scattering or turbidity at controlled particle concentration, pH and temperature; check that added ions do not specifically adsorb or change the surface chemistry.

A Ragone plot has specific energy es​=E/m on its horizontal logarithmic axis and specific power ps​=P/m on its vertical logarithmic axis, normally in Whkg−1 and Wkg−1. The comparison must use the same mass convention: complete devices cannot fairly be compared with electrode-active-material values. At approximately constant delivered power, the discharge time is
th​=ps​es​​,ts​=3600ps​es​​.
(10)
Thus constant-time lines slope upward on the log–log plot. Ordinary capacitors lie toward high power and low energy; batteries toward higher energy and lower power; supercapacitors bridge the two. Technology and operating limits produce overlapping regions, not universal sharp boxes.

ii OurBigBook.com
An ordinary capacitor stores energy by separating charge across a dielectric: E=CV2/2 and Q=CV. Electronic charge redistribution is rapid, giving high specific power, but the dielectric breakdown field and modest capacitance limit specific energy. Series resistance limits pulse power and produces heating; a large nominal capacitance does not remove those losses.
A supercapacitor has a much larger interfacial area and nanometre-scale charge separation. An electric double-layer capacitor stores charge without a bulk redox transformation; pseudocapacitance adds sufficiently fast surface or near-surface redox storage. ion motion in pores, electrolyte stability and series resistance constrain its response. Its specific energy usually exceeds an ordinary capacitor's while high specific power and cycling durability remain possible.
An electric battery stores chemical free energy through coupled electrode oxidation-reduction reactions. It can store substantial energy per mass, but charge transfer, ionic transport, diffusion and heat removal constrain rapid discharge. The terminal voltage and accessible capacity depend on discharge rate and state of charge. Accordingly the plotted order follows the storage and transport mechanisms; it is not a claim that every battery has less power than every supercapacitor.

For a weak phase object, the contrast transfer function gives the signed transfer of an object's spatial Fourier components into intensity contrast. In a common convention,
H(u)=E(u)sinχ(u),χ(u)=π(21​CS​λ3u4+Δfλu2),
(11)
where u is a cyclic spatial frequency, Δf the defocus, CS​ the spherical aberration coefficient, and E(u) a coherence envelope. A zero suppresses that frequency; crossing a zero reverses contrast. The overall sign depends on the image convention, but the zeros do not. Single-image division by H cannot recover information exactly at its zeros. The microscope manufacturer's discussion explicitly connects the first zero with interpretable resolution and the higher-frequency sign changes.[ref]
The plotted first nontrivial zero is at approximately u0​=4.0 nm−1. Hence the smallest spacing in the first uninterrupted contrast band is about d0​=1/u0​=0.25 nm. This axis is cycles per length, not an angular wave number requiring a factor of 2π. Smaller spacings can contribute in later bands, but their contrast has reversed and requires appropriate interpretation or multiple-defocus reconstruction.

From the illuminated side downward, the p-type gallium arsenide cap provides a low-resistance route to the front metal contact. It should remain beneath the contact grid but be removed from the optical aperture to reduce parasitic absorption. The wider-band gap p-type aluminium gallium arsenide window transmits much of the useful light and reduces front-surface recombination of carriers in the adjacent absorber. The optical-window and reduced-recombination rationale has a primary experimental basis in early heteroface devices.[ref]
The p-type gallium arsenide emitter and n-type gallium arsenide base form the p-n junction. Absorbed photons generate electrons and electron holes; diffusion and the junction's electric field direct them toward the appropriate contacts. The n-type aluminium gallium arsenide back-surface-field layer provides a barrier to minority holes moving toward the substrate and reduces rear recombination. The n-type gallium arsenide substrate supports the epitaxial layers and conducts majority carriers to the rear contact.
The front metal must collect current with limited shading and the back metal provides the return contact. Interpreting the patterned hatched region as the optical antireflection coating, it reduces reflected light over the exposed window. One workable route uses two resist coatings and a metal-defined optical aperture. The following are eleven fabrication operations; exposure/development is grouped as one lithographic operation, and rear metallization includes its contact activation if required. Detailed cleaning, rinsing and inspection occur between compatible operations.
1. Spin-coat a first photoresist on the front surface to define a mesa mask.
2. Expose and develop that mask, retaining resist over the complete active-cell mesa, including its future front contacts.
3. Etch the exposed semiconductor through the p-type layers and slightly into the n-type base. This removes the unwanted junction around the cell and produces the lateral mesa step.
4. Strip the first photoresist, leaving a clean mesa with its cap intact.
5. Spin-coat a second photoresist suitable for the lift-off process.
6. Expose and develop openings at the intended front grid or contact pads, positioned safely inside the mesa edge.
7. Evaporate a p-contact metal stack onto the exposed gallium arsenide and resist. Au/Zn-based contact metallization is a conventional candidate, subject to doping and contact-resistance qualification.
8. Dissolve the second photoresist to lift off unwanted metal, leaving the front contacts on the cap.
9. Selectively remove the cap from the light-receiving aperture, using the front metal as a hard mask and the aluminium gallium arsenide window as the etch stop.
10. Deposit the rear n-contact metallization, for example a qualified AuGe/Ni/Au system, with an appropriate contact-activation anneal if required and compatible with the front contact.
11. Deposit an antireflection dielectric over the optical aperture through a mechanical shadow mask that keeps the contact pads accessible. Its refractive index and thickness are chosen for the target optical band.
The essential material changes are junction isolation at the mesa edge, metal retained on the front cap, cap removed only from the optical aperture, and a conducting rear contact. The metal systems and anneal are a process design, not compositions specified by the drawing. A workable design must also qualify the passivation of etched sidewalls and the front-contact surface preparation.

Mesa definition comes first so that the two front contacts can be aligned to an already isolated active area and can avoid damaged mesa edges. Keeping the cap until after metallization provides the intended contact surface and protects the window during the contact process. lift-off process must precede selective cap removal if the retained metal is to act as that etch mask. In a galvanic cell, a spontaneous oxidation-reduction reaction supplies electrical work: Δr​G<0 for discharge, the anode is negative, and the cathode positive. A discharging electric battery and a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell are two practical examples. The first stores its reactants internally; the second is supplied continuously with fuel and oxidant.
An electrolytic cell consumes externally supplied electrical work to drive electrode reactions or material transfer under an applied potential. Its anode is positive and cathode negative. water electrolysis for hydrogen production and metal electroplating are two practical examples. oxidation always occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode in both cell types; the electrode signs change. The electrolyte conducts ions, while electrons flow through the external circuit. A rechargeable battery can operate in either regime depending on whether it is discharging or being charged.

A thermoelectric generator uses the Seebeck effect and needs a spatial temperature gradient: a couple develops V≃(Sp​−Sn​)ΔT between hot and cold junctions. It can deliver continuous power under a steady thermal gradient and has no moving parts, but requires sustained heat flow and adequate hot- and cold-side thermal contacts. Thermal conduction, internal electrical resistance and the material's thermoelectric figure of merit limit conversion.
A pyroelectric generator responds to a time-dependent spontaneous electric polarization. Under approximately uniform heating or cooling, I=pAdT/dt when the electrical boundary condition allows charge flow. It can harvest temporal thermal fluctuations without a persistent spatial gradient. However, at a constant uniform temperature its ordinary pyroelectric current stops; practical extraction needs cycling, low-leakage electronics and a suitable load. A large open-circuit voltage is not evidence of a large available average power. Temperature-driven nanowire measurements provide a primary experimental example of this distinction.[ref]

The secondary pyroelectric effect is the change of polarization density produced when thermal strain acts through piezoelectricity. It is additional to the primary change at fixed strain. A linear constitutive description along the polar axis is
ΔP3​=p3ε​ΔT+∑j​e3j​εj​,p3free​=p3ε​+∑j​e3j​αj​,
(29)
where e3j​ are piezoelectric stress coefficients and αj​ the freely developing thermal strains per kelvin. Thermal-expansion mismatch with a support or a deliberately chosen compliant structure can change the secondary contribution. It enhances the total response only if its sign reinforces the primary response; mechanical boundary conditions and thermal cycling must be characterized rather than assuming every restraint is beneficial.
Preventing axial expansion removes the free axial-strain contribution, but does not automatically eliminate every secondary contribution. In a one-dimensional idealization with only ε3​ coupling and perfect axial clamping, that strain-mediated term vanishes. A real rod may still expand transversely, and nonzero e31​ or e32​ can change P3​ through those strains. In a stress-based constitutive equation, the same restraint appears as thermal stress and a piezoelectric term; do not add it a second time to a coefficient already specified for the constrained condition. Full suppression of all relevant thermal strains leaves the primary coefficient at fixed strain. The answer therefore depends on which degrees of freedom are constrained, not simply on the word “nanorod”.

A wireless sensor node combines a sensor, signal conditioning and conversion, a processor, a radio and a power subsystem. It can use a battery, a wired supply where practical, or local harvesting such as light, vibration or a thermal gradient, usually with a storage capacitor, supercapacitor or rechargeable battery to bridge interruptions. Harvested power must meet the time-averaged demand and storage must supply peak radio current.
Reduce power by duty cycling the sensor, processor and radio; use a low-leakage sleep mode and switch off unused peripherals. Buffer and batch data, transmit fewer unnecessary bits, use event-triggered acquisition when it meets the application, and reduce retries with a reliable link and an appropriate transmit-power level. Include wake-up, sensing, processing and communication energy in the budget: reducing payload alone may not reduce a radio dominated by startup overhead.
