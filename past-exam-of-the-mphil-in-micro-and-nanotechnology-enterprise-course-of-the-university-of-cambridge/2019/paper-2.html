In selective-area epitaxy, precursors supplied by metal-organic vapour-phase epitaxy incorporate on exposed crystalline InP through patterned openings in a silicon-nitride mask. Epitaxial facet-dependent incorporation and surface diffusion select axial and radial growth rates. Advantages include lithographic position and pitch control, an epitaxial orientation relationship and the absence of a foreign metal catalyst. Limitations include mask patterning, selective nucleation requirements, facet-dependent composition, loading and pitch effects, and unwanted radial growth.
In vapour-liquid-solid growth, precursors decompose and supply material to a liquid catalyst droplet; supersaturation drives precipitation at the liquid-solid interface. Droplet size can control wire diameter and enable high aspect ratios. Limitations include possible catalyst contamination, droplet stability and coarsening, composition memory at heterointerfaces, and restrictions from the catalyst phase diagram. Patterned catalyst placement can also control position, so positional ordering is not exclusive to selective-area growth. Conversely, selective-area wires are not automatically free of taper or compositional interfaces.[ref]

A physical bound-state wave function must be single-valued, finite, square-integrable and normalizable. Across a finite step in a regular potential, both ψ and its first derivative are continuous when the particle mass is the same on either side; integrate the Schrödinger equation across an infinitesimal interval to obtain the derivative condition. The second derivative need not be continuous.
An infinite hard wall is an exception to derivative matching: the interior wave function vanishes at the boundary, but its derivative need not equal the exterior zero derivative. A delta-function potential also permits a prescribed derivative jump, and an effective-mass interface requires the appropriate mass-weighted matching condition. Thus ordinary finite-step conditions should not be asserted without qualification for every singular potential.

A spin valve contains a magnetically soft free ferromagnet, a thin nonmagnetic metallic spacer and a reference ferromagnet pinned by exchange bias from an antiferromagnet. The recording track's stray field rotates or reverses the free-layer moment while the reference remains approximately fixed. Spin-dependent scattering makes the resistance lower for parallel moments and higher for antiparallel moments. A sense current converts this into a voltage signal, or a fixed voltage into a current signal.
A useful angular approximation is R(θ)=RP​+(RAP​−RP​)(1−cosθ)/2. Biasing the equilibrium moments near 90∘ gives an approximately linear response to small track fields. Pinning stability, magnetic shielding and the sensor's spatial extent matter for reading adjacent narrow bits.[ref]

Spin-valve read-head noise limits the field-to-electrical response: sensitivity is a response relative to noise, not merely a large total resistance. A small free-layer volume gives larger thermally driven angular fluctuations and can lose magnetic stability when its anisotropy barrier is insufficient compared with kB​T. Edge damage, stray coupling to the reference layer, nonuniform domains and Barkhausen jumps cause hysteresis and noise. Johnson noise and low-frequency resistance noise compete with a limited GMR signal; raising sense current is restricted by heating and current-induced disturbance.
One useful design combines a single-domain, gently biased free layer with a flux-compensated synthetic-antiferromagnetic reference. This reduces reference stray fields, suppresses unstable domain switching and holds the free layer near a linear operating point. The active sensing width can remain small while magnetic and electrical design preserve adequate stability and signal. An alternative is a magnetic tunnel junction with an optimized barrier, giving a larger magnetoresistance signal; its resistance-area product, shot noise and barrier reliability still require optimization. Shrinking every magnetic dimension indefinitely is not a sensitivity improvement.

For a triangular resistance network with edge resistances A,B,C, replacing it by a star gives the arm at each terminal as the product of its two adjacent triangle resistances divided by A+B+C. This Delta-star transformation preserves the pressure-flow relation at all three terminals, just as in a linear electrical network.
Apply it recursively to the two-rail ladder. Represent the first n grooves by a three-terminal star with arms xn​,yn​,zn​ leading respectively to the fixed inlet, frontier of the left rail and frontier of the right rail. Grooves are added from inlet toward outlet. Adding the next pair of rail segments gives B=yn​+Rc​ and C=zn​+Rc​; the new groove Rg​ closes a triangle between the old star centre and the two new rail endpoints. Convert that triangle to a star and combine its centre arm in series with xn​.
This method retains unequal pressure drops and unequal flow division along the rails; their finite resistances cannot be ignored when assigning the groove flows.

For parabolic bands, the density of states near a subband edge scales as g1D​∝(E−En​)−1/2, g2D​∝Θ(E−En​), and g3D​∝E−Ec​​. A larger-gap AlGaAs barrier has a higher conduction-band edge than GaAs. Donors in AlGaAs supply electrons that transfer into lower-energy GaAs states, leaving a positively charged donor region. The resulting electrostatic field bends the GaAs conduction band into a triangular interface well. With ballistic conductance quantization, as a gate opens the channel, conductance plateaux at G=Nb​(2e2/h) appear; successive transverse modes add one quantum, approximately 77.5μS per spin-degenerate subband. Their appearance is the key low-temperature electrical signature.
Require transverse confinement with separated subbands, kB​T and measurement-bias energy well below the subband spacing, a mean free path exceeding the channel length, and smooth low-reflection coupling to reservoirs. Correct for appreciable external series resistance. Phase coherence is needed for additional interference oscillations, but perfect phase coherence across an entire macroscopic circuit is not a separate requirement for the basic mode-counting result. A field that resolves spin splitting can produce e2/h increments.[ref]

An amphiphile has a solvent-compatible polar head and a hydrophobic tail region. The three geometrical parameters are the tail volume v, effective head-group area a0​ at the interface, and maximum effective tail length ℓc​. Their dimensionless combination is the amphiphile packing parameter, P=v/(a0​ℓc​).
The head area is an effective interfacial area affected by solvent, electrostatic repulsion and composition; it is not simply a fixed atomic cross-section.

For ideal spherical micelles, v/a0​=r/3 and r≤ℓc​, giving P≤1/3. For a long cylindrical micelle, v/a0​=r/2, so cylinders can accommodate larger packing parameters while keeping r≤ℓc​: roughly 1/3<P≲1/2 favours cylinders. Relatively small head area or greater tail volume reduces positive curvature.
For a planar bilayer leaflet of hydrophobic thickness ℓ, v/a0​≃ℓ, with ℓ≤ℓc​. Thus 1/2≲P≲1 favours bilayer/vesicle structures, with nearly planar bilayers toward P≃1. The two leaflets put hydrophobic tails together and hydrophilic heads toward water. For P>1, inverse structures become possible. These bounds are approximate packing guides; chain stretching, head interactions and aggregate free energy determine actual phase boundaries.
