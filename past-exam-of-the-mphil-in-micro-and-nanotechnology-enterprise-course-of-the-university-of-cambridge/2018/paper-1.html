A scanning transmission electron microscope makes an image by assigning a transmitted-electron signal to each position of a scanned probe. Its essential chain is electron source, probe-forming optics, scan system, thin specimen, transmitted-electron detectors and synchronized acquisition.
An electron gun and accelerating supply provide a stable beam of the chosen kinetic energy. Condenser lens optics and an aperture control probe current and convergence; the probe-forming objective lens focuses a small spot on the specimen. Alignment, focusing and astigmatism correction keep the probe usable across the field.
Scan coils deflect the probe in a raster. A stable specimen holder positions an electron-transparent region, and a high-vacuum column limits gas scattering and contamination. A bright-field transmission electron microscopy detector collects the central transmitted beam; an annular detector can select annular dark-field electron imaging, including high-angle annular dark-field imaging. Different angular selections give different contrasts, rather than a universal map of height.
Detector electronics, scan timing and a computer assign the collected signal to the corresponding image pixel. Spectrometers are useful additions but not essential to basic image formation. The post-specimen optics can adjust detector collection geometry; they do not set scan-image magnification in the same way as the projection lenses of conventional transmission imaging.

ii OurBigBook.com
For a displayed width D representing a scanned specimen width d, the linear image magnification is
M=dD​.​
(1)
The user changes the scan-coil excursion, hence d. A smaller raster displayed at the same width gives larger magnification. Changing the display or print size also changes displayed magnification, so the calibrated scale bar is more portable than a magnification label. This is the scan/display principle described in the manufacturer's microscope guide.[ref]
At the low-magnification extreme, the probe scans the widest accessible field. Coil range, apertures, scan distortion and maintaining focus constrain the actual usable width. Formally increasing d without bound would make M arbitrarily small, but a real column has a finite accessible field.
At the high-magnification extreme, the scan covers a very small region. Formally d→0 makes D/d diverge, but exactly zero scan width samples one location and supplies no spatial image. Electron microscope resolution, finite probe size, pixel spacing, drift, noise and specimen damage set the useful limit. Enlarging a field already sampled more finely than its resolvable information produces empty magnification, not new structural detail. For N pixels across a field, the specimen sampling interval is d/N; this is distinct from the probe's spatial resolution.

A tip-change artefact occurs when the effective apex changes during scanning, for example through material pickup, loss of an attached particle or damage. In tapping-mode atomic force microscopy, a sudden change can introduce a false height offset between parts of the image, even on a continuous terrace.
The change alters the probe–surface interaction or the effective point at which the chosen amplitude setpoint is reached. The controller moves the vertical scanner to recover that setpoint, recording a spurious step or a change in line baseline. The sign is not fixed: different apex changes can move the apparent surface up or down.
The amplitude channel usually shows a transient deviation as the interaction changes; with stable feedback it returns toward its setpoint. It therefore need not retain the same persistent offset as the recorded height. A change in probe resonance or free amplitude can also change the operating condition. If the apex geometry has changed, widths and shapes can change too, or duplicated structures can appear as a double-tip artefact. A baseline correction cannot recover these lost geometric data.

AFM image flattening estimates an unwanted background and subtracts it from the recorded height:
zcorrected​(x,y)=zmeasured​(x,y)−b(x,y).
(2)
A plane removes overall tilt; a line-by-line constant or low-order fit removes line offsets or curvature. For a tip-change height jump, align the baselines of unchanged reference regions on either side of the event, using a piecewise offset or appropriately masked line fits. A single global plane cannot in general remove an abrupt step.
Exclude genuine features from the fit, use the same terrace as a reference where possible, and retain the unprocessed data. The corrected image has a consistent relative background; it does not establish that the interaction force, tip shape or absolute height calibration remained unchanged. In particular, flattening is a correction for the offset component of a tip-change artefact, not a repair of every consequence of a changed tip.

Sample tilt and scanner bow are other common backgrounds that AFM image flattening can remove. Fit a plane b=a+bx+cy to a genuinely flat reference area and subtract it to remove tilt. A low-order curved fit can approximate the vertical background produced by a piezoelectric scanner's non-planar motion.
Line-dependent offsets or slow drift can sometimes be removed by subtracting a reference level from each scan line. Mask pits, particles and true steps so that they do not bias the reference. An independently measured flat calibration specimen and scans in different orientations help distinguish instrumental background from actual specimen curvature.

Flattening removes fitted components of the image whether they are instrumental or real. A true specimen slope, broad dome or slow height variation can be erased if it resembles the chosen background. Strong features can bias a fit and create false depressions, streaks or halos; an overly flexible polynomial can fit away scientifically useful topography.
Line-by-line correction can destroy real differences between terraces, especially when no common flat region spans the lines. Height statistics, roughness and feature depths then depend on masking and fit order. Report those choices and compare profiles before and after processing.
Flattening does not restore structures inaccessible to the tip, undo a double-tip artefact, remove finite-tip broadening, reconstruct a saturated signal or recover data lost to unstable feedback. It also does not calibrate the scanner. These limitations are important when a superficially smoother image is mistaken for a more accurate measurement.

Treat the specified particles only as rigid geometric shadowing objects; no biological preparation or interpretation is needed. Their projected shapes are rounded-ended rods with straight central length 100−30=70nm and cap radius 15nm. Normal, collimated evaporation deposits metal in the gaps between projected particle footprints, leaving isolated small islands after mask removal, rather than a metal replica of each rod.
One concrete dense parallel packing has center translations
u=(70+153​,15)nm,v=(0,30)nm.
(3)
Adjacent rounded ends then touch at triangular arrangements. The open regions are small curvilinear triangular gaps near those ends. This is a specified geometrical packing for the sketch; the words close-packed do not uniquely prescribe the domain orientation or registry of all real sheets.
Particle size and shape distributions change the gap size and spacing. Misorientation, missing particles, boundaries between ordered domains, overlaps, incomplete coverage and drying-induced cracks interrupt long-range order. Substrate roughness and uneven adhesion distort the arrangement. These are generic geometry and materials effects, not assumptions about a biological assembly mechanism.

An approximately isotropic wet etch produces lateral undercut as well as downward removal. In the hydrofluoric acid and nitric acid mixture, oxidation of silicon and dissolution of the oxidized material cooperate. This is not the strongly crystal-anisotropic KOH-type picture.
If the exposed horizontal surface recedes about 200nm and the local rate is isotropic, the etch front advances a comparable distance beneath each resist edge. The precise contour depends on transport, chemistry and mask integrity; the diagram represents the isotropic geometrical limit.

Near-normal ion milling preferentially removes exposed horizontal silicon and gives a more directional trench with much less lateral undercut. Energetic argon ions eject surface atoms by momentum transfer. The resist blocks direct trajectories to the protected regions.
After about 200nm removal of exposed silicon, expect an approximately flat recessed floor and steep transitions near the mask edges. A real positive photoresist pattern usually has finite edge slope and rounding rather than mathematically sharp vertical steps. The optical aerial image and finite resist contrast give a graded exposure boundary. Absorption through the film, focus, reflections from silicon and development kinetics can make the opening narrower or wider at different heights.
Incomplete development can leave residual material at the floor; standing-wave exposure can cause sidewall ripples, and substrate-related chemistry can produce footing. Bake history and developer transport can modify corners and line width. An undercut can be engineered with a suitable resist stack, but it is not an automatic consequence of ordinary positive-tone processing. The final profile must therefore be measured rather than inferred solely from the mask artwork.

In contact-mode atomic force microscopy, a sharp tip on a compliant cantilever remains in contact with the surface while a feedback loop controls its normal deflection. A laser reflected from the cantilever onto a position-sensitive detector converts bending into an electrical error signal. With calibrated deflection d and stiffness kc​, the normal force is approximately F=kc​d under the stated contact convention.
A piezoelectric scanner raster-scans the sample or tip. The controller adjusts vertical position to maintain the chosen deflection setpoint; that vertical displacement is recorded as topography. The right panel of the microscopy diagram identifies tip, cantilever, optical deflection readout, controller and vertical actuator.
The result is a probe-accessible surface, affected by finite tip shape, sample deformation, adhesion and feedback bandwidth. Lateral friction can distort or damage a soft surface, so constant deflection is not a guarantee of unchanged specimen structure.

ii OurBigBook.com
Four useful interactions are dispersion attraction, electrostatic interaction, capillary attraction and short-range repulsion. Their ranges are relative to atomic contact and depend on geometry and environment.
Van der Waals forces provide an attractive contribution extending beyond direct atomic overlap; for an ideal sphere and plane it scales as F=−AR/(6z2) in a simple nonretarded model. Electrostatic forces can also be long-ranged: biased conducting surfaces are commonly attractive, whereas charged insulating surfaces can attract or repel depending on their charge and screening.
A capillary meniscus in humid air can pull tip and sample together. It acts over the formation and rupture range of the liquid bridge and can greatly exceed atomically localized forces; it is not a universal interaction in a dry vacuum. At the shortest separations, overlap-related Pauli repulsion provides a steep repulsive contact contribution.
Atomic-scale contact contrast relies on strongly localized short-range tip–surface forces, especially the rapidly varying repulsive interaction in the simple contact-mode picture. Long-range attraction mainly adds a background. Real atomic-resolution imaging also depends on apex structure, stability and operating mode; a generic long-range force alone does not resolve individual surface atoms.

⟨t(s)⋅t(0)⟩=e−∣s∣/Lp​,Lp​=kB​Tκb​​,​

A thermoelectric generator uses the Seebeck effect: a maintained temperature difference across suitable conductors produces an open-circuit electromotive force. For a pair of legs,
Voc​=∫Tc​Th​​(Sp​−Sn​)dT≃Seff​(Th​−Tc​)
(10)
when the effective Seebeck coefficient is approximately constant. Repeated p- and n-type legs are usually connected electrically in series and thermally in parallel.
Connecting a load lets part of the heat flowing from the hot side to the cold side become electrical power. Both a heat source and a cold-side heat-removal path are necessary: a uniformly warm device with no maintained temperature difference supplies no sustained output. Internal electrical resistance, heat leakage, contact losses and the finite temperature difference reduce the harvested power; the converter is a heat engine rather than a source of energy from temperature alone.

Assume a conventional finite-radius tapping tip whose apex radius and shank are not negligible compared with a 10nm trench. The recorded depression is shallower and tip-limited, because the tip contacts the sides before its apex reaches the sharp bottom. Its apparent shape is the accessible envelope of the tip and specimen, not a faithful geometric section.
At a descending edge the separation initially increases, so the oscillation amplitude tends to rise above its setpoint until the controller moves downward. At an ascending edge it tends to fall, until the controller moves upward. For a narrow trench these transients can overlap or partly cancel. With ideal tracking the amplitude remains at its setpoint even when the height is wrong because of tip geometry. Thus a small amplitude error is not proof that the trench bottom was reached.
Width alone does not determine a unique trace: depth, tip radius, cone angle, scan speed and feedback bandwidth are also needed. The plotted traces illustrate the common finite-tip interpretation rather than infer those missing quantities from the drawing.

Measure pit dimensions from calibrated height data after background correction, with an explicit pit-boundary convention. Preserve the raw image, lateral calibration and acquisition settings. Level the surrounding terraces and correct line offsets with the pits excluded from the fit; otherwise the deep features bias the background.
Segment each depression relative to its local surface, for example by a reproducible height threshold or a fitted rim. Inspect segmentation overlaid on the image. For noncircular pits, report a metric such as equivalent-area diameter
deq​=2Apit​/π​,​
(14)

Conductive atomic force microscopy can map current near the pits while recording topography. Use a conducting tip, a suitable counter-contact to the film and an imposed bias, then compare current maps and local current–voltage curves near and away from dislocations. Spreading resistance microscopy is another useful electrical mode when its contact conditions and calibration are suitable.

A surface-potential image can complement these measurements, but it does not by itself measure conductivity.

LiftMode is a two-pass measurement separating a measured topographic path from a longer-range interaction measurement. The first pass records height with ordinary topographic feedback. On the second pass the tip retraces that line at a chosen vertical offset above it, reducing contact and short-range contributions while detecting electrostatic or magnetic interactions.
The lift height is relative to the recorded path, not a single constant laboratory height over a corrugated sample. Its usefulness depends on first-pass accuracy and whether the relevant long-range signal remains detectable at that separation.[ref]

Use Kelvin probe force microscopy to quantify a local contact-potential difference. A conducting tip and sample form a position-dependent capacitor. Apply a voltage with adjustable direct and small alternating components,
V(t)=Vdc​−VCPD​+Vac​cosωt.
(15)
The electrostatic force is proportional to C′(z)V(t)2/2, so its component at ω is proportional to
Fω​=C′(z)(Vdc​−VCPD​)Vac​cosωt
(16)
up to the chosen force-direction convention. A lock-in amplifier detects this component and a second controller adjusts Vdc​ to null it. At the null, Vdc​=VCPD​. The compensation voltage becomes the potential image.[ref]
A lift pass can reduce direct topographic interactions. Calibration on a reference with a known work function is required for an absolute work-function interpretation, and the sign convention must state which electrode is biased. On a semiconductor the result can include surface band bending, trapped charge and surface chemistry, so it need not equal a simple map of bulk electrochemical potential.

c OurBigBook.com
The measured potential is a spatially weighted electrostatic signal, not an infinitesimal-point voltage. The apex, cone and cantilever all contribute capacitance; increasing lift height weakens the signal and broadens its lateral averaging. Small pits can consequently show reduced or distorted contrast.
Imperfect topographic tracking changes the true separation on the lift pass, producing crosstalk correlated with pit geometry. Tip contamination or work-function drift changes the reference. Adsorbed water, mobile charges, illumination, atmosphere and surface states can change the specimen during measurement; semiconductor band bending can also depend on the applied electrical excitation.
There is a tradeoff between small lift height and suppressing short-range/topographic interference. Repeat at several heights, check trace/retrace and an electrical reference, and compare with independently measured electrical response. A stable potential contrast is evidence of a local electrostatic difference, not by itself proof of a particular dislocation's conductivity.

A useful scale estimate is d∼k1​λ/NA, where NA=nsinα. Five complementary developments are immersion optics, illumination shaping, phase-shifting masks, computational proximity correction and multiple patterning. They address different parts of the process rather than repeal diffraction.
Immersion lithography raises numerical aperture by putting a compatible high-index liquid between the final lens and wafer. Off-axis, annular or dipole illumination places useful diffraction orders within the projection pupil and improves contrast for selected pattern directions.
A phase-shift mask uses controlled optical phase differences to sharpen destructive interference around selected features. Optical proximity correction modifies mask edges and adds assist features to compensate pattern-dependent image formation, resist and etch biases.
Multiple patterning divides a dense final layout into more widely spaced optical patterns, or uses spacer-based pitch division after an optical definition step. Overlay, etch control and pattern decomposition are then critical. For example, with λ=193nm, NA=1.35 and k1​=0.25, a single-exposure scale is roughly 36nm; obtaining dense features of order 20nm commonly requires pitch multiplication rather than attributing everything to one exceptionally sharp aerial image.
These resolution enhancements also require suitable resist contrast, focus control and metrology. The manufacturer's description explains the numerical-aperture benefit of immersion; no claim about a particular 2018 production process is inferred from its current product pages.[ref]

Replace one-wire discovery and bespoke alignment with reproducible placement, uniform device geometry and a scalable interconnect process. A circuit needs wires at known sites and orientations with controlled diameter, channel length, composition and electrical properties. Directed assembly, transfer or patterned growth can address placement, but must be validated for occupancy and alignment yield.
Use aligned array patterning rather than locating every randomly deposited wire individually. Control contact resistance, dielectric continuity and gate overlap across the wafer. Plan dielectric isolation, crossovers, vias and multiple interconnect levels so that connections do not short neighboring gates or channels. A reliable supply of the required device polarities and threshold voltages is also needed for the intended logic architecture.
Circuit manufacture additionally requires contamination control, compatible thermal budgets, passivation, dimensional and electrical metrology, defect detection, test structures and repair or redundancy strategies where appropriate. Serial electron-beam writing is useful for prototypes but can become a throughput bottleneck; scalable optical or imprint-based pattern transfer is attractive when its alignment and resolution meet the design. A successful isolated transistor does not establish circuit-level yield or reliability.

The contact angle is measured through the liquid between the solid surface and the tangent to the liquid–vapor interface at the contact line. The right panel of the wetting diagram labels that angle and the three interfacial tensions.
At 0∘, the ideal macroscopic limit is complete wetting: a film spreads rather than remaining a finite-angle cap. Angles between 0∘ and 90∘ are conventionally called hydrophilic for water; the drop wets the surface relatively well. At 90∘ the tangent is vertical. Between 90∘ and 180∘ the water drop is conventionally hydrophobic, with increasingly weak wetting. The exact 180∘ limit represents vanishing macroscopic contact in an ideal model, not a routine finite-area sessile drop on a real solid.
The categories describe water under specified conditions. Roughness, contamination and contact-line pinning can make advancing and receding angles differ from each other and from the ideal equilibrium angle.

Conducting organic materials can be attractive for flexible, lightweight and near-ambient heat harvesters; inorganic materials offer a wider established range of high-performance and high-temperature options. The appropriate comparison includes device geometry and durability as well as peak ZT.
Conducting polymers can have low lattice thermal conductivity, be solution processed and accommodate mechanically compliant large-area devices. Their carrier concentration and ordering can be modified through doping and processing. Contact resistance, environmental stability, reproducibility and obtaining stable complementary p- and n-type materials can limit practical modules. Their organic character does not imply that they remain stable at high temperature.
Inorganic thermoelectrics can provide high power factors, temperature stability and mature transport data over application-specific ranges. Some compositions are brittle, dense, expensive or contain elements requiring careful environmental management; they are not all interchangeable. Interfaces, thermal cycling and interconnects can dominate device lifetime.
An experimental example of organic optimization controlled the oxidation level of a conducting polymer to balance transport and its low thermal conductivity, showing that organics are not categorically excluded from thermoelectric performance improvements.[ref] Material selection should use measured properties across the actual temperature interval and the achievable thermal gradient, not a blanket organic-versus-inorganic ranking.
