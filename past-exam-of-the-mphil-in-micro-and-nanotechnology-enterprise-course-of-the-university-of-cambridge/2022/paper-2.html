Three useful growth mechanisms are monomer addition, Ostwald ripening and oriented attachment. They can occur sequentially or compete; they are not necessarily three distinct experimental recipes.
In monomer-addition crystal growth, dissolved atoms, ions or molecular growth units attach to an existing nanocrystal. Supersaturation provides a chemical-potential driving force, approximately Δμ=kB​Tln(c/ceq​) for an ideal solution. Transport through solution and incorporation at steps or facets determine the rate. Growth consumes the excess solute without requiring collisions between whole crystals.
In Ostwald ripening, smaller crystals dissolve while larger ones grow through solution. Curvature raises the surface contribution to chemical potential, approximately μ(r)=μ∞​+2γΩ/r for a sphere with surface free energy γ and atomic volume Ω. Dissolved material therefore moves from high-curvature particles to lower-curvature ones, reducing total interfacial free energy.
In oriented attachment, whole crystallites align compatible lattice planes and join. Removing exposed surface and forming a coherent interface lowers free energy; rotation, ligand removal and interface healing can be rate limiting. Misalignment can leave twins or other defects. Simple aggregation without crystallographic registration is related particle attachment, but is not necessarily oriented attachment.

Symmetry breaking in nanocrystal growth makes growth rates along initially equivalent crystallographic directions unequal, so the final shape has lower symmetry than an unperturbed seed. It need not change the underlying face-centered cubic lattice. A cubic lattice does not require a cubic external shape.
Anisotropy can originate in a twin or stacking fault, unequal ligand coverage of nominally equivalent sites, selective adsorption or etching, a substrate or external field, or a local fluctuation amplified because newly deposited material changes subsequent growth. Facet-selective binding suppresses some normal growth rates; concentration and reduction kinetics determine whether atoms can redistribute towards a near-equilibrium shape or remain in a kinetically selected one. A facet with slow normal growth tends to remain exposed, while a fast-growing facet can disappear.
For a one-dimensional silver example, begin with a small seed with multiple regions related by crystal twinning containing five sectors around a common axis. Ligand-protected lateral faces and more active ends favour axial addition, producing an elongated pentagonal rod and then a wire. The defect geometry and surface kinetics jointly distinguish the axial direction. Fivefold twinning is a composite structure with strain accommodation, not a perfect single fcc crystal with forbidden fivefold lattice symmetry.
For a two-dimensional example, begin with a seed containing a planar twin or stacking fault. Basal-face protection and enhanced incorporation at suitable edges favour lateral extension over thickening, producing a thin triangular or hexagonal plate. The accompanying schematic emphasizes seed, symmetry-breaking event and anisotropic growth stages. These are representative routes, not universal requirements for every rod or plate. Experimentally, silver-seed growth can also be redirected into lower-symmetry forms by controlling reduction kinetics.[ref]

In surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy, electromagnetic SERS enhancement comes from the strong local optical field near a plasmonic metal nanostructure. A localized surface plasmon resonance in gold or silver can increase both the field driving a molecular Raman transition and the coupling of its emitted light to the far field. A common estimate is
GEM​∼​E0​Eloc​(ωL​)​​2​E0​Eloc​(ωS​)​​2,
(1)
often summarized as an ∣E∣4 enhancement when pump and Stokes frequencies see similar responses. Sharp tips and narrow interparticle gaps can produce intense hot spots.
Chemical SERS enhancement changes the molecule's Raman polarizability through adsorption, electronic hybridization or a metal–molecule charge-transfer resonance. It is more sensitive to molecular identity and binding geometry than a purely electromagnetic field enhancement, and generally requires close contact.
Tune particle size, shape and gap geometry so that a plasmon resonance overlaps the excitation and Raman frequencies, and bring the target into the enhanced-field region. Reproducible adsorption sites, low fluorescence background and clean reference spectra are as important as a large local enhancement. A hot spot does not guarantee that a dilute sample occupies it; a detection limit cannot be computed from concentration alone. Au or Ag improve the signal per suitably located molecule, while sampling and background determine the practical limit.

In atomic layer deposition, expose the surface to a molybdenum precursor, purge unreacted precursor and volatile products, expose it to a sulfur reactant, and purge again. Each half-reaction should saturate the available reactive sites rather than continue indefinitely with dose. Repetition builds a molybdenum disulfide film, with nucleation, incubation and layer closure checked independently.
Self-limited surface chemistry is not the same as depositing exactly one complete crystal monolayer per cycle. A cycle may add only a fraction of a layer, and initially isolated islands can thicken before all bare substrate is covered. Reactions on hydroxylated silicon dioxide can also differ from those on the comparatively inert MoS2​ basal surface. A precursor pair must actually show saturation under the chosen conditions; simply alternating gases is insufficient evidence of ALD.
In metalorganic chemical vapor deposition, metalorganic and sulfur-containing precursors are typically supplied simultaneously or with overlapping residence times, and film growth depends on decomposition, transport and surface reaction rates. Both methods involve chemistry, but ALD separates saturating half-reactions and purge steps. An ALD process need not use a metalorganic precursor, and pulsed CVD is not automatically ALD.

Potential advantages are reproducible thickness increments, large-area uniformity, conformal access to features and compatibility with moderate-temperature integration. Saturated exposure can make coverage less sensitive to small precursor-flux variations than an unsaturated deposition rate. The cycle count provides a useful process variable for matching an optical or electrical thickness target.
For a monolayer device, control of nucleation and coalescence is especially valuable, because excessive second-layer growth and uncovered patches both change the optical response. The advantages are conditional: a reliable ALD window and suitable precursor chemistry are required, and conformality does not imply a single-crystal film. Long exposure and purge steps reduce throughput, residual ligands or impurities can remain, and post-growth crystallization may require temperatures that the integrated device cannot tolerate.

Many independently oriented nuclei form on an amorphous support and then coalesce before they can become one large crystal. The SiO2​ substrate provides no unique epitaxial orientation. At relatively low deposition temperature, short surface diffusion lengths and slow bond rearrangement restrict the distance over which deposited species can find a well-ordered configuration. Island collisions therefore leave grain boundaries; precursor residues and incomplete sulfurization can add disorder.
Raising temperature or annealing can improve crystallinity, but may conflict with the ALD reaction window, substrate compatibility or sulfur retention. Small grains are consequently a frequent process outcome, not a fundamental theorem that ALD can never form a well-ordered layer. A direct ALD experiment on SiO2​-covered wafers found nanocrystalline MoS2​ by diffraction and electron microscopy, while demonstrating self-limited precursor exposure behaviour.[ref]

Use a thin, continuous cap of hexagonal boron nitride, preferably assembled without exposing the cleaned MoS2​ surface to ambient contamination. It is chemically inert and relatively transparent in the visible range, and can isolate the film from adsorbing water, oxygen and hydrocarbons during optical measurements. Mr,∥​=Ms​cosθ(tCo​).​

In current-perpendicular-to-plane magnetoresistance, the resistance difference between parallel and antiparallel ferromagnetic layers relies on spin-dependent transmission and retention of spin information. Here, an electron traversing the stack must pass a platinum layer between the two Co films. Strong spin-orbit coupling and associated spin relaxation in Pt, together with spin-memory loss at interfaces, can reduce the polarization reaching the second Co layer and weaken giant magnetoresistance. This is more relevant to the stated perpendicular current than an in-plane shunting explanation.
First, redesign the spacer path so a low-spin-relaxation conductor such as Cu directly separates the two ferromagnets, while retaining perpendicular anisotropy through other interfaces or a different layer sequence. Minimize unnecessary Pt traversal and improve interface sharpness. Merely thinning the Cu layer is not sufficient if it reintroduces magnetic coupling or leaves the Pt spin-relaxation bottleneck unchanged.
Second, replace the metallic spin-transport section with a suitable thin insulating tunnel barrier, for example an engineered MgO-based magnetic tunnel junction, and choose compatible magnetic electrodes. Spin-selective tunnelling can give a much larger resistance contrast, but pinholes, disorder and a poorly matched barrier defeat it. Retaining distinct switching fields and a stable antiparallel configuration remains essential in either design.

A gallium arsenide/aluminium gallium arsenide heterojunction has a conduction-band offset that can confine electrons on the GaAs side. Donors in the wider-gap AlGaAs supply electrons; band bending produces a narrow interfacial potential well. Motion normal to the interface is quantized into subbands, while motion within the plane remains approximately free with GaAs effective mass. Occupying the lowest confinement subband creates a two-dimensional electron gas.
Modulation doping spatially separates the ionized donors from the mobile electrons. An undoped spacer between the donor layer and the channel reduces Coulomb scattering from donor ions while still allowing charge transfer. This can produce higher mobility than doping the conducting channel itself. The spacer thickness balances lower scattering against weaker carrier supply and electrostatic control; interface roughness, alloy disorder, phonons and background impurities can still limit mobility. The channel is a finite-width quantum wavefunction, not a mathematically zero-thickness sheet.

Symmetric wetting means that both film boundaries prefer the same block: A at both, or B at both. Asymmetric wetting means opposite preferences, such as A at the substrate and B at the free surface. A neutral boundary has approximately equal interfacial free energies against A and B and therefore imposes no first-order preference between them.
These terms compare the substrate–polymer and atmosphere–polymer interfaces, not the volume fractions or molecular weights of the two blocks. A film can have one neutral boundary and one selective boundary; saying only that its top surface is neutral does not make both interfaces neutral. Surface preference competes with chain stretching and the energetic cost of A/B contacts in selecting a block copolymer morphology.

With identical A-selective boundaries, stress-free parallel stacks favour approximately integer multiples of L0​. An arbitrary imposed thickness generally cannot preserve both the bulk period and those terminations everywhere.

Positive dielectrophoresis acts up the gradient of the squared RMS field, towards stronger-field regions; negative dielectrophoresis acts down that gradient, towards weaker-field regions. The sign is the sign of ℜK(ω).
The force is not generally tangent to an electric field line. Field lines show the direction of the field itself, whereas ∇∣ERMS​∣2 describes how its strength changes in space. A spatially uniform field gives zero dipole-order dielectrophoretic force even when it is large. Reversing the field each half-cycle does not reverse this time-averaged force, because the relevant intensity-like quantity is quadratic.

Zinc blende and wurtzite differ in their stacking of tetrahedrally bonded bilayers: an ideal cubic sequence is ABCABC, while the hexagonal sequence is ABAB. During vapor–liquid–solid growth, each new interfacial nucleus can choose between continuing the existing sequence and changing it.

Schematically, its free energy combines a favourable chemical-potential term with new edge/interface costs and, when appropriate, a stacking penalty. Supersaturation changes the relative importance of these terms; the droplet shape and contact angle change which nucleation sites are available.
Control is therefore possible through temperature, III/V supply ratio, precursor fluxes, catalyst composition, droplet volume/contact angle and wire diameter. Monitoring growth rather than only inspecting cooled specimens helps determine which parameter actually changes the interface. A nucleation model for Au-catalysed III–V wires explicitly showed how triple-line nucleation can favour wurtzite under suitable interface energies and supersaturation.[ref] There is no universal rule that one phase always corresponds to the larger temperature or smaller diameter regardless of catalyst and interface geometry.

One consistent five-postulate formulation is the following; different textbooks group the same content differently.
  • State. A pure state is a normalized vector ∣ψ⟩ in a complex Hilbert space, with ⟨ψ∣ψ⟩=1; vectors differing only by an overall phase represent the same state. For a spinless particle in position representation, ψ(r)=⟨r∣ψ⟩ is its wavefunction.
  • Observables. A physical observable A is represented by a self-adjoint operator A^. In a Cartesian position representation, x^=x and p^​x​=−iℏ∂x​, with ℏ=h/(2π).
  • Outcomes and probabilities. Allowed ideal measurement outcomes are spectral values a of A^. The Born rule gives P(a)=⟨ψ∣Pa​∣ψ⟩, where Pa​ projects onto the corresponding eigenspace; continuous spectra require the analogous spectral probability measure. The expectation is ⟨A^⟩=⟨ψ∣A^∣ψ⟩.
  • Ideal measurement update. Conditional on a nonzero-probability outcome a, an ideal projective measurement leaves Pa​∣ψ⟩/⟨ψ∣Pa​∣ψ⟩​. For a nondegenerate result this is its eigenstate. This is the ideal projection rule, not a description of every general measurement apparatus.
  • Time evolution. Between measurements a closed system evolves by the Schrödinger equation, iℏ∂t​∣ψ(t)⟩=H^∣ψ(t)⟩, with Hamiltonian H^. For time-independent H^, this gives unitary evolution e−iH^t/ℏ.
The position-dependent probability density is ∣ψ∣2, and its flux is the probability current. These distinguish the wave amplitude from the measurable particle flow in the following scattering problem.

Scanning tunneling microscopy uses electron tunnelling between a conducting tip and a sample across a narrow vacuum gap.
