i OurBigBook.com
Diffraction contrast arises because elastic scattering transfers transmitted electron intensity between the direct beam and Bragg diffraction beams. A local change in crystal orientation, strain or thickness changes that transfer. An objective aperture selecting the direct beam makes strongly diffracting regions relatively dark in bright-field transmission electron microscopy; selecting a diffracted beam reverses the selection in dark-field transmission electron microscopy. This is a change in scattering conditions, not necessarily a change in chemical composition.
Three characteristic effects are bend contours, thickness fringes and dislocation contrast. Bend contours follow positions where a gently curved crystal meets a particular Bragg diffraction condition. Thickness fringes arise from thickness-dependent exchange of intensity between coupled transmitted and diffracted waves. A dislocation distorts nearby planes, changing their diffraction condition and producing localized contrast; changing the diffraction vector can suppress particular dislocations. Moiré fringes from overlapping lattices provide another acceptable example, but their coarse spacing is a beat spacing rather than an atomic spacing.

a OurBigBook.com
Use conventional bright-field transmission electron microscopy, with nearly parallel illumination, a suitably small objective aperture and low magnification covering the whole cross-section. More strongly scattering material sends more intensity outside the selected central beam and therefore looks darker. Tilt crystalline layers away from strong Bragg diffraction conditions to reduce diffraction contrast, and use a near-focus image so that electron phase contrast does not dominate.
The nominally uniform 150nm thickness helps separate composition from thickness variations, but does not make the contrast purely compositional: density, atomic number and multiple scattering still matter. Choose an accelerating voltage sufficient to transmit this thickness, commonly 200–300kV for a robust inorganic film, subject to its damage limit. High-angle annular dark-field imaging in scanning transmission electron microscopy is a useful alternative for approximately monotonic mass-thickness contrast, but is a different acquisition mode and still requires thickness and detector-angle control.

b OurBigBook.com
Use a low-dose, dose-efficient acquisition rather than assuming that a lower accelerating voltage is always gentler. Focus and align on a sacrificial neighbouring region, blank the beam between exposures, illuminate only when recording and use an efficient detector. A broad, low-current-density transmission electron microscopy exposure can avoid a high instantaneous local dose; scanning can also work if probe current, dwell time and repeated visits are controlled.
Radiolysis starts with inelastic scattering and ionization. For an appropriate thin specimen, increasing the electron energy can reduce the inelastic interaction probability and energy deposition per incident electron, so a relatively high voltage can help. However, increasing voltage can also activate knock-on displacement. The optimum therefore depends on the material and the amount of useful information obtained per damaging event; 150nm alone does not determine it. Cooling may reduce diffusion-driven chemical changes but does not eliminate primary ionization. An experimental study of nonbiological layered polymer films explicitly found that the voltage choice must balance damage with image signal.[ref]

Use high-resolution transmission electron microscopy with a coherent, well-aligned beam and an objective aperture admitting the direct beam and the spatial frequencies required at the interface. Orient a crystalline region near a suitable zone axis so that useful lattice fringes are excited; minimize drift, vibration and astigmatism. Choose defocus and aberration correction to transfer the target frequencies, rather than treating the image as a direct map of atoms at arbitrary defocus.
An aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy probe with high-angle annular dark-field imaging can instead resolve an interface with more directly interpretable atomic-number contrast. A nominal 150nm thickness may be too great for clean atomic-resolution interpretation: thin the interface further if necessary, finish focused ion beam milling at lower ion energy to reduce damaged surface layers, and check for curtaining and implantation. The best mode depends on crystallinity and damage sensitivity; an amorphous interface calls for resolving its boundary and composition rather than inventing lattice fringes.

In piezoresponse force microscopy, a conducting atomic force microscopy tip contacts a sample with a counter-electrode. Apply V(t)=Vdc​+Vac​cosωt. The converse piezoelectric effect produces an oscillatory displacement, approximately uω​=deff​Vac​. Contact with the moving surface bends the cantilever, and a lock-in amplifier records the displacement amplitude and phase relative to the drive.
For opposite out-of-plane ferroelectric domains, the effective piezoelectric coefficient changes sign. Their vertical response therefore differs by approximately 180∘ in phase. Equivalent antiparallel domains can have similar amplitudes, while cancellation or reduced response near a wall can produce an amplitude minimum. Phase identifies the relative response sign; an uncalibrated detector phase is not an absolute polarization direction.
Use a small drive below the switching threshold while imaging, a stable contact force and a well-characterized contact resonance. Electrostatic forces, contact stiffness and surface charging can also create signals at the drive frequency; observing contrast alone is insufficient evidence for polarization switching.[ref]

ii OurBigBook.com
Measure cantilever torsion with the lateral channel of a four-quadrant optical detector. An in-plane surface displacement perpendicular to the cantilever axis twists the cantilever. The reflected laser spot moves sideways, so the left-minus-right detector signal, demodulated at ω by a lock-in amplifier, gives the amplitude and phase of the lateral piezoresponse. The top-minus-bottom signal primarily records bending.
Displacement along the cantilever axis can instead cause buckling and leak into the nominally vertical channel. Rotate the specimen by 90∘ and repeat the measurement to sample the other in-plane component; use a reference specimen and sensitivity calibration before converting a voltage into displacement or force. A torsional spring constant and lever-arm calibration are additionally needed for an actual lateral-force value. Thus lateral piezoresponse is not automatically a calibrated force measurement.

A surface slope converts some vertical displacement into lateral motion of the tip and changes the normal contact geometry. Roughness also changes friction, contact area and local contact stiffness, and can mix cantilever bending, buckling and torsion. A lateral image that follows a height gradient can therefore appear even when the relevant in-plane polarization component is absent.
Record topography and both piezoresponse force microscopy channels simultaneously, and compare the lateral signal with the local slope rather than height alone. Reverse the scan direction to identify frictional contributions, rotate the sample relative to the cantilever, and compare the vector transformation expected for an in-plane response with the geometrical cross-talk. Repeat on a smoother region or a polished specimen, at controlled force and drive amplitude. Track contact resonance or compare sufficiently far from it so that local stiffness does not masquerade as a change in response.
A useful control is a nonpiezoelectric surface of similar roughness. Electrically switching a domain while leaving its topography unchanged provides a further comparison when feasible. No single trace–retrace test proves the absence of cross-talk: genuine in-plane response and slope-induced signals can both transform under rotation, so several controls must agree.

A negative photoresist is removed at low dose and retained at high dose. Its remaining-thickness curve rises from zero above a gel or retention threshold and approaches one above a larger full-retention threshold. A useful schematic model is
f−​(D)=⎩⎨⎧​0ln(Db​/Da​)ln(D/Da​)​1​D≤Da​,Da​<D<Db​,D≥Db​.​
(5)

Halving exposure narrows and can lower the retained ridge; doubling it broadens the ridge and can raise its shoulders. A saturated central plateau cannot become thicker than the original film in this simple model. If the half-dose peak is below Da​, no ridge survives; if it lies between Da​ and Db​, the centre survives only partially.

Take the ArF wavelength as λ=193nm and assume an on-axis coherent illuminator. A grating of period P has diffraction orders with transverse spatial frequencies m/P. To reproduce amplitude modulation, the pupil must pass the zero order and at least the first spatial-frequency components. For symmetric on-axis illumination this requires 1/P<NA/λ.
For equal lines and spaces, the printed feature width is the half-pitch w=P/2. The ideal limiting values are therefore
wmin​=2NAλ​=1.2193​=161nm​,Pmin​=322nm​.
(6)
These are limiting values approached from within the pupil, with adequate mask contrast and a suitable resist; a weakly modulated mask need not supply sufficient image contrast. They are not the Rayleigh separation of two incoherent point objects. Off-axis coherent illumination can shift the admitted orders and changes this particular geometry.

The projection lens maps the mask's angular spectrum into its back focal plane: within scalar Fourier optics this is the Fraunhofer diffraction or spatial-Fourier plane. A periodic mask produces discrete diffraction orders, separated by 1/P in object-side spatial frequency after accounting for projection magnification. The finite numerical aperture bounds the transverse wave vectors admitted by the lens, so the pupil is a spatial-frequency filter.
For an ideal on-axis coherent system,
Uimage​(νx​,νy​)=P(νx​,νy​)Umask​(νx​,νy​),∣ν∣<λNA​.
(7)
A second Fourier transformation forms the image, and the recorded intensity is ∣Uimage​∣2. If only the zero order passes, the field is uniform and the line pattern disappears. Passing zero and first orders restores a periodic modulation; passing more orders sharpens the edges. Thus the loss of fine structure is explained by rejected diffraction orders, not by a geometrical inability to draw narrow lines. The field-transfer cutoff differs from the twice-as-large incoherent intensity-transfer cutoff.[ref]

Increasing the angular acceptance can collect higher spatial frequencies, but rays at large angles and away from the optical axis are harder to bring to the same focus. Spherical aberration, coma, astigmatism and field curvature can degrade contrast across a large exposure field. The depth of focus also decreases approximately as λ/NA2, making wafer height errors more important. A larger geometrical aperture is therefore not automatically better over the whole wafer field.
Immersion lithography raises NA=nsinθ by increasing the refractive index of the medium between the final lens and wafer. A suitable UV-transparent liquid permits a larger numerical aperture without increasing the ray angle by the same factor, easing the purely geometrical demand on the lens. Lens design and focus control remain necessary; immersion does not abolish aberrations. The liquid must also be compatible with the resist and free of bubbles and contamination, and absorption at 193nm must be low.

Cyclodextrins are truncated-cone-shaped hosts that form host–guest complexes. Their hydroxyl-rich exterior interacts favourably with water, while the internal cavity is relatively nonpolar and can accommodate a suitably sized organic guest. The six-, seven- and eight-unit rings have different cavity sizes, so shape and size selectivity matter. Binding can involve London dispersion forces, displacement of cavity water and changes in solvation; no covalent modification of the guest is necessary.
An odorous organic guest can be included in the cavity, decreasing its free concentration and hence its tendency to enter the vapour phase. The same inclusion chemistry can increase the apparent aqueous solubility of a poorly soluble compound, and reversible association allows release when conditions or concentrations change. In a separation, guests with different association constants partition differently between free and bound states; a suitably immobilized host can therefore act as a selective stationary phase.
The unifying mechanism is reversible molecular inclusion in a size-selective cavity. These are chemical explanations of binding and partitioning. Biological action, therapeutic efficacy and physiological delivery mechanisms are outside the present nonbiology scope.

The gas-phase attraction is a cation–π interaction. The positively charged ion interacts with benzene's electron distribution and polarizes the aromatic molecule. An orientation above the ring face allows stabilization without a conventional covalent bond. A modest association constant reflects the balance of that attraction against the entropy cost of joining two independent species, at the stated gas-phase standard state.
In water, a small ion such as Li+ is strongly solvated. Forming a direct benzene complex requires rearranging or losing favourable ion–water contacts; benzene is also solvated, and the solvent screens electrostatic interactions. The relevant solution free energy therefore includes desolvation and solvent reorganization, not just the attractive energy of the isolated pair. The gas-phase association constant cannot be transferred to water without changing this thermodynamic cycle and the standard states. The interaction is not forbidden in solution, but it need not give appreciable direct complexation under the specified conditions.

The receptor is a triazolophane with a relatively rigid central cavity. Its inward-facing triazole and phenylene C–H groups act cooperatively as hydrogen bond donors to an anion. The supplied preference is Cl−>Br−≫F−>I−, so it is not a simple ranking by charge density or basicity.
Chloride has the best fit for several favourable C–H···anion contacts simultaneously; bromide is close enough in size to retain strong binding. Fluoride is too small to engage the whole donor array optimally, and its desolvation penalty can further oppose binding in solution. Iodide is too large for the cavity and cannot sit in the same favourable geometry. Molecular preorganization reduces the conformational penalty for binding a matched guest, but makes the cavity less adaptable to mismatched ones.
The exact solvent is not specified in this question, so a quantitative hydration argument would be unwarranted. The structure and observed nonmonotonic order support size complementarity plus cooperative C–H hydrogen bonding, with solvation modifying the measured constants. The original receptor study experimentally demonstrated halide size selectivity and tunability through the phenylene substituents.[ref]

A useful aqueous anode must deliver the desired oxidation current while limiting parasitic corrosion and hydrogen evolution. Thermodynamics sets the driving force, while activation overpotential, surface films and mass transport control the rate.
For zinc, the acid calculation favours dissolution with hydrogen evolution, but a substantial kinetic barrier to hydrogen evolution on a clean zinc surface can slow this loss. Impurities and catalytic inclusions can increase it. In alkaline electrolyte, zinc can be useful in batteries, but the unit-activity Zn2+ calculation is incomplete: soluble zincate and zinc oxide or hydroxide alter the relevant equilibrium. A surface film can protect against corrosion or obstruct useful discharge, depending on its conductivity and transport properties.
For lead, acid corrosion has a much smaller driving force in the stipulated model, and slow hydrogen kinetics can permit practical use. The electrolyte anion matters: a lead sulfate layer in a lead–acid system has a different role from arbitrary soluble lead salts. In alkaline media, hydroxide formation, soluble complexes and passivation again need consideration before drawing a practical conclusion from the free-ion potential.
Metallic lithium is unsuitable as an exposed anode in an ordinary aqueous electrolyte. Its strongly favourable reaction with water overwhelms the argument for simply relying on a hydrogen overpotential. A lithium-ion host electrode is a different material and must be judged by its own operating potential and interfacial chemistry.

A conventional capacitor stores separated electronic charge across a dielectric, with capacitance set approximately by C=εA/d. An electric double-layer capacitor stores charge at the interface between a porous electronic conductor and an ionic electrolyte. Electrolyte counterions screen charge on the electrode over a very small interfacial separation, and a porous electrode provides a large accessible area. The separator prevents an electronic short while permitting ionic transport; the two electrode interfaces form series capacitances.
Pseudocapacitance is charge storage by fast, reversible Faradaic processes that give a capacitor-like relation between stored charge and potential over a useful range. Examples include surface redox and suitably rapid insertion processes. It is distinct from purely electrostatic double-layer charging. Merely observing a redox peak does not establish pseudocapacitive behaviour: a slow two-phase battery reaction can remain battery-like even when its electrode is nanostructured.

For constant current and nearly constant capacitance,
I=CdtdV​,V(t)=V(0)±C∣I∣​t.
(13)
An ideal supercapacitor therefore has approximately linear charge and discharge ramps, making a triangular voltage–time trace. Series resistance adds an immediate voltage step when current starts and a step of magnitude 2∣I∣Rs​ when it reverses directly from +I to −I. Leakage and voltage-dependent capacitance introduce curvature.
A secondary battery instead follows electrode chemical potentials as its state of charge changes; many chemistries have broad plateaus or sloping plateaus, with polarization separating the charge and discharge curves. This distinction is typical rather than universal: some batteries have steeply sloping profiles, while strongly potential-dependent pseudocapacitive materials can depart substantially from a triangle.

Csp​(V)=m1​dVdQ​​,[Csp​]=Fg−1.

Porous carbon emphasizes power and cycle life. Activated carbon, graphene-derived structures and nanotubes can provide high accessible area, electronic conduction and relatively reversible double-layer charging. Cost and stability can be favourable. However, inaccessible micropores, tortuous ionic paths, poor packing density and surface functional groups can reduce useful rate performance or volumetric energy density. Area measured by gas adsorption is not automatically accessible to electrolyte ions.
Metal oxides can add substantial redox charge storage. A suitable oxide offers high pseudocapacitance, but its electronic conductivity, ion-transport distance and electrochemical stability can limit power. Some high-performing compositions are expensive or contain scarce elements. Structural changes and dissolution can shorten life. Conductive composites and thin active layers help, but adding inactive support dilutes device-level energy storage.
Conducting polymers combine redox activity with electronic conduction and flexible processing. Their oxidation state and counterion population change during charging. Repeated ion and solvent uptake causes swelling and shrinkage, which can crack or delaminate an electrode and shorten cycle life. Conductivity is also state-dependent, and potential windows are constrained by overoxidation or reduction. A practical comparison must include both electrodes, electrolyte, packaging and the usable voltage: E≃CV2/2 makes voltage stability as important as a large gravimetric capacitance.

In physical vapor deposition, material is physically released from a source and transported to the substrate, where it condenses. Three examples are thermal evaporative deposition, electron-beam evaporation and sputtering. Their angular distributions, source heating and energetic bombardment differ, even though all belong to the physical-deposition family.
In chemical vapor deposition, volatile reactants undergo chemical reactions that produce a solid film at or near the substrate, with gaseous products removed. Examples are atmospheric-pressure thermal CVD, low-pressure CVD and plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition. These are three established process variants, distinguished by pressure and activation method. Surface chemistry, precursor delivery and temperature determine growth, rather than condensation alone.
Atomic layer deposition is a related cyclic surface-reaction method, but need not be counted as a separate conventional CVD example to satisfy the requested three. Reactive sputtering can involve chemistry, so the boundary is best explained by the dominant source and transport mechanism rather than by claiming that a PVD chamber can contain no chemical reactions.

An organic liquid electrolyte gives good wetting and relatively high room-temperature ionic conductivity. A dissolved lithium salt supplies mobile ions, while a suitable solvent mixture supports operation beyond the narrow water-stability window. For one homogeneous thermoelectric material, the dimensional thermoelectric figure of merit is
Z=κS2σ​=ρκS2​​,[Z]=K−1.
(30)
The dimensionless quantity is ZT. The Seebeck coefficient S measures the open-circuit voltage response to a temperature difference and reflects the energy asymmetry of charge-carrier transport. The electrical conductivity σ depends on carrier density and mobility and sets electrical loss; ρ=1/σ. The thermal conductivity is κ=κe​+κl​, with contributions from carriers and phonons, and represents unwanted heat leakage.
Increasing carrier concentration usually raises σ while reducing ∣S∣ once transport becomes strongly degenerate. The electronic part of κ also tends to rise, approximately through the Wiedemann-Franz law where applicable. This competition produces an intermediate optimum, commonly in heavily doped semiconductors, rather than in a very resistive insulator or an ordinary high-carrier-density metal. The optimum depends on band structure and scattering; the schematic is not a universal measured concentration curve.
