Three credible architectures are a welded silver nanowire network; a thin silver nanowire network with a conducting polymer overcoat; and a nanocrystalline silver grid formed from nanoparticle ink. Silver provides low intrinsic resistivity. A sparse long-wire network can be both flexible and highly transmitting if its junctions are well connected. A thin, optically weak conducting polymer overcoat can bridge junctions and smooth the surface, provided its added absorption and moisture sensitivity are controlled. A fine grid offers a more directly designed optical/electrical trade-off and does not rely on operating near random percolation.
For a quantitative grid feasibility screen, take pitch p=5 μm, line width w=100 nm and metal thickness t=200 nm. An opaque-line square-grid estimate gives Tgrid​≃(1−w/p)2=0.9604. With a transmitting polymer substrate of 0.95, the combined estimate is 0.912. The sheet resistance is approximately R□​=ρeff​p/(wt). Even an effective silver resistivity four times 1.6×10−8 Ωm gives R□​≃16 Ω, with a coating thinner than 400 nm. There are about 20 mesh periods across the stripe, making the finite-width design more credible than a pitch comparable with its whole width.
These are feasible material/process choices, not guaranteed specifications for every film carrying those names. For the two nanowire architectures, choose wire dimensions, loading and junction treatment to pass the same finished-stripe measurements; the hybrid's optical budget must include its overcoat. Measure transmission at 550 nm with its reference stated, sheet resistance, profile thickness, bend-cycle stability and pattern continuity. Bare nanotube or graphene networks should not be assumed to meet this demanding resistance–transmission combination without additional metallic conduction or other demonstrated improvement.
