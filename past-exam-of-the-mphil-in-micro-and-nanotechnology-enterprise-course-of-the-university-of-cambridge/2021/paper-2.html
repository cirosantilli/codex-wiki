Rolling graphene into a single-walled carbon nanotube quantizes the circumferential wave vector, leaving a set of one-dimensional longitudinal subbands. At each subband edge the group velocity tends to zero, producing a Van Hove singularity in the density of states. For a simple gapped Dirac subband, the contribution scales as ∣E∣/E2−Δj2​​ outside its edge. At low temperature the valence states below the Fermi level are occupied and the conduction states above it are empty. A semiconducting tube has a gap; an ideal metallic tube has gapless channels as well as higher-subband singularities.
For light polarized along the tube, the strongest allowed interband transitions connect corresponding subbands while approximately conserving axial wave vector and circumferential angular momentum. The large joint density of states makes distinct absorption resonances, conventionally labelled E11​,E22​,…. Strong electron–hole interactions produce bound excitons, so the observed optical resonances differ from the noninteracting band separations. Defects, doping, bundling and dielectric screening change peak energies and oscillator strengths. The original schematic below depicts the subband-edge structure, not measured spectra.

One application is a semiconducting nanotube transistor array. Removing metallic tubes prevents conducting bridges that would bypass gate control; a narrow chirality distribution also narrows the spread of band gaps and switching behaviour. High charge carrier mobility and thin flexible channels are attractive, but contact resistance, tube alignment, residual metallic contamination, dielectric traps and the cost/yield of sorting still limit uniform large-area circuits.
A second is a wavelength-selective near-infrared photodetector or emitter. A selected chirality concentrates absorption and emission around its characteristic nanotube optical transitions, avoiding the broad mixed-tube response. Optical selectivity can be tailored by choosing the species. Limitations include low absolute emission yield, dark exciton states, contact-related recombination, environmental spectral shifts and quenching by bundles or metallic contaminants. Optical and electrical characterization after device processing is needed; the sorting label alone does not guarantee device performance.

Prepare size-characterized gold-particle ensembles, heat them on a calibrated in-situ electron diffraction stage, and follow the loss of sharp fcc diffraction features and appearance of liquid-like diffuse scattering. Measure the particle size distribution rather than treating a polydisperse ensemble as one diameter. Temperature-dependent weakening of a diffraction peak alone is insufficient: thermal vibrations, particle motion, coalescence, evaporation and beam damage can also reduce intensity. Low-dose repeats, heating/cooling runs, size checks before and after heating, a bulk-like reference and support-dependent controls help distinguish melting from those effects. Electron diffraction measurements on gold particles down to about 2 nm are a classic demonstration.[ref]
The physical origin is the large interface contribution to free energy at small radius. A curved solid–liquid interface raises the solid's thermodynamic equilibrium chemical potential relative to the flat-interface case, so coexistence occurs below the bulk melting temperature. Reduced coordination and surface disorder provide a microscopic interpretation, but the quantitative continuum relation is a free energy balance, not simply a claim that every surface atom has a universal lower melting point.

Let gl​−gs​ be the liquid-minus-solid free energy density at the same temperature. Near the bulk coexistence temperature Tm​, linearization gives
gl​−gs​≃ΔsV​(Tm​−T)=Tm​ΔHV​​ΔT.
(3)
For a sphere, an interface displacement changes area and volume with dA/dV=2/r. Equality of the bulk driving force and the capillary cost at a curved solid–liquid interface therefore gives gl​−gs​=2γ/r∗. Equivalently, differentiating 4πr2γ−(4π/3)r3(gl​−gs​) gives the same stationary radius. Combining the expressions yields
ΔT=r∗ΔHV​2γTm​​.​
(4)
This form of the Gibbs-Thomson effect assumes isotropic interfacial energy, a spherical interface, bulk-like latent heat per volume and a near-coexistence free energy expansion. More detailed free-particle models must distinguish solid–vapor, liquid–vapor and solid–liquid energies.

For an isolated easy-axis ferromagnet undergoing coherent reversal, the ideal low-temperature loop is approximately rectangular, switching between ±Ms​ at ±Hc​. It has remanent Magnetization and a large response near switching. An ideal compensated antiferromagnet has essentially no remanence and a small, approximately reversible linear net Magnetization M=χAF​H at fields far below its exchange/spin-flop scales. Strong antiferromagnetic exchange makes substantial canting costly, so its low-field magnetic susceptibility is small relative to the ferromagnet's unsaturated switching response.
This comparison does not say the ferromagnet has a larger differential slope at every field: on its saturated branches dM/dH can also be small. Nor does it rule out spin-flop transitions at much larger fields, outside the sketched range. The original plot uses arbitrary normalized scales.

Heat the bilayer into a window TN​<T<Tc​, apply an in-plane magnetic field sufficient to align the ferromagnet, and cool through the antiferromagnet's ordering/blocking range while retaining the field. The ferromagnet supplies a preferred interfacial spin direction as the antiferromagnet orders. Sufficient antiferromagnetic anisotropy then pins part of the interfacial spin configuration, producing unidirectional magnetic anisotropy rather than merely uniaxial magnetic anisotropy in the ferromagnet.
Measure a full major hysteresis loop after removing the cooling field, and compare with the isolated ferromagnet, a zero-field-cooled bilayer and the result of reversing the cooling-field direction. Those controls separate a true loop-centre shift from a changed coercive width, minor-loop artefacts or trapped applied field. Write the descending and ascending switching fields as H−​ and H+​. The exchange bias loop centre and magnetic coercivity half-width are
HE​=2H+​+H−​​,Hc​=2H+​−H−​​.​
(11)
With exchange bias, a field-cooled bilayer has HE​=0, often a shift opposite to the cooling field for the conventional negative-bias case shown in the diagram. The sign is not universal; it depends on interfacial coupling and preparation. A simple rigid-pinning estimate is HE​∼−Jint​/(μ0​Ms​tF​).
Cooling farther below the blocking range usually strengthens antiferromagnetic anisotropy and suppresses thermal relaxation, so the magnitude of the shift and often the coercive width grow. For this self-assembly into a colloidal crystal, four useful elements are building blocks, interactions, environment and reversible dynamics. The building blocks here are nearly monodisperse polymer spheres; their shape and size distribution constrain the accessible packing. Their interactions include excluded volume, Van der Waals forces and electrostatic or steric stabilization, which set the free energy landscape. The aqueous environment supplies temperature, viscosity, ionic strength, confinement and concentration controls. Finally, Brownian motion and rotation must let particles encounter one another and rearrange or detach from incorrect contacts. Attractions that bind every collision irreversibly favour an amorphous aggregate rather than an annealed colloidal crystal. These four categories include both thermodynamic selection and the transport/kinetic access needed to realize it.

Increase particle volume fraction slowly by controlled evaporation or gentle concentration until ordering becomes favourable, while preserving sufficient colloidal repulsion to prevent irreversible sticking. Hard-sphere-like monodisperse suspensions can crystallize by packing entropy; weak, reversible attractions can also favour ordered assembly if they allow defect correction. A two-dimensional electron gas is confined into discrete subbands perpendicular to an interface while retaining nearly free motion in its plane. It is not a literal zero-thickness electron sheet. In a GaAs/AlGaAs semiconductor heterostructure, the band offset and self-consistent band bending confine electrons on the GaAs side. This is transverse magnetic focusing. The depleted gates form reflecting boundaries and two constrictions. A narrow quantum point contact collimates carriers into cyclotron trajectories with cyclotron radius rc​=m∗vF​/(e∣B∣)=ℏkF​/(e∣B∣). When an integer number of semicircular skipping arcs links the two openings, the collector couples particularly strongly to the injector population. A floating voltage contact then changes its electrochemical potential to satisfy zero net current, producing a peak in the measured nonlocal voltage. Sweeping the field changes the arc diameter, moving the focused population into or away from the collector.

The effect needs ballistic electron transport, sufficiently narrow openings and a smooth boundary. Electron phase coherence can add interference structure, but the basic geometrical focusing condition is semiclassical.[ref]

Reversing conventional current reverses the occupation perturbation and voltage sign, but does not reverse the curvature of an individual electron. Thus the standard injector-to-collector explanation describes the focusing mechanism, while the asserted signed voltage increase requires a consistent field/lead convention. Brownian motion is the irregular thermal displacement produced by fluctuating molecular forces from the surrounding liquid. In the overdamped regime its long-time displacement is diffusive, with mean squared displacement proportional to time. It brings nanoscale building blocks into contact and lets them sample orientations and escape weak incorrect contacts. Interparticle forces bias this exploration toward lower-free-energy structures; thermal motion alone does not select a particular crystal. Strong irreversible attraction, high viscosity or glassy arrest can stop the defect correction needed for successful self-assembly.

For a no-slip spherical particle in a Newtonian fluid at low Reynolds number, Stokes law gives ζ=6πηa. The fluctuation-dissipation theorem then gives the Stokes–Einstein relation D=kB​T/ζ. Using the conventional radial Brownian displacement in three dimensions,
drms​=⟨∣Δr∣2⟩​=6Dt​=πηakB​Tt​​.​

Here kB​ is the Boltzmann constant, T absolute temperature, η dynamic viscosity, a particle radius and t elapsed time. Larger thermal forcing raises diffusion; larger viscous drag reduces it. The formula requires times long relative to momentum relaxation and neglects walls, interactions and external drift.
The word “average” needs a statistic: the vector mean is zero, while the mean radial displacement for Gaussian three-dimensional diffusion is ⟨∣Δr∣⟩=4Dt/π​, not 6Dt​. Both scale as t1/2. Neither is the total length of a microscopic random path.

The Flory interaction parameter χAB​ measures the effective free energy penalty for unlike A–B contacts relative to like contacts, and N is the total number of statistical segments per chain. Their product is the segregation strength: contact-energy preference competes with the conformational entropy cost of separating covalently joined blocks. N does not grow during solvent evaporation. For an approximately nonselective solvent, increasing polymer concentration instead increases the effective importance of A–B contacts, so a roughly fixed-composition path moves from weak toward stronger effective segregation.
The diagram's labels are DIS: disordered; S: block-copolymer sphere phase; H: hexagonal cylinder block-copolymer phase; G: double gyroid; L: lamellar microphase. At fA​=0.15, A is the minority component and B forms the continuous matrix. Reading the supplied diagram vertically at this composition gives disorder initially, a sphere region when segregation first becomes sufficient, and a cylinder region if sufficiently strong segregation is reached within the displayed range. The thin gyroid and central lamellar regions are not the thermodynamic equilibrium path at this composition. The dry endpoint cannot be fixed numerically without the final χAB​N value. The cited theoretical calculation determines thermodynamic equilibrium phase stability, not a universal evaporation trajectory.[ref]
Crucially, at least one block is glassy at room temperature. Solvent can initially plasticize it, but solvent loss can raise its effective glass-transition temperature above the processing temperature. Chain relaxation then becomes too slow to equilibrate. Even slow evaporation can therefore freeze disorder, spheres, cylinders or another transient morphology before the thermodynamic equilibrium dry state is reached. Solvent concentration, annealing time and segmental mobility must be followed alongside the phase diagram.

One explanation is block-copolymer kinetic trapping. A solvent-swollen transient sheet structure can be immobilized when a block vitrifies during drying; accurately measured block fractions do not guarantee thermodynamic equilibrium. Thermal annealing above the relevant glass transition, or a controlled nonselective solvent-vapour anneal followed by structural characterization, can test whether the sheets relax toward the bulk composition-appropriate morphology.
A second is surface or confinement selection of lamellar microphase. The diagram describes a bulk reference model. A thin film, preferential wetting at the substrate/free surface or a commensurate confining thickness adds interfacial and geometrical free energy terms that can favour layered arrangements even at an asymmetric global fraction. Unequal layer thicknesses can accommodate fA​=0.15; equal block fractions are not required for every layered metastable or confined structure. Varying film thickness and surface chemistry, and comparing bulk-like and confined samples after adequate annealing, distinguishes this explanation from kinetic arrest.
