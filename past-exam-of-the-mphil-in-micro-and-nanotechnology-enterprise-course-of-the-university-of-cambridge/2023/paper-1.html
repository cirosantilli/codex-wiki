Moiré fringes arise when two overlapping periodic structures contribute closely spaced reciprocal lattice vectors to the transmission electron microscopy image. Their relative phase varies slowly across the image, producing a coarse modulation with a period much larger than either atomic spacing. The structures may have different lattice spacings, a small relative rotation, or both. The modulation is an interference or beat phenomenon; its spacing is not itself a new atomic-plane spacing. Its orientation is perpendicular to the difference vector of the two contributing projected reciprocal-lattice vectors.

For each spacing pair, the largest geometrical period occurs with parallel projected reciprocal vectors. The closest pair is dCu​=0.208nm and dsubstrate​=0.212nm, giving
Dmax​=0.212−0.2080.208×0.212​nm=11.024nm≃11.0nm.​
(2)
However, the crystal is only 10nm across. It contains at most 10/11.024≃0.91 of this period, so the largest formal period cannot appear as a repeated fringe train inside one crystal. It can instead give a broad variation of contrast. A period of order the particle diameter is the largest directly sampled scale, and even that is poorly constrained by one particle.
The distinction matters if “see” means a complete repeat. Of the exactly parallel spacing pairs, the largest other period is approximately 2.09nm from the 0.208 and 0.231nm pair. If relative rotation is continuously variable, the closest pair can instead be rotated slightly to shorten its period below 10nm, so 2.09nm is not a universal maximum visible period for arbitrary orientations.

A coarse fringe in a high-resolution transmission electron microscopy image need not directly identify the lattice spacing of a new phase. Check whether overlapping crystals, a particle and its substrate, or differently oriented domains can produce the corresponding moiré fringe. Compare the underlying reciprocal-space peaks and their difference, use the particle boundary to constrain how many periods are actually sampled, and examine neighbouring or differently oriented regions. For the closest pair here, fitting a periodicity from less than one complete period is particularly unreliable. Distinguishing lattice contrast from a beat envelope prevents misidentifying a phase or over-interpreting local strain.

With suitable atomic force microscopy feedback gains, the trace follows the accessible surface while maintaining the chosen oscillation-amplitude setpoint. The measured trench is narrower than the accessible opening because the finite tip encounters an edge before its apex reaches that edge's true lateral position. For an ideal tip flank angle α=15∘ relative to the tip axis and height change h=1.3μm, the geometrical setback is htanα≃0.35μm per side.

In tapping-mode atomic force microscopy, the cantilever is driven near a resonance and its oscillation amplitude is monitored. Tip-surface interaction changes that amplitude. A controller compares it with a chosen setpoint and moves the vertical piezoelectric actuator to reduce the error. The recorded actuator displacement supplies the topography channel. A smaller amplitude during approach commonly triggers withdrawal and an increased amplitude during separation triggers approach, with the actual feedback sign set by the instrument's operating convention. Correct tracking requires enough response bandwidth without destabilizing the loop.

A conventional tapping tip tapers toward its apex, so its shank contacts an overhanging feature before its apex can probe underneath it. Critical-dimension atomic force microscopy instead uses a calibrated flared or laterally extended tip, with a relatively narrow neck and side-sensitive control. The lateral extension can contact a reentrant sidewall while the neck clears the upper edge. The measured profile still includes the tip's finite shape and must be interpreted with a tip calibration; changing the tip does not automatically eliminate all convolution errors.[ref]

An on-axis point illuminator increases spatial coherence. It is not a point object, and it does not remove diffraction. For an ideal aberration-free pupil, coherent imaging is linear in field amplitude and has cutoff
νc​=λNA​​.
(10)
For a real amplitude grating U(x)=a[1+mcos(2πνx)], with 0≤m≤1 and all three field orders inside this pupil, the intensity contrast is C=2m/(1+m2). Flat coherent field transfer preserves this contrast; weak input modulation does not itself meet a high resist contrast threshold. Assume a sufficiently high-contrast amplitude mask: for example, m=1 gives C=1, exceeding both resist thresholds. Under this assumption, both resists have the same limiting equal-line/space width
wc​=2νc​1​=2NAλ​=241nm​,Pc​=483nm.
(11)
This is an approach to the cutoff from below; finite dose latitude, aberrations and a real binary mask can make usable resolution worse. A general coherent intensity image cannot be obtained by applying an incoherent intensity MTF unchanged: I=∣h∗U∣2, rather than I=∣h∣2∗∣U∣2.[ref]
With ν0​=2NA/λ, this coherent cutoff is 0.5ν0​: the low-contrast resist improves from roughly 302 to 241nm, while the high-contrast resist worsens from roughly 201 to 241nm. Coherence improves contrast within its passband but reduces the ultimate bandwidth relative to incoherent illumination.
With the literal Rayleigh-spacing normalization ν0​=1/RR​, the same physical cutoff is instead 0.61ν0​, not 0.5ν0​. The corresponding widths from the preceding part would improve from roughly 368 and 245nm toward 241nm. The cutoff in physical units is unambiguous under the stated ideal on-axis model; the claimed direction and size of a change inferred from the plotted normalized axis depend on its definition. Off-axis illumination, partial coherence and arbitrary phase objects require a fuller imaging model.

A simple chemical vapor deposition model separates transport through a gas boundary layer, with coefficient hm​, from surface reaction, with coefficient ks​:
J=hm​(Cb​−Cs​)=ks​Cs​,J=hm​+ks​hm​ks​​Cb​​.
(13)
For ks​≫hm​, growth is mass-transfer-limited deposition. A typical cold-wall, flowing reactor heats the substrate strongly while keeping most chamber walls cooler. A shaped inlet, controlled flow and tilted or rotating susceptor help control boundary-layer thickness and precursor depletion. The essential design problem is delivering a similar flux everywhere; heating a surface still further gives little benefit once delivery limits growth.
For ks​≪hm​, growth is reaction-limited deposition. A hot-wall, low-pressure batch tube can hold many wafers in a nearly uniform-temperature zone. Faster gas transport makes surface concentrations more uniform, while slow chemistry reduces consumption before precursors reach downstream surfaces. Temperature uniformity is particularly important because surface rates can depend strongly on temperature.
These are illustrative architectures, not a rule that wall type alone determines the regime. Temperature, pressure, flow and precursor chemistry jointly set ks​/hm​.

At fixed reactant partial pressures and temperature, their ideal-gas bulk concentrations Ci​=pi​/(RT) remain approximately fixed. Lowering total pressure means removing diluent rather than automatically lowering the reactant concentration.
In the continuum molecular-diffusion regime, gas diffusivities scale approximately as D∝1/Ptotal​. Delivery through a comparable boundary layer is therefore faster, increasing hm​ relative to ks​. The system tends toward reaction-limited operation, where concentration differences between substrate locations are smaller. This commonly improves thickness uniformity and access to recessed surfaces.
The qualification is important: changing pressure can also change flow velocity and the boundary layer, gas-phase chemistry and precursor depletion. In narrow structures at low pressure, Knudsen transport limits the simple 1/P improvement. Improved transport does not correct a large substrate-temperature gradient in a strongly temperature-sensitive reaction-limited process.

In evaporative deposition, atoms travel mainly along straight trajectories from a compact source. For an ideal cosine-emitting source below a parallel wafer, a point at lateral radius r and source separation H receives approximately
F(r)∝R2cosθs​cosθw​​=(H2+r2)2H2​.
(14)
Consequently F(r)/F(0)=[1+(r/H)2]−2. Increasing H makes arrival angles and distances more similar across a fixed wafer, improving uniformity. But the central flux falls as H−2 for a fixed source output, so the deposition rate decreases and less evaporated material is collected.
Rotation averages azimuthal variations, and source shaping or multiple sources can help, but simple rotation does not remove a radial flux gradient. Raising source output can partly compensate at the cost of source heating, consumption and possible material limitations. The trade-off follows from geometry rather than an intrinsic prohibition on simultaneously optimizing a more elaborate system.

Zero step coverage means that a sidewall or hidden surface receives essentially no film even though a horizontal reference surface is coated. Deposition shadowing occurs when an upper edge intercepts trajectories that would otherwise reach a lower region. A directional high-vacuum evaporant flux can therefore coat a feature top while leaving vertical or reentrant walls uncoated.
Sputtering generally releases material from an extended target with a broad angular distribution; gas collisions can further redistribute arrival directions. More oblique trajectories can reach sidewalls, so the extreme zero-coverage behavior of an ideal collimated evaporant beam is reduced.
The absolute claim that these effects never occur in sputtering is too strong. Low-pressure directional sputtering and high-aspect-ratio or reentrant features can still exhibit shadowing and poor coverage. Broader incidence improves access; it does not make a physical deposition process universally conformal.

The left-hand open quinoid dianion is pink/purple; the right-hand closed lactone is colorless. In the colored form, a longer conjugated electronic system extends through the quinoid framework. The resulting lower-energy electronic transitions can absorb visible light, producing the observed complementary color.

A reference electrode should provide a stable, reproducible potential from a well-defined reversible equilibrium, with fixed relevant activities. It should pass negligible measuring current, resist contamination and drift, and avoid changing the sulfate sample through incompatible ions or reactions.
Silver is suitable when coupled to a sparingly soluble silver salt and a controlled filling electrolyte; a silver sulfate reference electrode avoids introducing chloride into a sulfate system. Mercury is also electrochemically suitable in a mercury/mercurous-sulfate reference system, although its toxicity and handling requirements make silver attractive where practical.
Sodium is unsuitable because it reacts with water rather than maintaining a quiet metal/ion reference equilibrium. Calcium and strontium are likewise too reactive in water. Magnesium is not a dependable reference simply because it sometimes reacts slowly: corrosion, oxide/hydroxide films and mixed potentials make its open-circuit potential dependent on surface history and solution conditions. A relatively inert-looking surface is not equivalent to a reproducible reversible electrode.
Even a suitable reference is not just an arbitrary piece of metal immersed in the sample. The metal, equilibrium salt, controlled electrolyte and liquid junction together define the reference system.

i OurBigBook.com
At fixed discharge current, delivered voltage can be represented as
V=Eeq​(Q)−IR−ηact​−ηconc​−ηphase​,
(24)
where Q is discharged charge and the terms denote distinct physical losses. A typical trace has an initial drop from open circuit, an approximately flat useful region and a final decline toward cutoff. Its exact shape depends on the cell chemistry and current; not every battery has an equally flat plateau.
Four distinguishable contributions are:
  • Ohmic polarization: electronic, ionic and contact resistance cause a rapid IR drop on applying current. This need not wait for a concentration gradient to develop.
  • Activation overpotential: finite electrode-reaction kinetics require a departure from equilibrium to sustain charge transfer. It is important when the exchange current is small.
  • Concentration overpotential: electrolyte transport and diffusion within active particles can deplete reactants or accumulate products near reaction sites. These losses often grow near the end of discharge or at high current.
  • Phase-transformation polarization: nucleation and growth of a new phase can require extra driving force, produce an initial voltage dip or cause hysteresis. It is especially relevant to conversion and two-phase electrodes, rather than a universal independent loss in every chemistry.
Terminology sometimes groups solid-state transport or phase-change effects differently. The important distinction is the mechanism, not counting the same diffusion loss under two names. A falling equilibrium voltage as composition changes is also not, by itself, polarization.
