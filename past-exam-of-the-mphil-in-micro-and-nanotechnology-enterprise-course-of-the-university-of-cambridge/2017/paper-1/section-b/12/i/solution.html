A material exhibiting piezoelectricity converts cyclic mechanical stress into electric charge; its mechanical and electrical boundary conditions jointly set the extractable energy. piezoelectric ceramics such as lead zirconate titanate are stiff and often have large charge coefficients and electromechanical coupling, but are brittle and can be difficult to match to large-strain flexible motion. piezoelectric polymers such as polyvinylidene fluoride are compliant, light and compatible with flexible large-area films. Their lower charge coefficient is partly offset in voltage-sensitive applications by lower permittivity: the voltage coefficient is g=d/ϵ.
Ceramics often favor strong charge generation and coupling; polymers favor flexibility and strain accommodation. The larger open-circuit voltage of a low-capacitance film does not itself imply more useful power. For a simple fixed-stress comparison the electrical energy scale involves d2/ϵ, while coupling also depends on mechanical compliance. At fixed imposed strain, different stiffnesses change the available mechanical energy. resonance tuning, fatigue, mechanical mounting, rectifier losses and load matching can reverse a simplistic material ranking; compare complete devices under the same actual vibration source.
