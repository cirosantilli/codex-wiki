A La Mer diagram plots the concentration of growth species against time. Label the equilibrium solubility Cs​ and a higher effective nucleation threshold Cn​. Generate growth species until the concentration briefly exceeds Cn​; a short burst nucleation interval then creates seeds. Consumption brings the concentration below Cn​ while leaving it above Cs​, allowing existing particles to grow without substantial continuing nucleation. Rapid mixing, a short seed-formation interval and a common growth period help produce a narrow particle size distribution. Suppress secondary nucleation and aggregation, then quench or consume the remaining growth species when the desired size is reached. The threshold is an operational kinetic scale, not a sharp new equilibrium phase boundary.
Reducing the solid-liquid interfacial tension lowers the classical barrier, which scales as γ3 at fixed supersaturation. More seeds can then form at a smaller supersaturation; at fixed total precipitated mass, more seeds generally mean a smaller mean final particle size. A narrower distribution is not guaranteed: if nucleation continues throughout growth, particles have different ages and the distribution can broaden. A brief well-separated burst and uniform subsequent growth are the controlling requirements, not low interfacial tension alone. The historical model concerns separated nucleation/growth in sulfur hydrosols, rather than a universal demonstrated mechanism for every nanoparticle synthesis.[ref]
