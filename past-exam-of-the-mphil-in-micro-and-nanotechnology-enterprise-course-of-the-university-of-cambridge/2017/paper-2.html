A decahedral nanoparticle is a multiply twinned nanoparticle: five fcc-like tetrahedral domains share a common fivefold axis and meet on coherent twin planes. The angle of an ideal fcc tetrahedral wedge is arccos(1/3)=70.53∘, so five wedges leave about 7.36∘ of angular deficit. Elastic distortion and defects accommodate closure. The external shape can be a pentagonal bipyramid or a truncated variant; it is not one unstrained, periodically repeated fcc crystal.
An icosahedral nanoparticle comprises twenty tetrahedral domains arranged around a common centre, with twenty approximately triangular outer facets, twelve vertices and thirty edges. Five domains meet around each radial fivefold direction. Coherent twinning exposes many low-energy close-packed facets, but fivefold symmetry cannot tile ordinary three-dimensional space periodically without strain. The competition between favourable surface/twin energies and volume-dependent elastic strain explains why such morphologies are especially plausible for small particles.
These labels describe the observed domain geometry; cobalt can adopt different crystal phases, so identifying fcc-like twinning also requires diffraction rather than assuming every cobalt particle is bulk fcc.

In cluster-beam deposition, evaporated or sputtered cobalt atoms cool in an aggregation gas, nucleate clusters and are carried to a substrate. Source temperature, gas pressure, aggregation length and residence time tune the mean size and distribution. A mass selector can narrow the delivered size range, at the cost of transmitted flux; an unfiltered beam does not automatically produce monodisperse particles.
Shape control is less direct. Growth kinetics and cluster temperature can favour particular structures, while multiple morphologies can coexist at one size. Landing energy, substrate interactions and post-deposition annealing can change the delivered shape or cause coalescence. Soft landing helps preserve gas-phase clusters. In solution, ligands, selective facet binding, seed-mediated growth and precursor feeding can give stronger chemical control over shape and size, but introduce ligand/solvent residues and require purification. Neither method guarantees a unique shape merely by selecting particle size.

A bulk fcc atom has twelve nearest neighbours, whereas surface atoms are undercoordinated: ideal (111) terrace atoms have nine and (100) terrace atoms eight. Steps, kinks, edges and corners can have still lower coordination and different bond-angle environments. These sites change electronic structure, adsorption energies and activation barriers, making geometrically different sites catalytically inequivalent.
In a Pt-Pd particle, a site's neighbours may be Pt, Pd or a mixture; surface segregation means surface composition need not equal overall composition. Alloying also changes strain and electronic interactions. Surface relaxation, reconstruction, adsorbed ligands, oxide or contamination can further alter accessibility and chemical state. A high nominal Pt fraction therefore does not establish either accessible Pt sites or a particular catalytic activity.

Use complementary composition, spatial and population measurements:
  • Insufficient Pt: digest representative aliquots and measure calibrated ICP-OES or ICP-MS to obtain bulk Pt/Pd ratio and trace Fe. Blanks, recovery checks and standards control contamination and matrix effects. This is sensitive and quantitative but destructive and supplies no particle-by-particle spatial information.
  • Poor Pt-Pd mixing: use STEM-EDS maps or line profiles of many individual particles. Characteristic X-ray energies identify the elements; background subtraction and calibrated sensitivity factors permit local composition estimates. HAADF-STEM supplies complementary atomic-number-sensitive contrast, with Pt generally brighter than Pd for comparable thickness. Core-shell segregation or distinct domains can then be tested. Projected overlap can imitate mixing, and low counts, thickness and beam damage limit nanoscale quantification; tomography or multiple views can resolve important ambiguities.
  • Fe impurities: EDS/EELS can locate Fe-rich inclusions or surface-associated material, while the bulk digest establishes whether a trace signal is representative. Failure to detect Fe in a few TEM fields does not rule out dilute contamination below local detection limits. XPS can additionally assess near-surface composition and oxidation state, but is not a bulk assay.
  • Size uniformity: obtain calibrated TEM images from randomly chosen independent fields, segment individual particles and report a number-weighted size histogram with sample count and shape descriptors. Avoid overlap and selection of only well-dispersed or attractive particles. A projected diameter is not a complete three-dimensional volume; scattering methods can corroborate ensemble size but weight particles differently.
The strength of TEM is correlating structure, size and local chemistry; its limited sampling is why a representative bulk chemical assay remains valuable.

One suitable platform is a monolayer molybdenum disulfide semiconductor. An electrostatic accumulation gate supplies conduction electrons whose motion is confined to the atomic sheet; source and drain contacts act as reservoirs. A clean insulating substrate and dielectric or encapsulation reduce disorder and prevent gate leakage. Two split gates deplete the adjacent sheet and leave a narrow conducting gap, thereby confining the in-plane transverse direction while retaining transport along the gap.
Apply a small source-drain voltage, measure the current and determine linear-response conductance while tuning the split-gate voltage and carrier density. A graphene sheet is another two-dimensional carrier system, but its lack of an ordinary gap complicates purely electrostatic confinement; etched boundaries or a suitable gapped architecture may be needed. A two-dimensional material does not by itself guarantee ballistic transport or resolved subbands.[ref]

G=Nh2e2​.​

In a vibrating-sample magnetometer, a magnetized specimen vibrates sinusoidally in a nominally uniform applied field. Its motion changes the magnetic flux through stationary pickup coils, producing an induced voltage by Faraday's law of induction. The component at the vibration frequency is proportional to the sample magnetic moment times vibration amplitude and frequency, for a calibrated coil geometry.
A lock-in detector referenced to the vibration extracts this small signal. Calibrate with a known moment, stabilize the vibration amplitude and sweep the applied field to measure a magnetization loop. Dividing moment by film volume gives M in Am−1; the silicon substrate and holder background must be subtracted for such a thin film.[ref]

A charged colloid in a polar electrolyte is surrounded by an electric double layer. For a negatively charged surface, counterions are positive and co-ions negative; their distributions reverse for a positive surface. A compact Stern layer contains specifically adsorbed or closely approaching ions, followed by a diffuse layer where electrostatic attraction competes with thermal motion. Mobile charge integrated over the surroundings balances the particle charge in an overall neutral system.
For a mean-field dilute electrolyte, ni​(r)=ni,∞​exp[−zi​eψ(r)/(kB​T)] with Poisson's equation relates charge density and potential. At weak potential, the diffuse potential outside a sphere decays approximately as ψ(r)∝e−κ(r−a)/r. The inverse screening length obeys κ2=e2∑i​zi2​ni,∞​/(ϵkB​T): added salt shortens the Debye length. The zeta potential is the potential at the hydrodynamic slip plane, not necessarily the surface potential. The simple model neglects ion size, correlations and chemical charge regulation; a polar solvent with almost no dissociated ions need not provide a short screening length.

An exciton is a Coulomb-correlated electron-hole pair, electrically neutral overall. Three useful properties are:
  • Exciton binding energy: the energy required to separate the correlated pair into unbound carriers. A larger value makes thermal dissociation less effective; dielectric screening and electronic delocalization influence it.
  • Spatial extent: the characteristic electron-hole separation or exciton radius. A localized Frenkel-like exciton is small compared with a Wannier-Mott exciton spread over many unit cells.
  • Lifetime: the characteristic time before radiative or nonradiative decay. Together with its diffusion coefficient it determines a transport scale, often expressed as LD​=DX​τX​​ in a specified diffusion-length convention.
In many molecular organic semiconductors, limited screening and localization make exciton binding important, so absorption need not immediately produce collectable free charges.

A suitable donor-acceptor heterojunction provides an energetically favourable route from a bound excitation to an interfacial charge-transfer state and then separated carriers. The electron is preferentially transported in the acceptor and the hole in the donor. The interface facilitates dissociation that is inefficient in a single strongly excitonic material; it must also avoid excessive energy loss and rapid interfacial recombination.
An idealized bulk heterojunction places most generated excitons within roughly a diffusion length of an interface while maintaining continuous donor paths to the hole contact and acceptor paths to the electron contact. Domain dimensions should therefore be comparable to the material's exciton transport scale, not arbitrarily large. The total absorbing thickness should capture substantial light yet remain within the charge-collection capability set by mobility, field and recombination. Excessive mixing can impair transport or increase recombination; very coarse domains waste excitons before they reach an interface.[ref]

In floating-catalyst CNT growth, a controlled sulfur additive acts as a growth promoter for the catalyst, commonly iron. Sulfur modifies catalyst surface composition, carbon uptake and the strength of the catalyst-carbon interaction; it can facilitate cap formation/lift-off and sustained tube growth instead of complete catalyst encapsulation. It also changes catalyst-particle evolution and hence tube yield, diameter and wall-count distributions.
There is an optimum range: excessive sulfur can occupy active sites or form an insufficiently active sulfide-rich catalyst and reduce growth. The additive is not simply a constituent that must be incorporated into every carbon wall. Its detailed action depends on catalyst, carbon source and process conditions; a single universal melting-point explanation is insufficient.[ref]

Each concentric shell has a different circumference, so it generally needs a different integer wrapping vector (n,m). Different vector lengths do not force different chiral angles, but exact atomic registry between all curved shells is geometrically restrictive. The shells can nucleate with different wrapping orientations, and strong covalent bonding within each shell dominates the weaker intershell interaction.
Consequently, it is usually unnecessary to strain every shell into a common orientation and registry; many multiwall tubes have incommensurate shells with different chiral angles. Identical chiral angles are possible, for example among suitable members of one armchair family, and should not be ruled out purely because the radii differ. Atomic indices, chiral angle and interwall commensurability are distinct descriptions.

The real-space carbon honeycomb has two atoms in a primitive cell of its triangular Bravais lattice. The reciprocal lattice is also triangular, and its Wigner-Seitz cell is a hexagonal first Brillouin zone. With a consistent orientation convention, the reciprocal-zone hexagon is rotated by 30∘ relative to a real-space carbon-ring hexagon; reciprocal lengths scale inversely with the direct lattice spacing.
Rolling into a tube imposes k⋅Ch​=2πℓ, leaving parallel allowed lines in graphene's two-dimensional reciprocal space. They run along the tube-axis direction, perpendicular to the circumferential wrapping vector. The nanotube's actual one-dimensional Brillouin zone is an interval of axial wave vectors with period 2π/∣T∣, where T is its axial translation. The parent hexagon is therefore a zone-folding construction, not a literal two-dimensional Brillouin zone for the finished one-dimensional tube.

The intended double-barrier resonant-tunnelling diode has a bias-dependent well level. Initially increasing bias can bring a quasi-bound level into alignment with available occupied emitter states, sharply increasing current. At larger bias the resonant level moves out of the effective injection window, for example below the emitter conduction-band edge, so resonant transmission and current decrease despite increasing voltage. In generic resistive-pulse sensing, two electrolyte reservoirs are connected through a small aperture. Electrodes apply a bias and a low-noise current amplifier records the pore current. Passage of a weakly charged insulating particle excludes conducting liquid, usually increasing resistance and giving a transient current decrease at fixed voltage. Pulse amplitude, duration and event rate contain size-, transport- and concentration-dependent information, subject to calibration.
Advantages include label-free single-particle counting, electrical readout and small active volume. Limitations include clogging, electrode/pore noise, finite bandwidth, uncertain particle trajectories and surface-conduction effects; a pulse is not intrinsically chemically specific. Strong particle charge or concentration polarization can complicate, or even reverse, the simple blockade signal. The schematic below uses only generic nonbiological particle transport.

Self-assembly is organization of interacting building blocks into a larger structure without individually placing every component. Static self-assembly approaches an equilibrium free-energy minimum under fixed conditions; a resulting structure may subsequently become kinetically trapped. Dynamic self-assembly maintains a nonequilibrium organized state through continuing energy or matter throughput and dissipation, and can change or disappear when that drive is removed.
For nanofabrication, parallel assembly of many small components can generate fine periodicity, hierarchical structures and adaptive or self-repairing arrangements with limited direct patterning. Building-block chemistry encodes interactions. However, access to a desired morphology depends on kinetics and competing states; assembly does not automatically eliminate defects or provide arbitrary device geometry.

Relevant interactions include van der Waals attraction, electrostatic attraction or screened repulsion, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic/solvophobic association. Coordination bonding, magnetic dipole interactions and capillary forces can also organize suitable building blocks. Entropic effects, including depletion attraction and packing entropy, can drive ordering even without a new chemical bond. Which contribution favours assembly depends on solvent, temperature, concentration and surface chemistry; the complete balance is a free-energy change, not a requirement that every interaction be attractive.

Brownian motion is the fluctuating motion of a suspended particle produced by thermal molecular collisions. Using the standard Brownian displacement conventions, in a uniform fluid without external drift, mean displacement is zero but mean-square displacement grows with time. For overdamped diffusion, ⟨Δx2⟩=2Dt in one coordinate and ⟨∣Δr∣2⟩=6Dt in three dimensions, with D=kB​T/ζ and Stokes friction ζ=6πηa for a no-slip sphere.
Brownian transport brings nanoparticles into encounters and allows them to explore configurations during self-assembly. Reversible interactions permit rearrangement toward low-free-energy structures; interactions that are too strong can instead trap disordered aggregates. Thermal fluctuations also oppose localization in shallow traps. The symbol ζ avoids confusing friction with an unrelated chemical potential.

For fluid density ρf​, speed U and characteristic length d, inertial kinetic energy scales as ρf​d3U2 while viscous work over distance d scales as ηUd2. Their ratio is the Reynolds number,
Re=ηρf​Ud​.​

Smaller particles at a fixed speed have smaller Re, so viscous stresses dominate fluid inertia when Re≪1. Increasing speed increases Re and eventually makes inertia more important, rather than making viscous dominance stronger.
Particle inertia is a separate issue. Its particle velocity-relaxation time is τp​=ρp​d2/(18η); comparing it with a travel time d/U gives a Stokes number τp​U/d. This uses particle density ρp​, whereas the fluid Reynolds number uses fluid density ρf​.

A tightly focused laser produces a strong intensity gradient. A small polarizable particle experiences a gradient force toward increasing intensity when the real polarizability has the appropriate positive sign; radiation pressure from scattering and absorption pushes predominantly along the beam. A high-numerical-aperture objective creates lateral confinement and a sufficiently strong opposing axial gradient for a three-dimensional optical trap.
