Boole's inequality (P(⋃i​Ai​)≤min{1,∑i​P(Ai​)})

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability inequality
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
Boole's inequality, also called the union bound, bounds the probability that at least one of several events occurs by adding their individual probabilities. For a finite or countable family of events Ai​ in the same probability space,
P(⋃i​Ai​)≤min{1,∑i​P(Ai​)}.
(53)
The union includes outcomes where several events occur together. There is no independence assumption: adding the individual probabilities counts such outcomes repeatedly, which can only make the sum larger. The cap at one uses the total mass of a probability measure. For infinitely many events, the sum is a nonnegative series, possibly infinite; an infinite sum gives only the bound one. This is countable subadditivity of a measure specialized to a probability measure. See Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett (Section 1.1, Theorem 1.1.1(ii), printed p. 2).
For example, choose uniformly from the twelve outcomes Ω={1,…,12}, using the discrete uniform distribution. Put A={1,2,3,4}. If B={5,6,7,8}, these are disjoint events and their union has probability 8/12=2/3, exactly the union bound. If B={3,4,5,6}, the union has only six outcomes, so its exact probability is 1/2, although both individual probabilities are still 1/3 and the bound is still 2/3. If B=A, the exact probability falls to 1/3 with the same bound. These examples isolate overlap in the union bound by keeping the individual probabilities fixed.
The union bound is useful when each event is easy to control but their joint behavior is difficult to determine. A short proof uses indicator functions: at every outcome,
1⋃i=1n​Ai​​≤∑i=1n​1Ai​​.
(54)
Taking expected values and using linearity of expectation proves the finite bound.[ref] For a countable family, the finite unions increase to the full union; continuity of probability passes the finite inequalities to the limit. The closure of a sigma-algebra under countable unions ensures that this limiting union is an event. For the continuity of probability step, see Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett (Section 1.1, Theorem 1.1.1(iii), printed p. 2).
