a OurBigBook.com
Write Zt​=z+Bt1​+iBt2​, where the coordinates are independent standard Brownian motions, and write f=u+iv. The Cauchy-Riemann equations imply Δu=Δv=0. Itô's formula therefore makes
Mt​=u(Zt​)−u(z),Nt​=v(Zt​)−v(z)
(1)
continuous local martingales. Their quadratic variations and quadratic covariation are
⟨M⟩t​=⟨N⟩t​=At​:=∫0t​∣f′(Zs​)∣2ds,⟨M,N⟩t​=0.
(2)
Indeed the two gradient norms are equal to ∣f′∣, and their dot product vanishes by the Cauchy-Riemann equations.
The clock is finite at every finite time, since a continuous Brownian path has compact range there and f′ is continuous. To show its lifetime is infinite, choose a small disc on which ∣f′∣2≥c>0; such a disc exists because the entire function is nonconstant. A supporting recurrence result is that planar Brownian motion spends infinite total time in every nonempty open disc almost surely. One proof combines recurrent returns to a smaller disc with the strong Markov property: each visit has a uniformly positive chance of lasting a fixed positive time in the larger disc. Equivalently, use the divergence of a positive planar Brownian occupation integral. Therefore
At​⟶∞almost surely.​
(3)
Finally the common-clock time change of orthogonal local martingales states that two continuous local martingales starting at zero with common bracket A, zero cross bracket, and A∞​=∞ are the coordinates of a planar Brownian motion evaluated at At​. Applying it gives a planar Brownian motion Z starting at zero with
f(Zt​)=f(z)+ZAt​​.​
(4)
This proves conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion in the entire-function setting, including the divergence of the conformal Brownian clock for an entire function.

Apply part (a) to the entire function f(z)=eiθz. Its derivative has modulus one, so its conformal Brownian clock is At​=t. Thus
eiθZt​=eiθZ0​+Zt​,
(5)
which is a planar Brownian motion starting from the rotated initial point. In particular, if the initial point is zero, its law is unchanged. This is rotational invariance of planar Brownian motion.

Start a planar Brownian motion B at zero and apply part (a) to the entire function f(z)=ez. Then
eBt​=1+ZAt​​,At​=∫0t​e2ReBs​ds.
(6)
The left side never vanishes. The clock is continuous, starts at zero, and tends to infinity almost surely, so its range is all nonnegative times. Consequently the complex Brownian motion 1+Zs​ never reaches zero at any time s. Every planar Brownian motion started at one has that same law, hence
P(T<∞)=0.​
(7)
This is the complex exponential construction of planar Brownian motion and proves that a fixed point is a polar point for planar Brownian motion. The divergence of the clock is essential: it makes this a conclusion about all Brownian times.

Let β(θ)=w−eiθ=∣β(θ)∣eig(θ). It is never zero, since ∣w∣<1. On the unit circle,
eiθ−w=−β(θ),1−weiθ=−eiθβ(θ)​.
(8)

f(eiθ)=e−iθβ(θ)​β(θ)​=exp(i(−θ+2g(θ))).​
(9)

g′(θ)=Im∣w−eiθ∣2−ieiθ(w−e−iθ)​=∣w−eiθ∣21−w1​cosθ−w2​sinθ​.

Also ∣w−eiθ∣2=1+∣w∣2−2(w1​cosθ+w2​sinθ). Subtracting one therefore gives
−1+2g′(θ)=∣w−eiθ∣21−∣w∣2​>0.​

c OurBigBook.com
The disk automorphism satisfies f(w)=0 and maps the unit circle bijectively to itself. Its derivative (1−∣w∣2)/(1−wz)2 is bounded above and away from zero on the closed disk. By conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion, the image path up to the exit time is a time-changed planar Brownian motion started at zero. Its exit point is f(ZT​); the clock changes when it exits, but not where.
The original exit time is finite almost surely. For example, ∣Zt∧T​∣2−2(t∧T) is a martingale, so E(t∧T)≤(1−∣w∣2)/2. Boundedness of f′ makes the image exit clock finite too. By rotational invariance of planar Brownian motion, the image exit angle is uniform on [0,2π).
Let Θ=argZT​. Parts (a) and (b) show that ϕ(Θ) modulo 2π is uniform, where ϕ is strictly increasing and increases by exactly 2π in one circuit. The one-dimensional change-of-variables formula therefore gives
h(θ)=2πϕ′(θ)​=2π1​∣w−eiθ∣21−∣w∣2​.​
(12)
Its integral is one because the total angle change is 2π. This harmonic measure density is the Poisson kernel for the unit disk divided by 2π. It is uniform for w=0 and concentrates near the boundary point closest to w as ∣w∣ increases.

a OurBigBook.com
A bounded holomorphic function of planar Brownian motion is a local martingale up to exit, because its real and imaginary parts are harmonic and Itô's formula has zero drift. Stop before the given S<TD​. Localization inside compact subdisks gives the local martingale f(Zt∧Sz​), and ∣f∣<1 makes it bounded. The bounded local martingale criterion therefore gives a true martingale.
Exit from the unit disk is finite almost surely, so S<TD​ is finite too. Taking t→∞ and using the dominated convergence theorem yields
E[f(ZSz​)]=f(z).​
(13)
The same proof works for any bounded holomorphic function, after rescaling if needed; it does not require the stopping time itself to be bounded.

Since f(0)=0, the function g(z)=f(z)/z extends analytically to zero by g(0)=f′(0). It is bounded on the disk: outside ∣z∣<1/2, ∣g(z)∣≤2, while on the closed half-radius disk its analytic extension is continuous and hence bounded.
Fix z=0 and choose ∣z∣<r<1. Let Tr​ be the first exit time of Brownian motion started at z from the radius-r disk. The bounded analytic martingale identity in part (a) gives
g(z)=Ez​[g(ZTr​​)].
(14)
On this exit circle, ∣g(ZTr​​)∣=∣f(ZTr​​)∣/r≤1/r. The triangle inequality gives ∣g(z)∣≤1/r. Letting r↑1 proves ∣g(z)∣≤1, and therefore
∣f(z)∣≤∣z∣for all z∈D.​
(15)
At zero the claim holds by the assumed zero value. This is the Schwarz lemma, obtained from Brownian optional stopping rather than an analytic maximum principle.

Let z0​=0 be an equality point and set c=g(z0​), so ∣c∣=1. For any ∣z0​∣<r<1, the same Brownian exit identity gives
1=Re(cg(z0​))=Ez0​​Re(cg(ZTr​​)).
(16)
Part (i) gives ∣g∣≤1, so the real random variable in the expectation is at most one. Equality of its mean to one forces g(ZTr​​)=c almost surely. By the scaled version of the exit density from Question 2, every nonempty arc of the exit circle has positive probability. Continuity of g on that circle therefore implies g=c at every boundary point of the radius-r disk.
For any w inside that circle, the Brownian exit identity now gives g(w)=Ew​g(ZTr​​)=c. Letting r increase to one makes this true throughout D. Writing c=eiθ yields
f(w)=weiθfor all w∈D.​
(17)
This completes the equality case of the Brownian proof of the Schwarz lemma.
