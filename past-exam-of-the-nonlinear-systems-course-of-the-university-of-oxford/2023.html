At a fixed point, the first equation gives y=0, the second then gives x=0, and the third gives z=−μ. Thus there is exactly one equilibrium for every real μ:
(x,y,z)=(0,0,−μ).​
(1)
The Jacobian matrix there is
J=​0−10​15ϵμ0​00−1​​,det(λI−J)=(λ+1)(λ2−5ϵμλ+1).
(2)
One eigenvalue is −1. The other two have product one and sum 5ϵμ. For μ<0 their real parts are negative, so the equilibrium is asymptotically stable; for μ>0 both real parts are positive, leaving two unstable directions and one stable direction. At μ=0 the eigenvalues are −1,i,−i, with
dμd​Reλ±​​μ=0​=25ϵ​>0.
(3)
Therefore the only local bifurcation of this equilibrium is a Hopf bifurcation at μ=0. A repeated real eigenvalue away from zero does not itself cause a bifurcation: the equilibrium remains hyperbolic there.
The cubic centre manifold calculation below gives the Hopf normal form radial coefficient −9ϵ/8<0, so this is a supercritical Hopf bifurcation. Small attracting periodic orbits occur on the μ>0 side. In particular, at μ=0 the leading cubic radial drift is inward, although linear stability alone cannot decide that boundary case.

Adjoin μ˙​=0 and put w=z+μ. The extended centre manifold is a graph w=H(x,y,μ) tangent to w=0 at the origin. The equations become
x˙=y,y˙​=−x+5ϵμy−5ϵyw,w˙=−w+x2,μ˙​=0.
(4)
Here ϵ>0 is fixed. The centre-manifold invariance equation, obtained by the chain rule, is
Hx​y+Hy​(−x+5ϵμy−5ϵyH)=−H+x2.
(5)
Write H=H2​+O(ρ3), where ρ=∣x∣+∣y∣+∣μ∣ and H2​ is a homogeneous quadratic polynomial. To quadratic order the centre-manifold invariance equation is
y∂x​H2​−x∂y​H2​=−H2​+x2.
(6)
The terms μx,μy,μ2 have zero coefficients: their equations are homogeneous, and the operator 1+y∂x​−x∂y​ is invertible on these quadratic polynomials. For H2​=ax2+bxy+cy2, comparison of coefficients gives
−b=1−a,2a−2c=−b,b=−c.
(7)
Hence a=3/5, b=−2/5 and c=2/5, giving
z=−μ+53​x2−52​xy+52​y2+O(ρ3).​
(8)
Substitution into the y equation gives
x˙=y,y˙​=−x+ϵ(5μy−3x2y+2xy2−2y3)+O(ρ4).​
(9)
The constant in the big O notation may depend on fixed ϵ. Thus the equations printed in part (b) are the retained terms of the local Taylor series, rather than an exact identity for the full reduced vector field.
For the Hopf bifurcation classification in part (a), set μ=0 and use x=rcosθ, y=−rsinθ. The radial derivative is r˙=(xx˙+yy˙​)/r. Its cubic angular mean is
rϵ​⟨−3x2y2+2xy3−2y4⟩=−ϵr3(83​+86​)=−89ϵ​r3.
(10)
Here ⟨⋅⟩ denotes the mean over one revolution. Removing the oscillatory cubic terms by a near-identity transformation gives this same cubic radial coefficient in the Hopf normal form. The linear radial coefficient is 5ϵμ/2.

Apply the Poincaré-Lindstedt method to the truncated system from part (b). Since y=x˙, its scalar equation is
x¨+x=ϵ(5μx˙−3x2x˙+2xx˙2−2x˙3).
(11)
Introduce the stretched time τ=ωt, with asymptotic expansions
ω=1+ϵω1​+O(ϵ2),x(t)=X(τ)=Acosτ+ϵX1​(τ)+O(ϵ2).
(12)
Primes in this part mean derivatives with respect to τ. At first order,
X1′′​+X1​=2ω1​Acosτ−5μAsinτ+3A3cos2τsinτ+2A3cosτsin2τ+2A3sin3τ.
(13)
The trigonometric identities
cos2τsinτ=4sinτ+sin3τ​,cosτsin2τ=4cosτ−cos3τ​,sin3τ=43sinτ−sin3τ​
(14)
reduce this to
X1′′​+X1​=(2ω1​A+2A3​)cosτ+(−5μA+49A3​)sinτ−2A3​cos3τ+4A3​sin3τ.
(15)
A periodic X1​ cannot have a secular term, so both fundamental-harmonic coefficients must vanish. Taking A>0 gives
A2=920μ​,ω1​=−4A2​=−95μ​.​
(16)
In particular μ>0 is necessary. Once these conditions hold, a bounded particular correction is
X1​=16A3​cos3τ−32A3​sin3τ;
(17)
adding fundamental sine and cosine terms adjusts the phase and the first correction to the amplitude. Consequently the required leading periodic orbit is
x=Acos(ωt)+O(ϵ),y=−Aωsin(ωt)+O(ϵ),ω=1−95μϵ​+O(ϵ2).​
(18)
Here A is the leading amplitude, not an assertion that the exact finite-ϵ amplitude is independent of ϵ.
To justify existence beyond formal cancellation, the leading radial displacement in a Poincaré return map is 2πϵB(r), where
B(r)=25μ​r−89​r3.
(19)
At the positive root A, B′(A)=−5μ=0. The implicit function theorem, applied after dividing the displacement by ϵ, continues this root to a periodic orbit for sufficiently small positive ϵ. Its radial Floquet multiplier is 1−10πμϵ+O(ϵ2), so it is attracting. These statements apply to the truncated system with fixed μ>0; its interpretation as a local approximation to the original three-dimensional system also requires μ small, so that A=O(μ​) stays near the Hopf bifurcation.

The Jacobian matrix at the origin and its characteristic polynomial are
M0​=(0−μ​μ1+μ​),p(λ)=λ2−(1+μ)λ+μ2.
(22)
The fixed-point multipliers are
λ±​=21+μ±(1−μ)(1+3μ)​​.
(23)
By the Jury stability criterion, both lie strictly inside the unit circle exactly when
∣μ2∣<1,p(1)=μ(μ−1)>0,p(−1)=μ2+μ+2>0.
(24)
The last condition always holds, since μ2+μ+2=(μ+1/2)2+7/4. Therefore the origin is asymptotically stable exactly for −1<μ<0 among the hyperbolic parameter values.
For completeness, its linear stability throughout the parameter line is as follows. When μ<−1, the complex-conjugate Floquet multipliers have modulus ∣μ∣>1, so the origin repels. When −1<μ<−1/3, their modulus is ∣μ∣<1, so it attracts with oscillation. At μ=−1/3 the repeated Floquet multiplier is 1/3, still attracting. For −1/3<μ<0, both Floquet multipliers are real and in (0,1). For 0<μ<1, p(1)<0 and p(0)>0, so one Floquet multiplier lies in (0,1) and the other exceeds one: the origin is a saddle fixed point of a map. For μ>1, the complex-conjugate Floquet multipliers have modulus μ>1, so it repels.
Possible changes at the unit circle are exhausted by p(1)=0, p(−1)=0, or a nonreal pair with product one. They give
μ=−1,0,1.​
(25)
At μ=0 the Floquet multipliers are 0,1; the nonzero fixed point branches in part (a) meet the origin, and part (c) identifies a subcritical pitchfork bifurcation. Indeed at μ=0 the exact restricted map is y↦y+y3, which makes the origin unstable despite its neutral linear direction.
At μ=−1 the Floquet multipliers are i,−i: this is a 1:4 strong resonance of a planar map, coinciding with the change from repelling to attracting as μ increases. A generic nonresonant Neimark–Sacker bifurcation conclusion would be unjustified. At μ=1 there is a double Floquet multiplier +1; the matrix has a nontrivial Jordan block, and the two further nonzero fixed point branches meet the origin. Linear stability alone is inconclusive at these unit-modulus boundary values. The repeated Floquet multiplier at μ=−1/3 is strictly inside the unit circle and gives no bifurcation.

Adjoin μn+1​=μn​. At the origin the stable direction is the x axis, with Floquet multiplier zero, while the y and parameter directions have Floquet multiplier one. By the centre manifold theorem for a discrete dynamical system, write the extended centre manifold as
x=h(y,μ),h(0,0)=0,Dh(0,0)=0.
(26)
On it the scalar map is Y=(1+μ)y−μh(y,μ)+y3. Its discrete centre-manifold invariance equation is
h(Y,μ)=μy.
(27)
Put h=h2​+O(ρ3) with ρ=∣y∣+∣μ∣. Since Y=y+O(ρ2), the quadratic equation is simply h2​(y,μ)=μy. Thus
x=μy+O(ρ3).​
(28)
In particular, setting the stable variable identically to zero would lose the leading parameter correction. The reduced map through total degree three is
yn+1​=yn​+μyn​−μ2yn​+yn3​+O(ρ4).​
(29)
In the natural pitchfork bifurcation scaling μ=O(y2) this has leading form yn+1​=yn​+μyn​+yn3​. Its nonzero fixed points satisfy y2∼−μ, and their reduced fixed-point multipliers satisfy 1−2μ+O(μ2)>1 for small μ<0. The transverse Floquet multiplier remains inside the unit circle. Therefore the bifurcation at μ=0 is a subcritical pitchfork bifurcation: the origin attracts for small negative μ, each nonzero branch is a saddle fixed point of a map, and the origin is a saddle fixed point of a map for small positive μ.
An exact check uses the full Jacobian matrix on either nonzero branch. Its trace is T∗​=1−2μ+3μ2 and its determinant is D∗​=μ2. Thus
p∗​(1)=1−T∗​+D∗​=2μ(1−μ)<0
(30)
whenever a nonzero branch exists. Also p∗​(0)=μ2>0, so one real Floquet multiplier lies in (0,1) and the other in (1,∞). This proves that each nonzero branch is a saddle fixed point of a map throughout μ<0 and μ>1, in particular near the requested bifurcation.

Use y vertically and μ horizontally in the bifurcation diagram; the other coordinate is recovered from x=μy. The origin is the horizontal branch y=0. Draw it solid on −1<μ<0, where it is asymptotically stable, and dashed on the remaining hyperbolic intervals, where it is unstable. Its three nonhyperbolic fixed points in parameter space are marked at μ=−1,0,1; open markers distinguish these boundary cases from the strict linear stability classifications on the intervening intervals.
The nonzero fixed point branches are y=±μ(μ−1)​ for μ<0 and μ>1. Each is dashed because it is a saddle fixed point of a map, as proved in part (c). Near zero their shape is y∼±−μ​: the unstable branches lie on the same parameter side as the attracting origin, displaying the subcritical pitchfork bifurcation. The branches issuing from μ=1 have y∼±μ−1​. At μ=−1 no additional fixed point branch appears; the Floquet multipliers cross the unit circle at ±i.

i OurBigBook.com
For the left shift, σp(s)=s precisely when the binary sequence is obtained by repeating its first p symbols. There are exactly 2p such sequences, although some have smaller least period. Hence the set of all periodic sequences is a countable union of finite sets and is at most countable. Passing from periodic points to periodic orbits cannot increase this cardinality.
To prove infinitude, for every integer p≥1 take the repeated word (0p−11)∞. Its least period is p, because its ones are separated by exactly p positions; a smaller positive period would carry a one to a zero. Distinct least periods give distinct periodic orbits. Thus there are countably infinitely many periodic orbits.

The space Σ of all infinite binary sequences is uncountable by the Cantor diagonal argument: given any list s(1),s(2),…, the sequence ti​=1−si(i)​ differs from its ith entry at position i, so it is absent from the list. Part (i) shows that only countably many sequences are periodic. Their complement is therefore uncountable.
Each forward orbit {σn(s):n≥0} is countable. If there were only countably many nonperiodic forward orbits, their union together with the periodic points would be countable, contradicting the preceding argument. Hence there are uncountably many nonperiodic orbits, rather than merely uncountably many nonperiodic starting points.
One can also exclude eventual periodicity. An eventually periodic sequence is specified by a finite initial word and a finite repeating word, so only countably many exist. Removing all of them still leaves an uncountable set. This proves the assertion even if “nonperiodic” is understood to exclude orbits that eventually enter a periodic orbit.

Construct one infinite binary sequence u by concatenating every binary word of length one, then every word of length two, then every word of length three, and so on, in lexicographic order within each length. Every finite binary word occurs somewhere in u.
Given any target sequence s and any η>0, choose N such that 2−N<η. The word s1​⋯sN​ occurs in u, beginning after some k symbols. Thus σku agrees with s in its first N positions, and the prescribed metric gives
d(σku,s)≤∑i>N​2−i=2−N<η.
(31)
Every open open ball about every point therefore meets the forward orbit of u. This forward orbit is dense in Σ. The argument identifies finite-prefix cylinder sets as arbitrarily small neighborhoods in this metric; no binary-expansion convention for real numbers is needed.

For the real logistic map, f(x)=x factors as
x(μ−1−μx)=0.
(32)
There is always the fixed point x0​=0. If μ=0 there is also x∗​=1−1/μ, distinct from zero unless μ=1. At μ=0 the map is constant and zero is its only fixed point. Negative μ must be retained because the paper specifies x∈R.
Since f′(x)=μ(1−2x), the fixed-point multipliers are
λ0​=μ,λ∗​=2−μ.
(33)
The fixed point stability for an iteration criterion gives x0​ attracting for −1<μ<1, and x∗​ attracting for 1<μ<3. The respective fixed points are repelling when ∣μ∣>1 and ∣2−μ∣>1. At the unit-modulus boundaries the linear criterion needs a nonlinear supplement.
At μ=1, the two fixed point branches meet and exchange stability: this is a transcritical bifurcation. Explicitly, with δ=μ−1,
f(x)−x=δx−(1+δ)x2,
(34)
which has the required nonzero parameter and quadratic terms. At the critical value itself, f(x)=x−x2 attracts small positive points and pushes negative points away, so the merged fixed point is not stable from both sides.
At μ=−1 the origin’s fixed-point multiplier crosses −1, while at μ=3 the nonzero fixed-point multiplier crosses −1. Both are period-doubling bifurcations. To check existence and stability of the new periodic orbit, factor the second iteration of a map:
f2(x)−x=−x(μx−μ+1)(μ2x2−μ(μ+1)x+μ+1).
(35)
The two non-fixed roots are
q±​=2μμ+1±(μ−3)(μ+1)​​.
(36)
They form a real period-two orbit for μ<−1 or μ>3. Its periodic-orbit multiplier is
f′(q−​)f′(q+​)=4+2μ−μ2.
(37)
It is less than one in absolute value just below −1 and just above 3, respectively. Thus these are supercritical bifurcations in the direction where the parent fixed point loses stability. At the critical values the fixed points themselves are weakly attracting: at μ=−1, f2(x)=x−2x3+x4; at μ=3, setting u=x−2/3 gives u↦u−18u3−27u4 after two steps. Both shrink sufficiently small nonzero absolute values. The bifurcation values for finite fixed points are therefore
μ=−1 (period doubling),1 (transcritical),3 (period doubling).​
(38)
The disappearance of the formula for x∗​ at μ=0 corresponds to that branch going to infinity, not a local bifurcation at a finite fixed point.

The set In consists precisely of points whose first n positions x,f(x),…,fn−1(x) all lie in L∪R. Hence
Λ={x∈[0,1]:fk(x)∈L∪R for every k≥0}.
(45)
Deleting the first position preserves this condition, proving f(Λ)⊆Λ. For the reverse inclusion, if y∈Λ, take x=gL​(y). Then x∈L and fk(x)=fk−1(y)∈L∪R for every k≥1. Thus x∈Λ and f(x)=y. In fact both inverse branches give preimages in Λ. Therefore
f(Λ)=Λ,​
(46)
which proves full invariance, not merely forward invariance.
For any infinite symbolic sequence s, the itinerary cylinders Is1​⋯sn​​ are nonempty compact nested intervals. The nested interval theorem gives at least one point in their intersection. Every x∈Λ has a unique itinerary because L∩R=∅. To justify that the itinerary also determines a unique point, it suffices to show the interval lengths shrink. The fact that logistic inverse branches contract in an angular coordinate works for every μ>4; a direct claim that ∣gL′​∣,∣gR′​∣<1 in the original coordinate would fail for some μ just above four.
Use the homeomorphism h(u)=sin2(πu/2) of [0,1]. The transformed inverse branches are
GL​(u)=h−1(gL​(h(u)))=π1​arcsin(μ​2​sin2πu​),GR​(u)=1−GL​(u).
(47)
Their derivatives satisfy
∣GL′​(u)∣=∣GR′​(u)∣=μ​1−(4/μ)sin2(πu/2)​cos(πu/2)​≤μ​1​<1.
(48)
The inequality follows by squaring and using 4/μ<1. Thus every length-n transformed itinerary cylinder has length at most μ−n/2. Since ∣h′∣≤π/2, its original interval has length at most (π/2)μ−n/2 and shrinks to a point. This gives the claimed one-to-one identification with the one-sided full shift.
Finally part (ii) gives
f(x)∈⋂n≥2​Is2​⋯sn​​,s1​s2​s3​⋯ ⟼ s2​s3​⋯.​
(49)
Thus f acts by the left shift. The identification is a topological conjugacy: matching long prefixes forces closeness by the shrinking estimate, while distinct finite itinerary cylinders are disjoint compact sets and so their inverse coding is continuous. In particular f need not be one-to-one on Λ, just as a one-sided left shift has two possible first symbols before any given tail.
