i OurBigBook.com
Write the lower central series of a Lie algebra as g1=g and gj+1=[g,gj]. Since the nilpotent Lie algebra is nonzero, there is a last nonzero term gc, with gc+1=0. Thus [g,gc]=0, so
0=gc⊆Z(g).
(1)
Consequently the centre is nonzero. This argument works over every field and includes an abelian algebra, for which c=1.

Fix x with xN=0 as a matrix. On the full matrix algebra define Lx​(y)=xy and Rx​(y)=yx. These endomorphisms commute and each has Nth power zero. The Adjoint representation of a Lie algebra is ad(x)=Lx​−Rx​. The binomial theorem gives
(adx)m(y)=∑j=0m​(−1)j(jm​)xm−jyxj.
(2)
For m=2N−1, each summand has either j≥N or m−j≥N, and hence vanishes. Therefore ad(x) is nilpotent on the full matrix algebra and on its invariant subspace g.
Engel's theorem, in its adjoint form, states that a finite-dimensional Lie algebra is nilpotent if every ad(x) is a nilpotent endomorphism. Applying it to all x∈g proves
g is nilpotent.​
(3)
This is distinct from assuming that an arbitrary representation of a nilpotent algebra must act by nilpotent matrices, which is false.

If g is nilpotent, every Lie subalgebra a is nilpotent, since aj⊆gj in the respective lower central series of a Lie algebra. A two-dimensional nilpotent algebra has nonzero centre by part (i). If 0=z is central, complete it to a basis (z,u); every bracket of these basis elements is zero. Thus a is abelian.
Conversely, suppose g is not nilpotent. By Engel's theorem, some ad(x) is not nilpotent. Since the field is algebraically closed, it has an eigenvalue λ=0 with eigenvector y=0. Thus
[x,y]=λy.
(4)
The vectors x,y are independent: otherwise their bracket would be zero. Their span is therefore a two-dimensional Lie subalgebra and it is nonabelian. This contradicts the hypothesis. Hence
g is nilpotent⟺every two-dimensional subalgebra is abelian.​
(5)

Suppose ad(x) has a nonzero eigenvalue λ and corresponding vector y. Then [x,y]=λy, so
ad(y)x=−λy=0,(ady)2x=0.
(6)
For a diagonalizable endomorphism T, its eigenspace decomposition shows kerT2=kerT. The displayed equations therefore contradict diagonalizability of ad(y).
Thus no ad(x) has a nonzero eigenvalue. Each is diagonalizable over the algebraically closed field, so each is the zero endomorphism. Hence [x,z]=0 for every x,z∈g, proving
g is abelian.​

By the classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations over C, every finite-dimensional module is a direct sum of simple modules Lm​, indexed by integers m≥0. The module Lm​ has dimension m+1 and a basis v0​,…,vm​ with
hvj​evj​fvj​​=(m−2j)vj​,=jvj−1​,=(m−j)vj+1​,​
(8)
with out-of-range vectors interpreted as zero. In particular v0​ is a highest weight vector of weight m. The weights are m,m−2,…,−m, each with multiplicity one. The multiplicities of the Lm​ in a finite module determine that module up to isomorphism.

In the simple module Lm​, weight 0 occurs once if m is even and never if m is odd. Weight 1 occurs once if m is odd and never if m is even. Thus each simple summand contributes exactly one to dimV0​+dimV1​.
Complete reducibility of semisimple Lie algebra representations writes V=⨁m​Lm⊕am​​. Taking the two weight spaces commutes with this finite direct sum, so
dimV0​+dimV1​=m∑​am​.​
(9)
The right side counts irreducible summands with multiplicity, independently of a chosen decomposition.

iii OurBigBook.com
Every 2×2 matrix has the unique decomposition
A=2trA​I+(A−2trA​I).
(10)
Both summands define invariant subspaces for the Adjoint representation of a Lie algebra. Scalar matrices commute with everything, so CI is L0​. The traceless matrices form sl2​ with basis e,h,f of weights 2,0,−2. Their brackets satisfy [e,f]=h, [h,e]=2e, [h,f]=−2f; the raising and lowering operations connect all three weight lines. This is the simple module L2​. Therefore
gl2​(C)≅L0​⊕L2​.​
(11)
As a check, its zero-weight space has dimension two and its one-weight space is zero, agreeing with part (ii).

On a monomial XaYb, direct differentiation gives
E(XaYb)=bXa+1Yb−1,F(XaYb)=aXa−1Yb+1,H(XaYb)=(a−b)XaYb.
(12)
For example (EF−FE)(XaYb)=[a(b+1)−b(a+1)]XaYb=(a−b)XaYb. The same calculation with H gives
[H,E]=2E,[H,F]=−2F,[E,F]=H.
(13)
These are exactly the brackets of h,e,f, so the linear map ρ respects every bracket and is a Lie algebra representation.
For m≥0, let Wm​ be the space of homogeneous polynomials of total degree m. Each operator preserves total degree. Every polynomial is a finite sum of homogeneous polynomials, so
W=⨁m=0∞​Wm​.
(14)
In Wm​, the monomials vj​=Xm−jYj have distinct weights m−2j and exactly the actions described in part (i). To see simplicity directly, a nonzero invariant subspace is invariant under H and hence contains one of its weight components: polynomial interpolation in H projects onto each of its distinct eigenspaces. Starting with a nonzero multiple of vj​, repeated application of E reaches v0​, and repeated application of F then reaches all vj​. All coefficients used are nonzero in characteristic zero. Therefore Wm​ is simple and isomorphic to Lm​.
Thus
C[X,Y]=m≥0⨁​span{Xm,Xm−1Y,…,Ym}≅m≥0⨁​Lm​.​
(15)
This is an algebraic direct sum, not an infinite product or completion. Its simple submodules are precisely the Wm​: they are pairwise nonisomorphic and each occurs once, so projection onto homogeneous degrees and Schur's lemma exclude diagonal copies between distinct degrees. Complete reducibility here follows from the explicit decomposition, rather than applying a finite-dimensional theorem directly to the infinite-dimensional W.

Equip the finite-dimensional real vector space V with a positive-definite inner product. A reduced crystallographic root system is a finite set R⊂V∖{0} spanning V such that, for every α∈R,
R∩Rα={α,−α},sα​(R)=R,(α,α)2(β,α)​∈Z(β∈R),
(16)
where sα​(v)=v−2(v,α)α/(α,α) is the orthogonal reflection in α⊥. The Weyl group is W(R)=⟨sα​:α∈R⟩. It permutes the finite set R. Its action is faithful because R spans V: an element fixing every root fixes every vector. Hence it embeds in the finite symmetric group of R and is finite.
A Cartan subalgebra is a nilpotent self-normalizing Lie subalgebra. For a finite-dimensional complex semisimple Lie algebra it is equivalently a maximal toral subalgebra: a maximal abelian subalgebra whose elements have diagonalizable adjoint actions. The commuting semisimple operators ad(h) give the root-space decomposition
g=h⊕⨁α∈R​gα​,gα​={x:[h,x]=α(h)x for every h∈h}.
(17)
The nonzero weights α are the roots. Their real span inside the complex dual h∗, with the positive-definite real inner product induced by the Killing form, is the Euclidean space in which they form a reduced crystallographic root system. In particular one uses this real span, rather than treating all of h∗ as a real vector space of twice the rank.
For the specified Special orthogonal Lie algebra, put iˉ=7−i. The matrix relation is xij​=−xjˉ​,iˉ​. Thus its diagonal Cartan subalgebra consists of
H(t)=diag(t1​,t2​,t3​,−t3​,−t2​,−t1​).
(18)
Let εi​(H(t))=ti​. The matrix units satisfy [H,Eij​]=(di​−dj​)Eij​, where d=(t1​,t2​,t3​,−t3​,−t2​,−t1​). A basis of root vectors is supplied, for 1≤i<j≤3, by
rootεi​−εj​−εi​+εj​εi​+εj​−εi​−εj​​root vectorEij​−Ejˉ​,iˉ​Eji​−Eiˉ,jˉ​​Ei,jˉ​​−Ej,iˉ​Ejˉ​,i​−Eiˉ,j​​​
(19)
These are twelve independent off-diagonal matrices. Together with the three diagonal generators, they account for the full dimension 6⋅5/2=15, so no further roots occur. Consequently
R={±εi​±εj​:1≤i<j≤3},R has type D3​.​
(20)
Choose the simple roots
α1​=ε1​−ε2​,α2​=ε2​−ε3​,α3​=ε2​+ε3​.
(21)
The six positive roots are α1​,α2​,α3​,α1​+α2​,α1​+α3​,α1​+α2​+α3​, and every other root is their negative. In the standard orthonormal coordinates all roots have squared length 2, and their Cartan matrix is
​2−1−1​−120​−102​​.
(22)
Its Dynkin diagram is the connected chain α2​−α1​−α3​. A decomposition into mutually orthogonal nonempty root systems would separate this connected set of simple roots, which is impossible. Thus the root system is irreducible. The chain is also the diagram A3​, which is the root system of sl4​(C). The classification of complex semisimple Lie algebras by their root systems yields the Lie algebra isomorphism
so6​(C)≅sl4​(C).​
(23)
An explicit explanation of this coincidence uses the exterior-power Lie algebra representation of sl4​ on ⋀2C4, a six-dimensional space. After choosing a volume form, u∧v defines a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on that exterior square. The induced action preserves it because a traceless endomorphism acts trivially on the top exterior power. This gives a nonzero homomorphism sl4​→so6​. Its kernel is an ideal in the simple algebra sl4​, so it is zero; equal dimensions 15 then make it an isomorphism. Over C all nondegenerate symmetric forms in dimension six are equivalent, including the form with matrix J.
