Integer (Z)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 76
An integer is a whole number, positive, negative, or zero. The set of all integers is
Z={…,−3,−2,−1,0,1,2,3,…}.
(1)
The positive integers are 1,2,3,…; the negative integers are their opposites −1,−2,−3,…. Zero is neither positive nor negative. The nonnegative integers include zero and all the positive integers. On a number line, consecutive integers are one unit apart, negative integers lie to the left of zero, and positive integers lie to its right. [ref]
The integers let counting describe changes in both directions. If a counter starts at −3 and increases by five, it reaches 2: −3+5=2. Decreasing that result by four gives 2+(−4)=−2. In addition on the number line, the sign of the added integer chooses the direction and its absolute value gives the distance. The additive inverse −a reverses the change made by a.
The integers have closure under a binary operation for addition, subtraction and multiplication: whenever a,b∈Z, the results a+b, a−b and ab are still integers. Together these operations give the integers their ring structure.[ref] For instance, −3+5=2, −3−5=−8, and (−3)⋅5=−15. General division has no such closure: 1/2 is a rational number but not an integer. Some divisions are exact, such as (−6)/3=−2; division by zero is undefined. Euclidean division instead produces an integer quotient and a remainder, so 7=3⋅2+1 retains the information that ordinary division puts into the fraction 7/3.
An integer is the value, whereas its written numeral is an encoding. The decimal representation 20510​ and the binary representation 110011012​ name the same integer. Changing positional notation changes how that value is displayed and how calculations are organized; it does not change the value itself.
