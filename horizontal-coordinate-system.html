Horizontal coordinate system

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 7
A horizontal coordinate system describes a viewing direction using altitude γ above the observer's astronomical horizon and azimuth η around it. Here azimuth starts at true north and increases through east: north is 0∘, east 90∘, south 180∘, and west 270∘, with 0≤η<360∘. True north is the northward intersection of the celestial meridian with the astronomical horizon, rather than an uncorrected magnetic-compass reading. Other origins and directions are legitimate, but their formulae must be converted consistently. [ref].
Construct a local orthonormal basis of east eE​, north eN​, and upward eU​, with eE​×eN​=eU​. The upward unit vector points to the zenith; the horizontal plane is perpendicular to it. For a ground instrument, a freely hanging weight establishes the local vertical through gravity. In an elementary spherical-Earth model, this vertical is radial; precision work distinguishes it from the normal to a reference surface. With the observer as origin, write a direction's unit vector as
n=cosγsinηeE​+cosγcosηeN​+sinγeU​.
(10)
Its local components (E,N,U) obey E2+N2+U2=1. Conversely, γ=arcsinU and η=atan2(E,N) modulo one turn, except where E=N=0. The quadrant-aware arctangent takes east as its first argument and north as its second. A synthetic direction with η=60∘ and γ=30∘ has (E,N,U)=(3/4,3​/4,1/2). Thus its horizontal component has norm 3​/2; its altitude is not the angle of the projected arrow in the horizontal plane.
A horizontal coordinate system depends on the observer's site and the observation time. The Earth's rotation changes the local axes relative to a fixed distant direction, while changing the site changes the zenith and the astronomical horizon. This makes the horizontal coordinate system directly useful for pointing an optical telescope and checking whether terrain blocks a target. A pair of angles without a site, time, azimuth convention, and geometric or refracted interpretation is an incomplete pointing instruction.
For a check against an equatorial coordinate system, let θ be the observer's geographical latitude, δ the direction's declination, and h its west-positive hour angle, obtained from local sidereal time minus right ascension. In the spherical-Earth, unrefracted fixed-direction model, projection onto the local orthonormal basis gives
E=−cosδsinh,N=cosθsinδ−sinθcosδcosh,U=sinθsinδ+cosθcosδcosh.
(11)
Taking the dot product with the upward unit vector gives the last formula; the other two follow by the same projection. Recover altitude and azimuth from (E,N,U) as above, using radians inside software trigonometric functions. The minus sign in E is essential: a negative hour angle puts this synthetic direction east of the celestial meridian. [ref].
For illustrative geographical latitude θ=45∘ and invented declination δ=15∘, hour angle h=−60∘ gives γ≃31.636∘ and η≃100.729∘. At h=+60∘, the same altitude accompanies η≃259.271∘: the direction is now westward. At h=0, it is due south with altitude 60∘, agreeing with 90∘−∣θ−δ∣ at upper meridian transit. These are analytical checks, not present positions of any star. For nearby objects, the direction must also be referred to the observer; simply rotating a geocentric direction omits the change of viewing position. Catalogue-to-observation refinements are described under equatorial coordinate system.
