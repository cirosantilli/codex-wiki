a OurBigBook.com
Choose the planar streamfunction convention u=ψy​, v=−ψx​. For an irrotational flow, a velocity potential gives u=∇ϕ. Thus
u=ϕx​=ψy​,v=ϕy​=−ψx​.​
(1)
The streamfunction enforces incompressibility, since ux​+vy​=ψyx​−ψxy​=0. Conversely, incompressibility permits a local streamfunction, while zero curl permits a local velocity potential. These are the Cauchy-Riemann equations for the complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow w=ϕ+iψ.
The gradients of the two functions satisfy
∇ϕ⋅∇ψ=(u,v)⋅(−v,u)=0.
(2)
Each gradient is normal to its regular level curves. Therefore regular curves of constant velocity potential and constant streamfunction meet orthogonally. At a stagnation point, both gradients vanish and this tangent-direction argument needs a limiting interpretation. The conclusion concerns regular portions of the two families of curves.

Use instantaneous polar coordinates centered on the moving circular cylinder, but measure the fluid velocity in the laboratory frame. Impermeability equates the normal components of body and fluid velocity:
(u−Uex​)⋅er​=0(r=a).
(3)
Since ex​⋅er​=cosθ and ur​=ϕr​, the no-penetration boundary condition is
ϕr​(a,θ)=Ucosθ.​
(4)
The velocity potential obeys the exterior Laplace equation, by incompressibility. The boundary data have only the first cosine harmonic. A decaying harmonic potential of this form is ϕ=Ccosθ/r. Matching its radial derivative gives −C/a2=U, so C=−Ua2. The zero circulation condition rules out an angular potential term, and the integrated boundary condition gives zero net volume flux, ruling out a radial logarithmic source term. The remaining decaying harmonics have zero coefficients by the boundary data. Hence
ϕ=−rUa2​cosθ.​
(5)
Using the streamfunction in polar coordinates, ur​=r−1ψθ​, uθ​=−ψr​, gives
ψ=rUa2​sinθ,ur​=r2Ua2​cosθ,uθ​=r2Ua2​sinθ.​
(6)
One may also obtain both functions as the real and imaginary parts of w(z)=−Ua2/z, a two-dimensional potential doublet. The velocity decays as r−2 and the source of this singular representation lies inside the excluded body. These are the fields used to calculate the added mass of a circular cylinder.

The product rule, the Laplace equation and u=∇ϕ give
∇⋅(ϕ∇ϕ)=∣∇ϕ∣2+ϕ∇2ϕ=∣u∣2.​
(7)
First truncate the exterior fluid domain by a large circle CR​. Let nB​ point outward from the body into the fluid. The outward normal of the exterior fluid domain at the body is −nB​. The planar divergence theorem, equivalently Green's theorem, therefore gives
∫DR​​∣u∣2dA=∮CR​​ϕϕr​ds−∮∂B​ϕ∂nB​​ϕds.
(8)
Orient ∂B counterclockwise around the body. Then nB​ds=(dy,−dx) and
∂nB​​ϕds=ϕx​dy−ϕy​dx=ψy​dy+ψx​dx=dψ.
(9)
For the finite-energy exterior rigid-body flow intended here, the outer boundary term vanishes as R→∞. In particular, zero net volume flux and zero circulation eliminate the 1/z term in the exterior Laurent series of the complex velocity. Thus u=O(R−2) and, after choosing the additive potential constant, ϕ=O(R−1). The outer integral is then O(R−2).
Multiplying by half the constant mass density gives the boundary integral for kinetic energy of planar potential flow:
T=2ρ​∫R2∖B​∣u∣2dA=−2ρ​∮∂B​ϕdψ.​
(10)
The minus sign comes from the fluid's inner-boundary orientation, rather than a negative physical kinetic energy.
There is a necessary qualification to the printed hypotheses. Decaying velocity and zero circulation alone do not ensure a finite kinetic energy: ϕ=Alogr gives u=Aer​/r, zero circulation, and logarithmically divergent exterior kinetic energy. It carries a nonzero volume flux from the body and is not the flow of an impermeable rigid body. The finite-energy condition for planar exterior potential flow makes explicit the source-free assumption needed to discard the outer boundary term.

On the translating circular cylinder r=a, the preceding velocity potential and streamfunction give
ϕ=−Uacosθ,dψ=Uacosθdθ.
(11)
Using the boundary integral for kinetic energy of planar potential flow,
T=−2ρ​∫02π​(−U2a2cos2θ)dθ=2ρπa2U2​.
(12)
Therefore
T=21​MU2,M=ρπa2.​
(13)
As a direct check, ∣u∣2=U2a4/r4, and integration over r>a gives the same result.
The fluid kinetic energy has the same dependence on body speed as the kinetic energy of an extra mass M carried by the body. Thus M is its added mass, here equal to the mass of displaced fluid per unit cylinder length. For a cylinder of physical mass per length m, the combined kinetic energy is 21​(m+M)U2. Accelerating the body requires accelerating the surrounding fluid, producing the inertial reaction −MU˙ along the direction of motion. This is the added mass of a circular cylinder.

a OurBigBook.com
The Milne-Thomson circle theorem constructs a circular impermeable boundary in planar potential flow. Suppose f(ζ) is an incident complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow with no singularities on or inside ∣ζ∣=R. Then
W(ζ)=f(ζ)+f(ζˉ​R2​)​​
(14)
is a complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow outside the circle, with the same exterior singularities and their strengths as the incident flow; the added image singularities lie inside the excluded disk. On ∣ζ∣=R, R2/ζˉ​=ζ, so W=f+fˉ​ is real. Its imaginary part, the streamfunction, is constant on the circle, proving the no-penetration boundary condition. The theorem gives the usual zero-circulation construction; a separately prescribed circulation can be added by its logarithmic vortex potential.

A uniform flow with physical velocity (Ucosα,Usinα) has complex velocity Ue−iα and incident potential f(ζ)=Ue−iαζ. Apply the Milne-Thomson circle theorem with R=1:
W(ζ)=U(e−iαζ+ζeiα​).​
(15)
Indeed, on ζ=eiθ it is W=2Ucos(θ−α), which is real, so the boundary is a streamline. At infinity, W′(ζ)→Ue−iα, verifying the specified incoming uniform flow.

Parameterize the unit circle by ζ=eiθ. Under the Joukowski map,
z=eiθ+a2e−iθ=(1+a2)cosθ+i(1−a2)sinθ.
(16)
Consequently its image is the ellipse
(1+a2)2x2​+(1−a2)2y2​=1,​
(17)
with horizontal and vertical semi-axes
A=1+a2,B=1−a2.​
(18)
Both are positive because 0<a<1. The horizontal axis is the major axis. The derivative dz/dζ=1−a2/ζ2 is nonzero for ∣ζ∣≥1. Also, equality of two images implies (ζ1​−ζ2​)(1−a2/(ζ1​ζ2​))=0; two distinct exterior points cannot satisfy the second factor since ∣ζ1​ζ2​∣>1>a2. Thus the exterior map is one-to-one, mapping the exterior of the unit circle conformally onto the exterior of the ellipse.

At large ∣ζ∣, z=ζ+O(ζ−1), so the map tends to the identity. Its exterior inverse has the expansion ζ=z−a2/z+O(z−3). Therefore the physical complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow is w(z)=W(ζ(z)), with
w(z)=Ue−iαz+zU(eiα−a2e−iα)​+O(z−3).
(19)
Its leading term is unchanged, so the far-field velocity still has magnitude U and inclination α.
The chain rule gives the complex velocity:
u−iv=dz/dζdW/dζ​=U1−a2/ζ2e−iα−eiα/ζ2​=Uζ2−a2e−iαζ2−eiα​.​
(20)
The parameter ζ denotes the unique exterior preimage of z. If an expression solely in z is desired, take
ζ(z)=2z+z2−4a2​​,
(21)
where the exterior holomorphic square root is chosen so that z2−4a2​∼z at infinity. Its branch points lie inside the ellipse. This is uniform potential flow around an ellipse.

The complex velocity vanishes where e−iαζ2−eiα=0, provided its denominator is nonzero. Thus ζ=±eiα. Both lie on the unit circle, and ζ2−a2=0 since a<1. Mapping these points into the physical plane gives
zstag​=±[(1+a2)cosα+i(1−a2)sinα].​
(22)
Equivalently, the two stagnation points are the opposite points (Acosα,Bsinα) and (−Acosα,−Bsinα) on the ellipse. The conformal map preserves the zeros of the complex velocity here because its derivative is finite and nonzero on the boundary.

The total velocity fields on the two sides are
u+​=(U,0)+∇ϕ+​,u−​=∇ϕ−​.
(23)
Each base flow is divergence-free. Incompressibility therefore makes both disturbance velocity potentials satisfy the Laplace equation:
ϕ+,xx​+ϕ+,yy​=0,ϕ−,xx​+ϕ−,yy​=0.​
(24)
At the fixed horizontal walls, impermeability requires zero vertical velocity:
ϕ+,y​(x,h,t)=0,ϕ−,y​(x,−h,t)=0.​
(25)
The discontinuity in tangential base velocity at the interface is a vortex sheet. The assumption of irrotational flow is understood separately in each open fluid region; it cannot hold distributionally across that sheet when U=0.

The interface is material. Applying the kinematic boundary condition to y−η(x,t)=0 gives, exactly,
ϕ−,y​=ηt​+ϕ−,x​ηx​,ϕ+,y​=ηt​+(U+ϕ+,x​)ηx​(y=η).
(26)
The disturbance velocities and η are small. Products of two disturbance quantities are second order, and a Taylor expansion moves their evaluation to y=0 at linear order. Hence
ϕ−,y​=ηt​,ϕ+,y​=ηt​+Uηx​(y=0).​
(27)
The extra upper-fluid term is advection of the displaced interface by the base uniform flow.
Use the Unsteady Bernoulli equation separately on each side, with constant mass density ρ. There are no restoring terms specified at this internal interface, and its pressure is continuous. Choose the time-dependent potential gauges so that the undisturbed pressure is p0​ on both sides. Subtracting the base values from Bernoulli's principle gives, at linear order,
p−′​=−ρϕ−,t​,p+′​=−ρ(ϕ+,t​+Uϕ+,x​).
(28)
The quadratic disturbance-velocity terms have been discarded, while the cross term with the base velocity is first order and must remain. Equating the perturbation pressures yields the third condition:
ϕ−,t​=ϕ+,t​+Uϕ+,x​(y=0).​
(29)

c OurBigBook.com
Use complex normal modes and take their real parts at the end. Write η=Aei(kx−ωt), with k>0. The Laplace equation and the wall Neumann boundary conditions give the vertical forms
ϕ+​=C+​cosh(k(y−h))ei(kx−ωt),ϕ−​=C−​cosh(k(y+h))ei(kx−ωt).
(30)
At y=0, the two kinematic boundary conditions become
−ksinh(kh)C+​=−i(ω−kU)A,ksinh(kh)C−​=−iωA.
(31)
Thus
C+​=ksinh(kh)i(ω−kU)A​,C−​=−ksinh(kh)iωA​.
(32)
The dynamic condition is
ωC−​cosh(kh)=(ω−kU)C+​cosh(kh).
(33)
Substituting the two amplitudes and cancelling the common nonzero depth factor gives the dispersion relation for a vortex sheet in a symmetric rigid channel:
ω2+(ω−kU)2=0.
(34)
Equivalently,
(ω−2kU​)2=−4k2U2​,ω=2kU​±i2kU​.​
(35)
For the specified U>0, the plus sign gives positive temporal growth rate γ=kU/2 in the convention e−iωt. A real growing interface displacement is therefore
η(x,t)=A0​ekUt/2cos(kx−2kU​t).​
(36)
The other branch decays at the same rate. Consequently the interface is linearly unstable, exhibiting Kelvin-Helmholtz instability. There is no nonzero-amplitude mode with a purely real temporal frequency for k>0 and U=0; the paper's sinusoidal notation must allow a complex frequency, with the physical displacement obtained by a real part.
The wave pattern is advected at speed U/2, the mean of the two base velocities. The growth rate is independent of h here because both layers have the same mass density and the same depth, making their factors coth(kh) cancel. In an asymmetric channel those inertia factors would differ. In the zero-shear limit U=0, the two roots merge at zero and there is no exponential growth.
