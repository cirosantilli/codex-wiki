Solution

a OurBigBook.com
Write the planar velocity field as u=(u,v,0), with k=(0,0,1). Expanding the curl gives
u=∇×(ψk)=(ψy​,−ψx​,0),∇ψ=k×u=(−v,u,0).
(1)
Consequently, for a piecewise smooth path γ from the origin to x=(x,y),
ψ(x)=ψ(0)+∫γ​(k×u)⋅dr=ψ(0)+∫γ​(udy−vdx).​
(2)
The arbitrary additive constant ψ(0) may be set to zero. The integrand is exactly ∇ψ⋅dr, so the fundamental theorem for line integrals proves path independence immediately: the difference between two paths is the line integral of a gradient around a closed curve, which is zero.
One can also see the local integrability condition from Green's theorem:
∮(udy−vdx)=∬(ux​+vy​)dxdy=0,
(3)
because the given representation enforces incompressibility. This second proof applies whenever the region between the paths lies in the regular fluid domain. The question already assumes a globally defined streamfunction; without that assumption, incompressibility alone would guarantee a global streamfunction on a simply connected domain, but a hole carrying nonzero volume flux could obstruct it.

Substitute the given Stokes streamfunction representation into the divergence in spherical coordinates. The mixed derivatives cancel:
∇⋅u​=r21​∂r​(sinθΨθ​​)+rsinθ1​∂θ​(−rΨr​​)=r2sinθΨrθ​−Ψθr​​=0.​
(4)
Thus incompressibility is automatic for a smooth Stokes streamfunction. Since this is an axisymmetric flow with no azimuthal velocity, the only possibly nonzero component of its vorticity is
(∇×u)φ​​=r1​[∂r​(−sinθΨr​​)−∂θ​(r2sinθΨθ​​)]=−r3sinθ1​[r2Ψrr​+sinθ∂θ​(sinθΨθ​​)].​
(5)
Define the differential operator in the Stokes streamfunction equation for irrotational flow
LΨ=r2Ψrr​+Ψθθ​−cotθΨθ​.
(6)
The stated equation is LΨ=0, and therefore
∇⋅u=0,∇×u=0.​
(7)
The irrotational flow has a velocity potential locally, and globally on a regular simply connected domain. Regularity of the physical velocity field supplies the limiting interpretation on the polar axis, where the coordinate expressions contain sinθ in the denominator.

Put s=a2/r and define the image term G(r,θ)=(r/a)Ψ(s,θ). The key step is to verify that inversion preserves the Stokes streamfunction equation for irrotational flow. Direct differentiation gives
Gr​=a1​(Ψ−sΨs​),Grr​=ars2​Ψss​,Gθθ​−cotθGθ​=ar​(Ψθθ​−cotθΨθ​),
(8)
where the quantities on the right are evaluated at (s,θ). Hence
Lr​G=ar​[s2Ψss​+Ψθθ​−cotθΨθ​]=ar​Ls​Ψ=0.
(9)
By linearity, Ψ=Ψ−G obeys the same equation and describes an incompressible flow and an irrotational flow wherever the original exterior flow is regular.
On the sphere, s=a and the two terms cancel identically:
Ψ(a,θ)=0,ur​(a,θ)=a2sinθ∂θ​Ψ(a,θ)​=0.​
(10)
Thus the sphere is a stream surface and satisfies the no-penetration boundary condition. Tangential slip is allowed in this potential flow model. For every finite r>a, the image argument satisfies 0<s<a, where Ψ has no singularities. The prefactor r/a is regular there. Therefore the construction creates no new finite exterior singularities; any original exterior singularities remain those of the prescribed incident flow. This is the sphere theorem for a Stokes streamfunction.
For the far-field claim, the stated bound gives
∣G(r,θ)∣≤ar​cra2​=ca(r sufficiently large).
(11)
One must compare velocities, rather than require the correction to the Stokes streamfunction to tend to zero. Smoothness at the original origin, together with Ψ(0,θ)=0, gives Ψs​=O(1) and Ψθ​=O(s) there. Thus Gr​=O(r−1) and Gθ​=O(1), and the correction has
δur​=−r2sinθGθ​​=O(r−2),δuθ​=rsinθGr​​=O(r−2).
(12)
These estimates hold away from the coordinate poles, with the regular-axis limits supplied by the original smooth physical flow. Consequently
u−u⟶0(r→∞).​
(13)
The far-field velocity field is unchanged. For a fully smooth incident velocity field at the origin, the usual regular expansion is stronger and makes the sphere disturbance decay as r−3.

d OurBigBook.com
The incident uniform flow Uez​ has spherical components ur​=Ucosθ, uθ​=−Usinθ. Integrating the Stokes streamfunction formulas, with the additive constant chosen to vanish on the axis, gives
Ψ=21​Ur2sin2θ.
(14)
It is regular at the origin. Applying the sphere theorem for a Stokes streamfunction yields
Ψ(r,θ)=2U​(r2−ra3​)sin2θ.​
(15)
The resulting velocity components are
ur​=U(1−r3a3​)cosθ,uθ​=−U(1+2r3a3​)sinθ.
(16)
To find the velocity potential, integrate ϕr​=ur​ and verify r−1ϕθ​=uθ​. This gives
ϕ(r,θ)=U(r+2r2a3​)cosθ+constant.​
(17)
The first term is the incident uniform flow, and the second is a potential dipole located inside the excluded sphere. At r=a, the radial velocity vanishes, while the far-field velocity tends to Uez​. The poles are stagnation points; the surface tangential speed reaches 3U/2 at the equator. These formulas describe uniform potential flow around a fixed sphere.

Orient C counterclockwise around the body. For an arc element ds, the body-outward unit normal vector obeys nds=(dy,−dx). The pressure acts normally into the body, so its force per unit length in the suppressed third direction is
dF=−pnds=(−pdy,pdx).
(18)
Integrating gives
Fx​=−∮C​pdy,Fy​=∮C​pdx.​
(19)
Taking positive torque to mean counterclockwise about the origin, the infinitesimal moment is dM=xdFy​−ydFx​=p(xdx+ydy). Hence
M=∮C​p(xdx+ydy).​
(20)
All three expressions are per unit span. They are the normal-pressure contributions assumed by the inviscid model; a viscous traction would require extra terms. A constant pressure contributes zero net force and torque on a closed body, since ∮dx=∮dy=∮d(x2+y2)=0.

Use the complex velocity q(z)=w′(z)=u−iv. For this steady, constant-mass density, irrotational flow with no body force, the applicable Bernoulli equation is
p+2ρ​∣q∣2=P∗​,
(21)
with a single constant P∗​ throughout the connected regular fluid region. The body's impermeability makes the velocity tangent to C. Thus qdz is real there, giving
qdz=qˉ​dzˉ,∣q∣2dzˉ=q2dz.
(22)
The complex force from part (a) is
Fx​−iFy​​=−i∮C​pdzˉ=2iρ​∮C​∣q∣2dzˉ=2iρ​∮C​q2dz,​
(23)
where the constant-P∗​ term vanishes. This proves the Blasius force theorem:
Fx​−iFy​=2iρ​∮C​(w′(z))2dz.​
(24)
For the moment, observe that
zdzˉ=(xdx+ydy)+i(ydx−xdy).
(25)
Therefore M=Re∮C​pzdzˉ. The constant pressure contribution has zero real part, because xdx+ydy=21​d(x2+y2). Substituting Bernoulli's principle and the same tangency identity gives the Blasius moment theorem:
M=−2ρ​Re∮C​z(w′(z))2dz.​
(26)
The real-part operation is essential: ∮zdzˉ itself need not vanish, but its real part does.

c OurBigBook.com
The method of images places an equal line source at −ia, outside the physical upper half-plane. A convenient complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow is
w(z)=2πQ​[log(z−ia)+log(z+ia)],w′(z)=π(z2+a2)Qz​.​
(27)
The two logarithms can be combined locally as (Q/(2π))log(z2+a2); their branches of the complex logarithm affect only the potential convention, not the single-valued complex velocity. The point source in this two-dimensional problem has the line source normalization: its outward volume flux is Q per unit span.
Writing R−2​=x2+(y−a)2, R+2​=x2+(y+a)2, and using w′=u−iv, the velocity field is
u=2πQ​(R−2​x​+R+2​x​),v=2πQ​(R−2​y−a​+R+2​y+a​).​
(28)
On the wall y=0, the vertical components cancel:
v(x,0)=0,u(x,0)=π(x2+a2)Qx​.​
(29)
This is the required no-penetration boundary condition. Tangential slip is permitted. At large distance the velocity decays as 1/r, and near z=ia its singular part is exactly that of the specified source; the reflected source is regular throughout the fluid. Away from the injection point, this is an incompressible flow and an irrotational flow.

d OurBigBook.com
Since the velocity tends to zero at infinity, the steady Bernoulli equation gives p+ρ∣u∣2/2=p∞​. On the wall, therefore,
p(x,0)−p∞​=−2π2ρQ2​(x2+a2)2x2​.
(30)
Compute the finite source-induced force relative to uniform ambient pressure, or equivalently use the gauge pressure p−p∞​. The fluid lies above the wall, so its normal force on the wall points in −ey​ for positive pressure. The negative excess pressure found here consequently produces an upward net force, toward the source.
For the symmetric section −R≤x≤R, there is no horizontal force, and the vertical excess force is
Fy​(R)​=−∫−RR​(p−p∞​)dx=2π2ρQ2​[a1​arctanaR​−R2+a2R​].​
(31)
Here an antiderivative of x2/(x2+a2)2 is (1/(2a))arctan(x/a)−x/[2(x2+a2)]. Taking R→∞ gives the hydrodynamic attraction of a wall to a line source:
Fx​=0,Fy​=4πaρQ2​.​
(32)
Each component is a force per unit length of the wall in the out-of-plane direction. The result is an attraction even though the source sends fluid away from itself: faster tangential motion reduces the wall pressure.
The excess torque about the origin is
M(R)=−∫−RR​x(p−p∞​)dx=0
(33)
for every R, because the integrand is odd. Thus the symmetric infinite-wall limit is
M=0.​
(34)
The symmetric prescription matters: the separate one-sided moment integrals grow logarithmically, whereas the force integral converges absolutely. This is the appropriate Cauchy principal value for the infinite wall. If one instead asks for the absolute fluid force with no balancing ambient pressure on the other side, its vertical component on a finite section is −2Rp∞​+Fy​(R) and has no finite infinite-wall limit. The finite answer above is the flow-induced load.

a OurBigBook.com
Let V(t) be any material volume, moving with the velocity field u. The Reynolds transport theorem in the required form is
dtd​∫V(t)​fdV=∫V(t)​[∂t​f+∇⋅(fu)]dV,
(35)
applied componentwise when f is vector-valued. Mass conservation gives
0=dtd​∫V(t)​ρdV=∫V(t)​[ρt​+∇⋅(ρu)]dV.
(36)
Since the material volume is arbitrary, the local continuity equation follows:
ρt​+∇⋅(ρu)=0.​
(37)
For an inviscid fluid with no body force, the balance of linear momentum equates the rate of change of total momentum to the boundary pressure force:
dtd​∫V(t)​ρudV=−∫∂V(t)​pndS=−∫V(t)​∇pdV,
(38)
using the divergence theorem. The integrand on the left expands as
∂t​(ρu)+∇⋅(ρu⊗u)=ρDtDu​+u[ρt​+∇⋅(ρu)].
(39)
The bracket vanishes by mass conservation, leaving the Euler momentum equation
ρDtDu​=−∇p,DtD​=∂t​+u⋅∇.​
(40)
Unlike the incompressible flow case, the mass density need not be constant along a fluid element.

The rest state is ρ=ρ0​, p=p0​, u=0. At linear order in the perturbations, the continuity equation, Euler momentum equation and the given equation of state become
ρt′​+ρ0​∇⋅u=0,ρ0​ut​=−∇p′=−c2∇ρ′.
(41)
For the acoustic component take u=∇ϕ. This is consistent with initially zero vorticity, since taking the curl of the linear momentum equation gives ∂t​(∇×u)=0. Any pre-existing linear vortical component is time independent and is separate from the potential oscillations being sought.
Spatial integration of ρ0​∇ϕt​=−c2∇ρ′ gives ρ0​ϕt​+c2ρ′=K(t). Adding a function of time to the velocity potential removes K(t) without altering the velocity field. Hence
ρ′=−c2ρ0​​ϕt​,p′=−ρ0​ϕt​.​
(42)
Substitute the first relation into mass conservation:
−c2ρ0​​ϕtt​+ρ0​∇2ϕ=0.
(43)
Thus the velocity potential satisfies the wave equation
ϕtt​−c2∇2ϕ=0.​
(44)
Differentiating that equation in time and using the same density-potential relation gives
ρtt′​−c2∇2ρ′=0.​
(45)
Alternatively, differentiate the linear continuity equation in time and take the divergence of the linear momentum equation. The coefficient c is the sound speed, since dp/dρ=c2.

c OurBigBook.com
The fixed upper and lower walls have zero normal velocity. Since v=ϕy​, their no-penetration boundary conditions are
ϕy​=0at y=0,H.​
(46)
For the moving end wall, use its level-set equation F(x,y,t)=x−X(y,t)=0. The kinematic boundary condition DF/Dt=0 gives the exact impermeability relation
u=Xt​+vXy​at x=X(y,t).
(47)
Both v and Xy​ are first order, so their product is second order. A Taylor expansion from x=X to x=0 changes the first-order perturbation velocity only at second order as well. Thus
ϕx​(0,y,t)=Xt​(y,t)=ϵωcos(ωt)cosHπy​.​
(48)
At infinity, select a bounded response with no waves incident from x=+∞. A propagating acoustic waveguide mode must satisfy the outgoing radiation condition for a waveguide mode, while a nonpropagating mode must decay. For a time dependence e−iωt, a propagating axial factor has the form eiqx, q>0, and obeys Φx​−iqΦ→0. An evanescent factor is e−αx, α>0.
Requiring ϕ→0 for all frequencies would incorrectly exclude the radiated traveling waves. In this lossless channel they retain finite amplitude arbitrarily far downstream.

d OurBigBook.com
Let μ=π/H. The end-wall forcing has transverse factor cos(μy), which already satisfies both fixed-wall Neumann boundary conditions. By linearity and separation of variables, seek its periodic response as
ϕ(x,y,t)=Re{Φ(x)cos(μy)e−iωt}.
(49)
Substitution into the wave equation and the moving-wall condition gives
Φ′′+(c2ω2​−μ2)Φ=0,Φ′(0)=ϵω.
(50)
The acoustic waveguide cutoff is therefore
ωc​=cμ=Hπc​.​
(51)
It is the lowest nonzero transverse-mode frequency. The uniform transverse mode has no cutoff, but is not excited here because ∫0H​cos(πy/H)dy=0.
For 0<ω<ωc​, put α=μ2−ω2/c2​>0. The axial equation has solutions e±αx. Boundedness removes the growing exponential, and Φ′(0)=ϵω fixes the coefficient. Thus
ϕ=−αϵω​e−αxcosHπy​cos(ωt)(0<ω<ωc​).​
(52)
This evanescent wave oscillates in time but decays with penetration length 1/α. Its axial velocity is proportional to cos(ωt), while its excess pressure p′=−ρ0​ϕt​ is proportional to sin(ωt). They are in phase quadrature, so their mean product, the axial acoustic energy flux, is zero.
For ω>ωc​, put q=ω2/c2−μ2​>0. The axial solutions are e±iqx. The outgoing condition selects eiqx for the chosen time convention, and
iqΦ(0)=ϵω,Φ(x)=iqϵω​eiqx.
(53)
Taking the real part gives
ϕ=qϵω​cosHπy​sin(qx−ωt)(ω>ωc​).​
(54)
Its axial velocity is ϵωcos(πy/H)cos(qx−ωt), so the moving-wall condition is satisfied with the correct sign. This is a traveling acoustic waveguide mode carrying energy toward positive x. Its dispersion relation is
ω2=c2(q2+μ2),cphase​=qω​>c,cgroup​=dqdω​=ωc2q​<c.
(55)
The larger axial phase velocity does not imply faster energy transport: the group velocity controls that transport.
At ω=ωc​, the axial equation becomes Φ′′=0. The imposed nonzero derivative forces Φ=ϵωc​x+ a constant, which is unbounded. Thus there is no bounded periodic response at exact cutoff for this infinite, undamped, velocity-driven model. The divergent coefficients as q→0 or α→0 also show why the assumed small perturbations fail sufficiently close to cutoff at fixed forcing. Initial conditions can add free transients; the displayed formulas give the selected periodic forced response.
