The velocity of a fluid element is the time derivative of its position with its Lagrangian coordinates X,Y held fixed. Direct differentiation gives
x˙y˙​​=−e−t(XcosΩt+YsinΩt)+Ωe−t(YcosΩt−XsinΩt)=−x+Ωe−2ty,=et(YcosΩt−XsinΩt)−Ωet(XcosΩt+YsinΩt)=y−Ωe2tx.​
(1)
Replacing the Lagrangian coordinates by the current Eulerian coordinates therefore gives the velocity field
u(x,y,t)=(−x+Ωe−2ty, y−Ωe2tx)​.
(2)
The distinction between following a fluid element and differentiating at fixed Eulerian coordinates is what connects the given deformation map to this velocity field.

For a planar incompressible flow, the divergence of the velocity field must vanish. Here
∇⋅u=∂x∂u​+∂y∂v​=−1+1=0.
(3)
Equivalently, the Jacobian determinant of the deformation map is
det∂(X,Y)∂(x,y)​=e−tet(cos2Ωt+sin2Ωt)=1,
(4)
so every material area is preserved. However, the out-of-plane vorticity is
ωz​=∂x∂v​−∂y∂u​=−Ω(e2t+e−2t)=−2Ωcosh(2t)​.
(5)
Since Ω>0, this vorticity never vanishes. The motion is an incompressible flow but is not an irrotational flow; its local spin is clockwise.

Adopt the streamfunction convention u=ψy​, v=−ψx​. Integrating the first component of the velocity field in y gives ψ=−xy+21​Ωe−2ty2+f(x,t). The second component then requires fx​=Ωe2tx. Thus, omitting an arbitrary function of time,
ψ(x,y,t)=2Ω​(e2tx2+e−2ty2)−xy​.
(6)
At a fixed instant, the streamlines are the connected curves ψ=K. To classify this quadratic form, introduce the invertible scaled and rotated coordinates
ξ=etx,υ=e−ty,s=2​ξ+υ​,d=2​ξ−υ​.
(7)
Then
2ψ=(Ω−1)s2+(Ω+1)d2​.
(8)
For Ω>1, both coefficients are positive. Every K>0 therefore gives an ellipse, and K=0 is the stagnation point at the origin. For 0<Ω<1, the quadratic form is an indefinite quadratic form: each K=0 gives a hyperbola. The level K=0 consists of the straight separatrices
d=±1+Ω1−Ω​​s,
(9)
whose rays approach or leave the origin; the origin itself is a stagnation point. At the transition Ω=1, ψ=d2, so the nonstationary streamlines are parallel straight lines and d=0 is a line of stagnation points. These are instantaneous streamlines; the time dependence means that they need not coincide with pathlines.

i OurBigBook.com
Use w=ϕ+iψ for the complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow, so its complex velocity is q=w′=u−iv. The incident uniform flow and line source have
f(z)=Uz+log(z−b).
(10)
The coefficient of the complex logarithm is one because a line source of strength Q has complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow (Q/(2π))log(z−b), and here Q=2π. Applying the Milne-Thomson circle theorem to the unit circle gives
w(z)=f(z)+f(1/zˉ)​=Uz+log(z−b)+zU​+log(1/z−b).
(11)
Since 1/z−b=−b(z−1/b)/z, locally consistent branches of the complex logarithm give
w(z)=Uz+zU​+log(z−b)+log(z−1/b)−logz+C​.
(12)
Changes of branch only alter the constant in a local expression; w′ is single valued away from its singularities. The added terms represent a two-dimensional potential doublet at the origin, an image line source at 1/b, and a compensating line sink at the origin. The image line source and line sink have equal and opposite strengths, so no net volume flux enters the cylinder.
On ∣z∣=1, the Milne-Thomson circle theorem makes w=f(z)+f(z)​ up to a constant. Consequently the streamfunction is constant there, establishing the no-penetration boundary condition. All line source strengths are real, and the two-dimensional potential doublet has zero circulation, so the circulation around the cylinder is zero. There is no additional line vortex term.

Let Fx​,Fy​ denote the force per unit length exerted by the fluid on the cylinder, and let ρ be the constant mass density. For a counterclockwise contour C around the unit circle, the Blasius force theorem gives
Fx​−iFy​=2iρ​∮C​q(z)2dz,q(z)=U−z2U​+z−b1​+z−a1​−z1​,a=b1​.
(13)
An efficient use of the residue theorem finds the sum of the enclosed residues by subtracting the external pole at b from the sum of all finite residues. At large z,
q(z)=U+z1​+O(z−2),q(z)2=U2+z2U​+O(z−2).
(14)
The sum of all finite residues of q2 is therefore 2U. At b, write q=(z−b)−1+H(z), with H a holomorphic function near b. Then
Resz=b​q2=2H(b),H(b)=U−b2U​+b−a1​−b1​.
(15)
Thus
∮C​q2dz=2πi[2U−2H(b)],Fx​−iFy​=2πρ[H(b)−U].
(16)
Using a=1/b and 1/(b−a)−1/b=1/[b(b2−1)] gives
Fx​=2πρ(b(b2−1)1​−b2U​),Fy​=0​.
(17)
The vanishing vertical force also follows from reflection symmetry about the real axis. This force on a unit cylinder due to a uniform flow and an external source uses the counterclockwise orientation in the Blasius force theorem to fix the sign of the horizontal force.

The Joukowski map is a holomorphic function for z=0, with
g′(z)=1−z21​.
(20)
A holomorphic function is a local conformal map wherever its derivative is nonzero. Hence the two finite nonzero critical points are
z=1,z=−1​.
(21)
They lie on the boundary of the fluid region. At z=0 the displayed formula has a pole and is undefined as a map to the finite complex plane; zero is outside the fluid region. Thus g is locally conformal everywhere in the fluid.

ii OurBigBook.com
For z=x+iy,
Img(z)=y(1−x2+y21​).
(22)
Both factors are positive in the fluid region, so its image is contained in the upper half-plane. The boundary correspondence is also explicit: on the upper unit semicircle, z=eiθ gives g(z)=2cosθ∈[−2,2], while the real rays x>1 and x<−1 map onto the rays ζ>2 and ζ<−2.
To prove the image is the whole upper half-plane, fix any Imζ>0. The equation g(z)=ζ is
z2−ζz+1=0.
(23)
Its roots have product one. Neither root can have unit modulus, since that would make ζ=z+1/z real. Exactly one root therefore has modulus greater than one. For that root the formula for Img forces y>0, so it belongs to the fluid region. Moreover, if g(z1​)=g(z2​), then
(z1​−z2​)(1−z1​z2​1​)=0.
(24)
Two distinct roots in the fluid region would require z1​z2​=1, impossible when both moduli exceed one. Thus the Joukowski map of the upper exterior of a unit disk is a bijection from the fluid region onto the upper half-plane.

The quadratic formula gives two algebraic possibilities. To select one consistent holomorphic square root, set
S(ζ)=ζ−2​ζ+2​,Imζ>0,
(25)
where each factor is the principal square root. Both arguments lie in the upper half-plane, so neither crosses its branch cut. This choice satisfies S(ζ)2=ζ2−4 and S(ζ)∼ζ at large ζ in the upper half-plane. The required inverse function is
z=g−1(ζ)=2ζ+S(ζ)​=2ζ+ζ−2​ζ+2​​​.
(26)
For ζ=iR, R>0, this gives z=i(R+R2+4​)/2, whose modulus exceeds one. As ζ varies continuously in the upper half-plane, this root cannot cross ∣z∣=1, since that would make ζ real. Hence the selected root always lies in the fluid region. The other root is its reciprocal and lies inside the unit disk. Simply writing the principal square root of ζ2−4 would not specify this branch correctly throughout the upper half-plane.

Take positive Γ to mean counterclockwise circulation. A line vortex at ζ0​ has complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow Γlog(ζ−ζ0​)/(2πi). The method of images imposes the no-penetration boundary condition on the real axis by placing an image line vortex of strength −Γ at ζˉ​0​. Thus
W(ζ)=2πiΓ​log(ζ−ζˉ​0​ζ−ζ0​​)+C​.
(28)

ImW=−2πΓ​log​ζ−ζˉ​0​ζ−ζ0​​​=constant.

The physical complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow is w(z)=W(g(z)). Applying the chain rule gives the complex velocity
u−iv=w′(z)=2πiΓ​(1−z21​)[z+z−1−c−c−11​−z+z−1−cˉ−cˉ−11​]​.

g(z)−g(c)=z(z−c)(z−c−1)​.
(32)
Factoring the image expression in the same way makes the −1/z terms cancel, leaving
u−iv=2πiΓ​[z−c1​+z−c−11​−z−cˉ1​−z−cˉ−11​]​.

A free point vortex is advected by the regular part of the velocity field at its position. Its own singular complex velocity Γ/[2πi(z−c)] must first be subtracted. The image expression therefore gives
x˙c​−iy˙​c​=z→clim​[w′(z)−2πi(z−c)Γ​]=2πiΓ​[c−c−11​−c−cˉ1​−c−cˉ−11​]​.
(34)
Equivalently, c˙ is the complex conjugate of the boxed expression. This is the instantaneous velocity, evaluated with the current value of c.
To see why mapping the image velocity alone would miss a term, expand the mapped physical singularity:
g(z)−g(c)g′(z)​=z−c1​+2g′(c)g′′(c)​+O(z−c).
(35)
Thus the same point-vortex drift under a conformal map can be written
x˙c​−iy˙​c​=2πiΓ​[2g′(c)g′′(c)​−g(c)−g(c)​g′(c)​].
(36)
The finite term involving g′′ is part of the physical regular velocity field.
If c=id with d>1, direct substitution into the image expression gives
x˙c​−iy˙​c​​=2πΓ​[−d2+1d​+2d1​+d2−1d​]=2πΓ​[2d1​+d4−12d​]​.​
(37)
The bracket is strictly positive, and the result is real. Hence a positive line vortex initially moves horizontally to the right, a negative line vortex moves to the left, and the initial vertical velocity is zero. As d→∞, the leading term Γ/(4πd) is the familiar drift of a point vortex above a straight wall.

For an irrotational flow, write u=∇ϕ. The incompressible flow condition then gives the Laplace equation
ϕxx​+ϕyy​=0,−h<y<η(x,t)​.
(38)
At leading order in a small disturbance this equation holds on −h<y<0. Including gravity, the Unsteady Bernoulli equation is
ϕt​+21​∣∇ϕ∣2+ρp​+gy=B(t).
(39)
Choose the time-dependent additive freedom in the velocity potential so that B(t)=p0​/ρ. Neglecting surface tension, pressure continuity at the free surface gives the exact dynamic condition
ϕt​(x,η,t)+21​∣∇ϕ(x,η,t)∣2+ρp1​​cos(x−ct)+gη=0.
(40)
The free surface is a material boundary: applying the material derivative to y−η(x,t)=0 yields the kinematic boundary condition for a free-surface graph
ηt​+ϕx​(x,η,t)ηx​=ϕy​(x,η,t).
(41)
For a small disturbance, the Taylor expansion from y=η to y=0 changes the first-order velocity potential and its partial derivatives only at second order. Both ∣∇ϕ∣2 and ϕx​ηx​ are also second-order terms. The linearized free-surface boundary conditions are therefore
ϕt​+ρp1​​cos(x−ct)+gη=0,ϕy​=ηt​(y=0)​.
(42)
The fixed bed has the no-penetration boundary condition
ϕy​=0(y=−h)​.
(43)
The required approximation is small surface elevation, small wave steepness, and small velocity perturbations. The stated p1​≪p0​ alone does not ensure this approximation near resonance.

Seek the travelling forced component of the surface elevation in the form η=Acosχ, where χ=x−ct and A is a signed coefficient. The forcing has wavenumber one. Separation of variables in the Laplace equation, followed by the bed no-penetration boundary condition, gives
ϕ=Bcosh(y+h)sinχ.
(44)
Since ηt​=Acsinχ, the surface kinematic boundary condition requires Bsinhh=Ac, so
ϕ=sinhhAc​cosh(y+h)sin(x−ct)​.
(45)
At y=0, ϕt​=−Ac2cothhcosχ. The dynamic member of the linearized free-surface boundary conditions therefore becomes
A(g−c2cothh)+ρp1​​=0.
(46)
Consequently the signed response and the nonnegative amplitude are
A=−ρ(g−c2cothh)p1​​,a(c)=∣A∣=ρ∣g−c2cothh∣p1​​​.
(47)
The travelling-pressure response of finite-depth surface gravity waves is opposite in phase to the imposed pressure below the resonant speed and in phase above it. These expressions describe the travelling forced component; freely propagating surface gravity waves can be superposed to meet additional initial conditions.

The denominator of the travelling response vanishes at
c∗=gtanhh​​.
(48)
For the positive speeds in the paper, a finite travelling response exists whenever c=c∗. At c=c∗, the dynamic condition would reduce to p1​/ρ=0, which is impossible. The finite-depth dispersion relation of a surface gravity wave with wavenumber k is ω2=gktanh(kh). At k=1, its phase speed ω/k equals c∗: the moving pressure pattern drives a natural surface gravity wave at its own speed, producing resonance.
The restriction concerns a bounded travelling response. To distinguish this from existence of a solution of an initial value problem, set T=tanhh, P=p1​/ρ, and ω∗2​=gT. If
η(x,t)=Re(H(t)eix),ϕ(x,y,t)=Re(sinhhH˙(t)​cosh(y+h)eix),
(49)
the linearized free-surface boundary conditions reduce to the forced harmonic oscillator
H¨+ω∗2​H=−PTe−ict​.
(50)
For example, if the forcing is switched on at t=0 from a flat surface with zero initial velocity, then, for c=ω∗​,
H(t)=ω∗2​−c2PT​[cos(ω∗​t)−iω∗​c​sin(ω∗​t)−e−ict].
(51)
This satisfies H(0)=H˙(0)=0 and contains both free oscillations and the forced component. At c=ω∗​ a solution of the initial value problem still exists, but its particular solution contains
Hsec​(t)=−2ω∗​iPT​te−iω∗​t,
(52)
which grows linearly with time. Thus the ideal inviscid flow model predicts resonant growth rather than a finite constant amplitude. Additional free oscillations can set the initial conditions but cannot remove that secular growth.
