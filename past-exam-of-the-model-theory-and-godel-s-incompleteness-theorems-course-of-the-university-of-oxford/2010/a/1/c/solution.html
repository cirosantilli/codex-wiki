An arithmetic theory is one-consistent if there is no bounded arithmetic formula δ(x) for which it proves ∃xδ(x) and also proves ¬δ(n) for every standard natural number n. A finite tuple of existential witnesses can be coded by one natural number. The associated Sigma-1 soundness property says that every Σ1​ sentence it proves is true in the standard natural numbers.
Suppose first that the theory is one-consistent, but proves a false Σ1​ sentence ∃xδ(x). For every standard n, ¬δ(n) is then a true bounded sentence. Bounded completeness makes each such sentence a theorem, violating one-consistency.
Conversely suppose the theory is Σ1​-sound. If it proved both the existential sentence and every negated standard instance, soundness would supply an actual witness n. Its positive instance δ(n) is a true bounded sentence and hence provable. Together with the assumed negative instance this would make the theory inconsistent; an inconsistent theory proves a false Σ1​ sentence, contrary to soundness. Thus
one-consistency ⟺ Σ1​-soundness​
