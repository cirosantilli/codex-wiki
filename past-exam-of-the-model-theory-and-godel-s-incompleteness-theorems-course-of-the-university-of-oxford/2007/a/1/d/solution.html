Start with the supplied bounded-complete, one-consistent first-order theory T proving a false first-order sentence F. Replace it by its deductive closure, which preserves these properties and contains F. Enumerate the arithmetic first-order sentences as X0​,X1​,…. Recursively extend the current first-order theory by either Xn​ or ¬Xn​, choosing an extension which remains one-consistent as permitted by the supplied lemma, and then take its deductive closure. Call the resulting increasing sequence Tn​, with T0​ the initial first-order theory, and put S=⋃n​Tn​.
Every stage is still bounded-complete. By (b), every stage has Sigma-1 soundness. If S proved a false Sigma-1 first-order sentence, the finite formal proof would use assumptions from one stage Tn​, contradicting its Sigma-1 soundness. Thus S has Sigma-1 soundness and is bounded-complete, so (b) makes it one-consistent.
This soundness of an arithmetic theory argument is essential: the definition of one-consistency involves infinitely many standard refutations, so it would be insufficient merely to say that a violation has a finite formal proof. What has a finite formal proof is the false existential sentence, and bounded completeness of an arithmetic theory is what turns one-consistency into the needed soundness of an arithmetic theory condition.
The union is deductively closed because each formal proof has finitely many assumptions. It contains either X or ¬X for every first-order sentence, by the numeralwise enumeration in arithmetic, and contains the false first-order sentence F. If it were omega-consistent, (c) would make it correct on all first-order sentences, contradicting F. We have therefore constructed
S complete and one-consistent, but not omega-consistent.​

Omega-consistency always implies one-consistency by restricting its matrices. This counterexample proves that the converse is strictly false.
