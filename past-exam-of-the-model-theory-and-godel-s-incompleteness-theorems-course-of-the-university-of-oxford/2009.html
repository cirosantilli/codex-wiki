Write □A for the arithmetical sentence asserting S-provability of A. If S⊢G, the first derivability condition gives S⊢□G. The stipulated fixed-point equivalence also gives S⊢¬□G. This contradicts consistency. Conversely an inconsistent classical theory proves every sentence, including G. Thus
S⊬G⟺S is consistent.​
(1)
Only the direction from an actual proof to its formalized provability is used here. There is no inference from a theorem □G to a theorem G, and no assumption that every theorem of S is true.

The Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions are: if S⊢A then S⊢□A; S⊢□(A→B)→(□A→□B); and S⊢□A→□□A. The first is an external rule about provability; the latter two are internal theorems. Standard numerical correctness and the stipulated Σ1​ form accompany the predicate in this question. These conditions do not include reflection □A→A.
Choose a fixed refutable sentence ⊥ and identify ConS​ with ¬□⊥. Different fixed refutable choices have provably equivalent consistency sentences, because S proves their equivalence to a contradiction. From ⊥→G, necessitation and distribution give □⊥→□G. Since G→¬□G, this yields G→ConS​.
For the converse, necessitate G→¬□G and distribute to get □G→□¬□G. Positive introspection also gives □G→□□G. The two boxed contradictories imply □⊥ by formalized propositional reasoning. Thus □G→□⊥, and contraposition gives ConS​→¬□G→G. Consequently
S⊢G↔ConS​.​
(2)
Finally the deduction theorem says that S+G is inconsistent exactly when S⊢¬G. The internal equivalence identifies S+G and S+ConS​ in their deductive consequences. Hence S⊬¬G exactly when S+ConS​ is consistent. Consistency of S alone need not ensure this stronger consistency condition.

For consistency of a negated-consistency extension, put A=S+¬ConS​. The arithmetized deduction theorem, together with double-negation elimination and formalized proof transformations, gives
S⊢□A​⊥↔□S​ConS​,S⊢ConA​↔¬□S​ConS​.

S⊢ConS​→ConS+¬ConS​​.​
(4)
This is an internal conditional statement. It does not assert that S proves its own consistency or permits the consistency premise to be discarded.

For unrestricted consistency self-strengthening is inconsistent, again take A=S+¬ConS​. In S++ the axiom ConS​ and the preceding implication prove ConA​. The additional rule then proves ConA+ConA​​.
However consistency of an extension implies consistency of its subtheory by the formalized inclusion of proofs: S⊢ConA​→ConS​. Thus the ordinary theory A+ConA​ contains ¬ConS​ and derives ConS​, so it has a finite contradiction proof. Checking that concrete proof in arithmetic gives S⊢¬ConA+ConA​​. This theorem is also present in S++, contradicting the result of the new rule. Therefore S++ is inconsistent.
The error in the added rule is its unrestricted internal use on arbitrary arithmetically presented systems, including systems containing false consistency assertions. A true external consistency progression for a specified sound system is not this unrestricted inference rule.

For separation of number sets in an arithmetic theory, a formula separates A from B in S if n∈A implies S⊢F(n), and n∈B implies S⊢¬F(n). No demand is made for numbers outside their union. Consistency forces disjointness when such a separator exists.

If S⊢¬R, then r is in the refutable set, so separation gives S⊢¬H(r), and the fixed-point equivalence gives S⊢R, again a contradiction. Thus
S⊬RandS⊬¬R.​

For Rosser separation by a bounded stage comparison, use the numeralwise enumeration in arithmetic properties meant by enumeration in S: membership supplies some standard stage with a provable positive instance, while nonmembership makes every standard-stage instance refutable. These are stronger than merely attaching a Σ1​ syntactic label to an arbitrary formula. The finite-initial-segment axiom lets us combine finitely many numeralwise refutations into a bounded universal assertion.
If n is provable, choose a standard stage p with S⊢P(n,p​). Consistency means n is not refutable. Therefore S⊢¬R(n,j​) for each j≤p. Using the displayed finite-segment axiom and equality substitution, combine these proofs to get S⊢∀z≤p​¬R(n,z). Existential introduction with stage p proves the separator at n.
If n is refutable, choose a standard stage q with S⊢R(n,q​). Consistency implies that n is not provable, so all standard instances of P(n,⋅) are refutable. In particular their finitely many instances through q give S⊢∀z≤q​¬P(n,z). For an arbitrary proposed witness z, the total-comparison axiom gives z≤q​ or q​≤z. In the first case its P conjunct fails; in the second case the known R instance at q contradicts its bounded no-refutation conjunct. Generalize on z to refute the existential separator. Hence the proposed first-proof-before-refutation formula separates the two sets.

The literal semantic hypothesis is insufficient: a semantic Gödel fixed point need not be canonical. A sentence can be false and provable in a consistent, unsound arithmetic theory, in which case both sides of the stipulated semantic biconditional can be false.
For a concrete counterexample, assume PA is consistent and set S=PA+¬ConPA​. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem makes this extension consistent: otherwise PA would prove its own consistency. It is still bounded-complete. Use ordinary numeralwise proof and refutation enumerators for this recursively axiomatized theory, and take G=¬ConPA​. This G is false in the standard natural numbers but is an axiom of S. Since it is provable, ∃yP(┌G┐,y) is true; its negation is false. Thus the semantic fixed-point biconditional required in this part is true, with both sides false.
Choose R by the usual canonical Rosser construction for these proof matrices. Its undecidability follows from the preceding parts, and it is a true Π1​ sentence: if it were false, its true Σ1​ negation would be provable in this bounded-complete theory, contradicting undecidability. Hence G is false and R true, so their proposed semantic equivalence is false. This counterexample meets the literal assumptions and does not exploit a missing source page.
Under the intended canonical Π1​ Gödel/Rosser convention, the expected answer does hold. Consistency plus bounded completeness implies Π1​ soundness: a false Π1​ theorem has a standard bounded counterexample whose negation the theory proves. If canonical G were false, its truth fixed point would make it provable, contradicting this soundness; thus G is true. Canonical R is true by the preceding Σ1​-negation argument, so both canonical sentences are true and their equivalence is true. This last conclusion needs that canonical construction or an equivalent additional restriction; truth of a biconditional alone does not supply it.

Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says that a consistent effectively presented arithmetic theory with an adequate proof predicate cannot prove its formal consistency statement. The background strength must support the diagonal lemma and the derivability conditions, as in the present arithmetic setting.
Löb's theorem says
S⊢□A→A⟹S⊢A.​
(8)
It is a theorem about derivability for every sentence A, not merely an external soundness assertion about the interpretation of □. Taking A=⊥, a proof of consistency gives S⊢□⊥→⊥; Löb then gives S⊢⊥. Thus a consistent S cannot prove consistency. This is how the second incompleteness theorem is the contradiction-sentence instance of Löb's more general result.

Suppose S+A proves every specified reflection instance. Use only the instance with X=¬A. The deduction theorem gives
S⊢A→(□¬A→¬A).
(9)
Classical propositional reasoning turns this into S⊢□¬A→¬A: if ¬A already holds the implication is true, while otherwise A holds and the displayed theorem supplies it. Löb's theorem now yields S⊢¬A, making S+A inconsistent. Therefore no sentence consistent with S can supply all the local reflection instances in a one-sentence extension. The proof uses just one well-chosen instance, and never applies necessitation to a derivation with the extra assumption still open.

GL has all classical propositional tautologies, the normal distribution axiom □(A→B)→(□A→□B) and the Löb axiom □(□A→A)→□A, closed under uniform substitution, modus ponens and necessitation of theorems. We use its derived positive-introspection theorem □A→□□A, normal boxed reasoning, and the boxed replacement principle supplied in the question.
For completeness, positive introspection follows from these axioms. Put C=A∧□A. Distribution gives □C→□A, so propositional logic proves A→(□C→C). Necessitate and distribute to get □A→□(□C→C); Löb's axiom gives □A→□C, and boxing C→□A finishes □A→□□A.
For a GL fixed point by truth substitution, set ⊤=(⊥→⊥), U=□Y(⊤) and V=□Y(U). From the tautology U→(⊤↔U), boxed reasoning gives □U→□(⊤↔U). The supplied replacement principle and distribution then give
GL⊢□U→(U↔V).
(10)
Since U is itself boxed, positive introspection gives U→□U, and hence U→V.
For the reverse direction, the displayed implication entails the theorem V→(□U→U). Necessitate it and distribute to obtain □V→□(□U→U). Since V is boxed, V→□V holds. The Löb axiom therefore yields V→□U, and the same displayed implication then yields V→U. Consequently
GL⊢U↔□Y(U),U=□Y(⊤).​
(11)
All occurrences of the unknown letter have been removed from U by substitution of truth; any other parameter letters remain available. The proof does not assume the invalid reflection principle □U→U as an unconditional axiom.

The fixed point of boxed negated provability gives, in fact, that GL proves F↔□⊥. Here is a direct derivation. Since F is provably equivalent to a boxed formula, positive introspection and necessitation of that equivalence give F→□F. Another introspection step gives F→□□F. The stipulated fixed point also gives F→□¬□F. Combining these two boxed contradictories yields F→□⊥. Conversely □⊥→□¬□F follows by normal boxed explosion, and the fixed point then yields □⊥→F.
Arithmetical soundness of GL, proved by translating distribution into the second derivability condition, necessitation into the first, and Löb's axiom into its internal arithmetic theorem, now gives
PA⊢F∗↔PrPA​(┌⊥┐).
(12)
Any remaining propositional parameters may be assigned arbitrary arithmetic sentences; this equivalence is uniform in those assignments. If PA is one-consistent, it is consistent and Σ1​ sound. Its standard provability predicate for a contradiction is then a false Σ1​ sentence and cannot be a theorem. Hence F∗ is not provable in PA. Moreover its negation is equivalent to PA's consistency statement and is unprovable by the second incompleteness theorem. Thus this fixed point is, under the stated hypothesis, undecidable rather than an unboxed reflection principle.

An L-embedding π:A→B is an injective map preserving the interpretations of all constants and functions and preserving and reflecting every relation. Explicitly π(cA)=cB, π(fA(aˉ))=fB(πaˉ), and A⊨R(aˉ) if and only if B⊨R(πaˉ). Injectivity handles logical equality. Equivalently it is an isomorphism onto an L-substructure of B, or a map preserving the truth of all quantifier-free formulas. Preserving relations in only one direction would instead give a weaker homomorphism notion, not an embedding.

The relation A⊆B means that the domain of A is a subset of the domain of B and the inclusion map is an L-embedding. Thus it contains all named constants, is closed under every named function, and uses the restrictions of the ambient relations.
The stronger relation A≼B means that for every first-order formula ϕ(xˉ) and tuple aˉ from A,
A⊨ϕ(aˉ)⟺B⊨ϕ(aˉ).​

If a model B of T contains A as a substructure, every existential sentence true in A remains true in B: use the same witness tuple and preservation of quantifier-free truth. Hence B satisfies the required joint theory.
For the existential-theory embedding criterion, conversely suppose that joint theory has a model. Expand the language by a constant ca​ for each a∈A, and add the atomic and negated atomic diagram D(A) to T. Any finite collection of its literals is a quantifier-free conjunction θ(cˉ) true of the corresponding tuple in A. Replacing those named constants by variables and existentially closing gives a sentence in Th∃​(A). A model of the assumed joint theory can supply its witnesses, and hence an expansion satisfying those finitely many diagram requirements and any finite subset of T.
Compactness therefore gives a model of T+D(A). Its named Elements define an embedding of A into its L-reduct: inequalities ensure injectivity, and all function and relation literals ensure preservation and reflection. Replace the ambient model by an isomorphic copy identifying this image with A itself. Thus a model of T containing A exists. The diagram step explains how parameter-free existential information nevertheless yields an embedding of the whole structure.

The natural-number successor chain embeds into the integer successor chain by ordinary inclusion. Thus Z witnesses the existential joint satisfiability in the first assertion. The two chains are not elementarily equivalent: every integer has a predecessor, whereas zero has none in the natural-number chain.
For the universal assertion, construct an elementary extension of the natural-number chain containing a two-sided successor chain. Existential sentences are preserved under extensions: witnesses remain present and quantifier-free truth is unchanged by an embedding. Therefore any existential axiomatization implies the specified extension-closure property.

A theory is κ-categorical if it has a model of cardinality κ and any two models of that cardinality are isomorphic. The existence clause prevents vacuous categoricity at cardinalities where there is no model.
To show that infinite-cardinal categoricity forces completeness, suppose T were incomplete. Then some sentence σ has both T+σ and T+¬σ satisfiable, by completeness of first-order logic. Their models must be infinite because T has no finite models. For an infinite κ≥∣L∣, the upward and downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorems produce a model of each extension of cardinality exactly κ: pass to an elementary submodel of size at most max(∣L∣,ℵ0​) and then to the desired size, or reduce a larger model to that size. Categoricity makes these two models isomorphic, but one satisfies σ and the other its negation. Contradiction. Therefore T is complete. If language size is defined without automatically including ℵ0​, the infinite-cardinal qualification is explicit; the assumed existence of a κ-sized model and absence of finite models already force it here.

Equivalently each such type is isolated. The countable-language and completeness hypotheses are essential parts of this formulation.

For quantifier elimination for infinite underlying vector spaces, we first justify that formulas cannot make finer distinctions than their linear-equation data. In any infinite underlying vector space, eliminate an existential variable from a disjunction of conjunctions of linear equations and inequations. If an equation has a nonzero coefficient of that variable, solve for it and substitute. If none does, the inequations forbid only finitely many values, leaving an available value in the infinite domain; the remaining equations concern only the other variables. Terms are linear combinations, so induction gives quantifier elimination for T and completeness, since closed terms reduce to zero.
For one variable there are two possible complete types, zero and nonzero. Both are isolated, and every definable set is their union. Thus
F1​/E1​(T)≅P({1,2}),∣F1​/E1​(T)∣=4.​

Every vector space has a basis and is classified up to linear isomorphism by its dimension. Over a finite field, a vector is a finite linear combination of basis Elements. If the basis has infinite cardinality λ, the set of finite such combinations has cardinality λ: there are at least λ basis vectors and at most a countable union of finite powers of λ, each of size λ. Thus the underlying cardinality of an infinite space equals its dimension.
Any two models of cardinality κ≥ℵ0​ therefore have bases of cardinality κ. A bijection between their bases extends uniquely to an L-isomorphism. A space with that basis size exists, giving the required existence clause as well. Hence T is categorical in every infinite cardinal.

The cardinality of a vector space is central: here “infinite” must continue to refer to the underlying set. Over a countably infinite field, every nonzero finite-dimensional space is already infinite. Thus the model class now consists of all nonzero K-vector spaces, not only infinite-dimensional ones.
Since independence of a pair is not definable over an infinite field, for (i), independence of a single vector is still x=0, but independence of tuples of length at least two is not definable by one formula in this one-sorted language. Quantifier elimination follows from the linear-equation argument in the preceding part for all these infinite domains. Any candidate two-variable formula therefore involves only finitely many linear equations. Take an independent pair, and compare it with (x,λa​x), where x=0 and a∈K avoids the finitely many ratios making one of those nontrivial equations vanish. Both pairs satisfy the same listed atomic formulas, contradicting a proposed definition of independence. Longer tuple independence would define pair independence by existentially completing a pair inside an infinite-dimensional model, giving the same obstruction.

For (v), the one-variable algebra still has two atoms and four Elements. In two variables the isolated atoms are (0,0) and the countably many rank-one directions, represented by x=0,y=λa​x and by x=0,y=0. There is also a nonisolated complete type of an independent pair. A formula using finitely many linear equations either picks finitely many isolated atoms, or contains that independent type and all but finitely many isolated atoms. Therefore F2​/E2​(T) is the finite-cofinite Boolean algebra on a countably infinite set of atoms, with the independent type the extra nonisolated point in its Stone space. A complete n-type over T is a maximal set p(xˉ) of formulas in n variables consistent with T; it decides each such formula or its negation. A partial type drops maximality. A tuple realizes the type if it satisfies every member. A complete type is principal, or isolated, if some formula θ(xˉ) consistent with T implies every member of the type modulo T; then θ itself belongs to that type.
The omitting types theorem says that in a countable first-order language, a consistent theory has a countable model omitting a specified nonprincipal type; more generally a countable family of nonprincipal types can be omitted simultaneously. In the present complete-theory setting it applies directly to complete nonisolated types. “Omitted” means that no tuple in the model realizes all formulas of the type, even though each finite subset is compatible with the theory.

A model is prime if it has an elementary embedding into every model of T. A model is atomic if every finite tuple realizes an isolated complete type over the empty set.
If A is prime, the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem gives a countable model M of T, and an elementary embedding A→M makes A countable. If a tuple of A realized a nonisolated type, the omitting types theorem would give a model of T omitting that type. The prime embedding into it would preserve the tuple's full type and realize the omitted type, a contradiction. Thus a prime model is countable and atomic.
Conversely enumerate a countable atomic model as a0​,a1​,…, and take any B⊨T. Build finite partial elementary maps into B. Suppose the current tuple aˉ maps to bˉ. Choose a formula θ(xˉ,y) isolating the type of (aˉ,an​). The assertion ∃yθ(aˉ,y) holds in A, so its counterpart holds at bˉ in B by partial elementarity. Choose a witness bn​. Since θ isolates the entire extended tuple type, the extended map is elementary on its finite domain; its equality formulas ensure distinct Elements remain distinct. The union is a total elementary embedding A→B. The empty initial tuple works because T is complete. Hence
A is prime⟺A is countable and atomic.​

An ℵ0​-saturated model realizes every complete type in finitely many variables over every finite parameter subset of itself. It suffices equivalently to require one-variable types and then realize finite tuples successively. The definition puts no countability restriction on the size of the model.
For any model M, collect all complete types over its finite parameter sets. Add to its elementary diagram, for each such type, a new tuple of constants satisfying all that type's formulas. Any finite selection of these requirements is realizable in M: each finite type fragment has its existential closure true there, since it is consistent with the complete theory of the corresponding named finite tuple. Distinct new tuples have no additional mutual restrictions. Compactness gives an elementary extension realizing all these types simultaneously.
Start from any model M0​⊨T and apply this construction repeatedly to obtain M0​≼M1​≼⋯, with each successor realizing all types over finite subsets of its predecessor. The elementary-chain theorem makes Mω​=⋃n​Mn​ a model of T with every stage elementary in it. A finite parameter set A⊆Mω​ lies in some Mn​. Every type over A consistent with Mω​ is consistent with Mn​, since the expanded complete theories at those parameters agree by elementarity. It is realized in Mn+1​, hence in the union. Therefore an ℵ0​-saturated model always exists, possibly with uncountable cardinality.
