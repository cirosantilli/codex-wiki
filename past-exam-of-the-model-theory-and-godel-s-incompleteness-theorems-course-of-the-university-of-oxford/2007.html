Bounded completeness of an arithmetic theory means that every bounded arithmetic first-order sentence true in the standard natural numbers is provable in S. If S is first-order consistent and proves a false bounded arithmetic sentence B, bounded completeness of an arithmetic theory also proves its true bounded negation, a contradiction. Conversely, if every bounded theorem is true, S cannot be inconsistent, because inconsistency would prove the false bounded arithmetic sentence 0=1. Thus
Con(S)⟺Σ0​-soundness.​
(4)

Conversely, Sigma-1 soundness excludes inconsistency. If S⊢∃xB(x), it supplies a standard witness n with B(n) true. Bounded completeness of an arithmetic theory proves B(nˉ), so first-order consistency forbids a formal proof of ¬B(nˉ). Hence there cannot simultaneously be refutations of all standard instances. Omega-consistency forbids a logical formula B(x) of any complexity for which S⊢∃xB(x) and S⊢¬B(nˉ) for every standard n. The difference from one-consistency is that the matrix is unrestricted. It implies first-order consistency.
For a complete first-order consistent classical first-order theory, theorem membership obeys the ordinary Boolean truth tables. For example, it proves a conjunction exactly when it proves both conjuncts; completeness and first-order consistency also make theoremhood of a negation the complement of theoremhood of the first-order sentence. Omega-consistency additionally gives the witness property
S⊢∃xB(x)⟺S⊢B(nˉ) for some standard n.
(6)
The reverse implication is existential introduction. For the forward implication, if no standard instance were provable, completeness would give a formal proof of each negated instance, violating omega-consistency. This works also after replacing additional free variables by numerals.
Now induct on logical formulas with their remaining free variables replaced by standard numerals. At the atomic level, the equivalence between theoremhood and standard truth follows from bounded completeness of an arithmetic theory and bounded soundness of an arithmetic theory from (b). The Boolean steps use the truth-table facts just proved. The existential step uses the witness property and the induction hypothesis on its matrix; universal quantification follows by negation. Hence
S⊢X⟺N⊨X​
(7)
for every first-order sentence X. Completeness ensures it decides each first-order sentence, and this equivalence ensures every decision is correct.

A canonical first-order structure is generated by its names: every element is the value of a closed term. In the function-free setting permitted here, this means every element is the interpretation of a constant symbol. Equivalently, the natural map from closed terms modulo logical equality in the first-order structure onto its carrier is surjective. When canonical is defined specifically using constant names, the same definition and all the arguments below apply in this function-free setting.
A full set of first-order sentences has the witness property in its own first-order language: whenever it proves an existential sentence ∃xϕ(x), some closed term t satisfies that the set proves ϕ(t). In a deductively closed set, one can equivalently state this using membership of the first-order sentences. In the function-free setting the witness first-order term is a constant symbol. This is the Henkin witness property; fullness does not by itself replace the separate requirements of first-order consistency and completeness.

A prime model of a complete theory T is a first-order model admitting an elementary embedding of first-order structures into every first-order model of T. Let A⊨T be canonical and B⊨T. For each closed term t, define
e(tA)=tB.
(42)

This proves well-definedness. If they are distinct in A, their inequality holds throughout the complete theory as well, proving injectivity.
For every logical formula ϕ(x) and named tuple tA, the substituted first-order sentence ϕ(t) has the same truth value in the two first-order models by completeness of T. Consequently
A⊨ϕ(tA)⟺B⊨ϕ(tB).
(43)

A first-order theory is a set of first-order sentences in a fixed first-order language, often identified with its deductive closure. It is κ-categorical if it has a first-order model of cardinality κ and all first-order models of that cardinality are isomorphic as first-order structures in that same first-order language. In particular, an isomorphism must preserve the named constants as well as the order.
A first-order theory is complete if it is first-order consistent and decides every first-order sentence: for each σ it entails either σ or ¬σ. Equivalently all its first-order models satisfy exactly the same first-order sentences. First-order consistency is included to exclude the vacuous first-order theory with no first-order models. The distinctions matter here: naming infinitely many ordered points changes countable categoricity without destroying completeness.

In a countable first-order model, the finitely many named constants split the order into the open intervals
(−∞,c1​), (c1​,c2​),…,(cn−1​,cn​), (cn​,∞),
(45)
together with the n named points. Each interval is a countable dense linear order without endpoints in its induced order. It is nonempty and infinite by density and the absence of endpoints.
Use Cantor's classification of countable dense linear orders without endpoints: each such interval is isomorphic to the rational order. It follows by a back-and-forth method extending finite increasing partial bijections alternately along enumerations of the two orders. Given two first-order models of DLOn​, choose isomorphisms between their corresponding intervals and send each named point to its counterpart. Their union is an increasing bijection preserving all constants, hence an isomorphism in Ln​.
There are countable first-order models, for example any finite increasing list in the rational order. Therefore
DLOn​ is ℵ0​-categorical.​
(46)
For n=0, the same argument is just the classification theorem for the whole order.

Use the completeness of the first-order theory of dense linear orders without endpoints and its quantifier elimination. After naming finitely many increasing constants, quantifier elimination still holds in the expanded first-order language: treat these constants as fixed parameters when eliminating quantifiers. Every quantifier-free sentence is a Boolean combination of logical equalities and order comparisons of the named constants, all decided by their stated strict increasing order. Hence DLOn​ is complete.
An alternative formal proof of this finite case uses the stated general theorem that a first-order theory in a countable first-order language with only infinite first-order models and a unique countable first-order model is complete: if first-order models disagreed on a first-order sentence, downward Löwenheim-Skolem would give disagreeing countable first-order models. Part (iii) supplies the uniqueness.
Every first-order sentence of Lω​ mentions only finitely many constant symbols, hence belongs to some Ln​. Its truth is decided by DLOn​, and every first-order model of DLOω​ has a reduct satisfying that finite first-order theory. Thus the larger first-order theory also decides every first-order sentence. The rational-order examples in (ii) establish first-order consistency. Consequently
DLOn​ and DLOω​ are complete.​
(47)
There is no contradiction with (ii): completeness is agreement on individual finite first-order sentences, while categoricity requires a global isomorphism respecting all constants simultaneously.

An n-type over a first-order theory T is a set of logical formulas in the displayed n free variables first-order consistent with T. A complete type chooses exactly one of ϕ and ¬ϕ for each such logical formula. Equivalently it is a maximal consistent such set. For parameter-free partial types the logical formulas use the original first-order language; partial types over parameters use additional names for those parameters.
A partial type p(x) is principal if some logical formula θ(x) first-order consistent with T implies every member of p modulo T:
T⊨∀x(θ(x)→ϕ(x))(ϕ∈p).
(48)
Such a θ is a principal formula of a type of the partial type. For a complete type it isolates that partial type, so principal complete types are exactly isolated types.
For a tuple a in a first-order structure A, its parameter-free partial type is
tpA(a)={ϕ(x):A⊨ϕ(a)}.​
(49)
It is a complete type over Th(A) because the first-order structure decides the truth of every logical formula at that tuple.

Let a∈An, with A finite, and put T=Th(A). There are only finitely many competing tuples b∈An. For each whose complete type differs from that of a, choose a logical formula ϕb​ true at a and false at b. Such a logical formula exists by the inequality of their complete types, negating a distinguishing logical formula if necessary.
Let θ be the conjunction of this finite list, or logical truth if the list is empty. It is true at a, and every tuple satisfying it has the same partial type as a. For each ψ∈tp(a), the first-order sentence
∀x(θ(x)→ψ(x))
(50)
is therefore true in A and belongs to T. The logical formula θ is first-order consistent with T because it is realized at a. It is consequently a principal formula of a type of the partial type, proving every partial type realized in a finite first-order structure is principal. Finiteness of the first-order structure, rather than finiteness of the first-order language, is what makes the conjunction finite.

Every automorphism preserves all logical formulas by induction on their construction, so an automorphism taking a to b necessarily gives equal partial types.
For the converse, first note that a finite first-order structure is atomic by (ii). Thus in both cases every realized finite-tuple partial type has an isolating logical formula. Suppose two finite tuples u,v have the same complete type, and choose a new element x to extend the first tuple. Let θ(z,w) isolate the realized partial type of (u,x). Since
A⊨∃wθ(u,w),
(51)
logical equality of partial types gives A⊨∃wθ(v,w). Choose a witness y. Isolation ensures (u,x) and (v,y) have the same complete type. In particular, if x is distinct from the entries already used, their inequality logical formulas ensure y is distinct from its corresponding entries. The resulting finite map is injective and partial elementary.
Start from a↦b, which is well-defined even for repeated coordinates because their logical equality patterns agree. Enumerate the carrier and extend alternately to include the next missing element in the domain and the next missing element in the range. For a range step apply the same extension argument to the inverse finite map. The union is an elementary bijection from A onto itself: every finite tuple and every logical formula involving it is handled at some stage. It is therefore an automorphism taking a to b. In the finite case the construction terminates once all elements have been included.
Hence
tp(a)=tp(b)⟺∃π∈Aut(A), π(a)=b.​
(52)
Countability makes the back-and-forth numeralwise enumeration in arithmetic exhaust the entire first-order structure; it cannot simply be dropped.

Use the first-order language with one equivalence relation E. Let A have exactly two equivalence classes, one countably infinite and one uncountable, with no further first-order structure. Its complete theory says that there are exactly two classes and both are infinite.
This first-order theory has quantifier elimination. To eliminate one existential logical variable from a conjunction of atomic formulas and negated atomic formulas, a logical equality either fixes its value, or finitely many inequalities exclude finitely many points. Its required equivalence-class membership is determined by the finitely many existing classes represented among the parameters. A required unused class exists exactly when fewer than two classes have already been used. Once its class is possible, infinitude supplies a point avoiding the finite exclusions. These tests are quantifier-free logical equality and equivalence conditions on the parameters. Disjunctive normal form and induction eliminate all quantifiers and also show completeness.
Thus a finite tuple's complete type is isolated by its finite equality-and-equivalence pattern. Every realized finite-tuple partial type is principal, so A is atomic. In particular all individual elements have the same complete one-type, isolated by x=x.
Choose a in the countable class and b in the uncountable class. Any automorphism taking a to b would give a bijection from their whole equivalence classes, impossible because their cardinalities differ. Therefore
A is atomic but not homogeneous.​
(53)
Its uncountability is precisely what avoids the conclusion of (iii).

fA(t1A​,…,trA​)=(f(t1​,…,tr​))A,

B⊆A,B⊨T.​

Adjoin to T the negations of all closed-term instances of θ. If the resulting set had a first-order model A, its ground-term substructure B from (ii) would still first-order model T. The assumed existential consequence therefore supplies a tuple b in B satisfying θ. Each bj​ is the value of a closed term tj​.
Quantifier-free agreement between a substructure and its ambient first-order structure gives
A⊨θ(t1​,…,tk​),
(57)

T∪{¬θ(t1​,…,tk​):t1​,…,tk​ closed terms}

T⊨i=1⋁m​θ(ti1​,…,tik​).​
