Ginzburg-Landau theory of superconductivity organizes a continuous superconducting transition through a slowly varying complex order parameter. In the simplest isotropic single-component SI model,
F−Fn​=∫d3x[α∣ψ∣2+2β​∣ψ∣4+2m∗​∣(−iℏ∇−qA)ψ∣2​+2μ0​∣B∣2​],B=∇×A.
(16)
Here β>0, m∗​>0, α changes sign at Tc​, and q=−2e fixes a charge/phase convention. The phenomenological normalization of ψ determines the coefficient values; it should not be confused without qualification with a literal density of independently identifiable pairs.
Microscopic derivation of Ginzburg-Landau theory expands the BCS response near Tc​ in small gap amplitude and slow spatial gradients. It identifies the complex order parameter with a scaled pair amplitude, supplies the coefficients from the electronic microscopic parameters, and explains the pair charge in the gauge coupling. The agreement is a controlled near-transition limit, not a statement that the truncated local functional remains exact at all temperatures. Lev Gor'kov established this connection explicitly in 1959.[ref]
Below the transition, minimization gives ∣ψ0​∣2=−α/β and condensation-energy density α2/(2β). A variation with respect to ψ∗ gives
2m∗​(−iℏ∇−qA)2​ψ+αψ+β∣ψ∣2ψ=0.
(17)
Variation with respect to A gives the supercurrent
j=m∗​qℏ​Im(ψ∗∇ψ)−m∗​q2​∣ψ∣2A,
(18)
coupled to the magnetostatic Ampère law. For nearly uniform amplitude this reduces to London screening and the Meissner effect. The theory supplies two different length scales:
ξ2=2m∗​∣α∣ℏ2​,λ2=μ0​q2∣ψ0​∣2m∗​​,κ=ξλ​.
(19)
The amplitude can vary over the superconducting coherence length, whereas the magnetic field is screened over the London penetration depth.
The surface-energy distinction at κ=1/2​ explains why type-I and type-II behavior are possible within the same framework. In the type-II regime, quantized winding produces an Abrikosov vortex with a finite core and circulating current. Periodic solutions yield an Abrikosov vortex lattice. Linearization about the normal state gives Bc2​=Φ0​/(2πξ2), while core and field energetics determine the lower entry field. These predictions connect a spatial field theory to measurable vortex patterns and critical fields, beyond a spatially uniform BCS gap equation. Thin-film critical-field observations helped motivate the large-κ regime; magnetic-decoration experiments subsequently made vortex positions visible.[ref]
These phenomena also enable applications. Type-II conductors support high-field magnets, but flux pinning is needed to limit vortex-motion losses. Phase coherence and fluxoid quantization permit persistent-current loops and interference devices. A weak link supplies Josephson coupling energy, allowing SQUID magnetometry and nonlinear quantum circuits; a junction's detailed current–phase law requires a weak-link model, rather than following automatically from the spatially uniform bulk functional. Microstructure can raise a useful critical current without greatly changing the equilibrium upper field, illustrating the difference between thermodynamic stability and transport performance.
The simple functional has identifiable limits. Far below Tc​, the small-gap expansion is uncontrolled; rapidly varying fields can require nonlocal microscopic electrodynamics. Very near a transition in a fluctuating low-dimensional or strongly anisotropic material, mean-field minimization can miss important thermal or quantum fluctuations. Unconventional or multiband pairing can require several order-parameter components and additional gradient couplings. Strong fields can introduce spin-polarization pair breaking omitted by the orbital model. Finally, the static functional predicts equilibrium extrema, not quasiparticle spectra, dissipation, vortex velocities or a unique amplitude-mode frequency without further dynamical input. These are reasons to choose an appropriate extension or a microscopic calculation, not reasons to discard the successful near-transition predictions.
