An Abrikosov vortex combines a core of suppressed superconducting order parameter, circulating electric current and a 2π winding of the quantum phase. In the bulk limit a singly wound vortex carries the superconducting flux quantum Φ0​=h/(2e). More precisely it is the fluxoid that is quantized; appreciable current on the integration contour prevents identifying enclosed flux alone with the integer.
For a type-II superconductor, the Ginzburg-Landau parameter is κ=λ/ξ>1/2​. The superconducting mixed state allows vortices between the lower critical magnetic field and upper critical magnetic field. A short superconducting coherence length permits a high orbital upper field,
Bc2​=2πξ2Φ0​​.​
(1)
Thus surviving a large applied field generally requires accommodating many vortices, not excluding flux everywhere. The Abrikosov vortex lattice provides their equilibrium arrangement in the simplest isotropic model. Material anisotropy, disorder and thermal fluctuations can change that arrangement and its stability. Spin-polarization limits can also constrain an actual upper field, beyond this orbital estimate.
Carrying a large transport current density is a separate problem. For a straight vortex with tangent t, the driving Lorentz force per unit length is fL​=J×Φ0​t. Vortex motion produces a mean electric field E=B×vL​ and hence dissipative power density J⋅E. A large Bc2​ alone therefore does not imply a large usable superconducting critical current.
Flux pinning holds vortices at favorable defects: suppressing the order parameter costs less where superconductivity is already weakened. Precipitates, dislocations, grain boundaries and deliberately introduced damage can provide pinning, subject to material-specific trade-offs. A defect scale comparable with a core size can be effective; a defect that destroys connected superconducting paths is harmful. In a coarse force balance, a maximum pinning force density Fp​ gives Jc​B≃Fp​ for transverse transport. Vortex depinning and thermally activated flux creep can produce a voltage below an ideal static threshold.
High-field magnet conductors consequently need both a large upper field and strong, persistent pinning while retaining mechanical integrity and electrical connectivity. Niobium-titanium and niobium-tin conductors illustrate why superconducting composition and microstructural processing are developed together. A critical-current measurement should specify field, temperature and voltage criterion; it is not simply a measurement of the temperature at which resistance first vanishes.
