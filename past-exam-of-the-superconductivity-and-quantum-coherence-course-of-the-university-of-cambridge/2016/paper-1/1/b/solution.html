The superconducting gap function is a momentum-dependent pairing amplitude Δ(k), including a phase as well as a magnitude. A fully gapped isotropic state and a state with symmetry-required nodes can have different low-temperature responses. Heat capacity, thermal transport, London penetration depth and tunnelling spectra probe the low-energy quasiparticle spectrum. Activated behavior suggests a nonzero minimum gap; power laws can indicate nodes. For example, clean line nodes give a penetration-depth correction linear in temperature, whereas disorder can change the apparent power.
Such magnitude-sensitive probes require care: impurities, several bands, anisotropic full gaps and surface states can imitate or modify simple expectations. A node does not by itself establish a particular sign pattern, and the absence of a coherence peak is not a complete symmetry determination. The strongest identification combines momentum-sensitive spectra with phase-sensitive superconducting gap experiments.
For an illustrative dx2−y2​ state, Δ(φ)∝cos2φ changes sign under a 90∘ rotation. A Josephson junction to a conventional s-wave reference can turn that sign into an intrinsic phase shift. In a corner DC SQUID with junctions on orthogonal crystal faces, one branch can acquire an extra π relative to the other. For equal junctions and negligible loop inductance,
Ic​(Φ)=2I0​​cos(Φ0​πΦ​+2δint​​)​.
(2)
Changing δint​ from zero to π shifts the interference pattern by half a flux period. The 1993 YBCO–Pb experiment compared phase-coherent junction geometries and found evidence for that sign reversal, while explicitly discussing SQUID asymmetry and trapped vortices. Those controls matter: an arbitrary shifted trace is not by itself a proof of pairing symmetry.[ref]
A tricrystal half-flux experiment arranges grain-boundary junctions so that a sign-changing order parameter produces an odd total π shift around a ring. Fluxoid consistency then favors a half-integer flux state, with the detailed spontaneous flux depending on loop inductance and junction strengths. Choosing comparison geometries without that frustration and controlling trapped external flux separates the pairing-phase effect from ordinary flux trapping.[ref]
The conclusion is strongest when the same candidate symmetry explains nodes, crystal-axis dependence and these phase-sensitive interference results. The phase-sensitive observation distinguishes a sign-changing gap from a merely anisotropic positive gap; no single temperature power law supplies that distinction.
