A unital Banach star-algebra is a complex unital Banach algebra with a continuous conjugate-linear algebra involution satisfying
(ab)∗=b∗a∗,(a∗)∗=a,1∗=1.
(1)
The algebra norm is complete and submultiplicative, with the unit conventionally normalized to norm one. Many definitions additionally require the involution to be isometric, ∥a∗∥=∥a∥; the function algebra here satisfies this stronger convention as well. A Banach star-algebra does not by definition require the extra C-star identity ∥a∗a∥=∥a∥2.
Here is the lattice proof of the Stone-Weierstrass theorem in its complex self-adjoint form. Let B be the uniform closure of the given unital star-subalgebra in C(Ω), and let BR​ be its real-valued part. Closure preserves multiplication and complex conjugation, so B is still a unital star-algebra. Real and imaginary parts lie in BR​, and separation by the complex algebra implies separation by its real-valued part.
For h∈BR​, polynomial approximation of absolute value on a bounded interval gives ∣h∣∈BR​. For example, this polynomial approximation follows from the binomial series for the square root, whose coefficients give uniform convergence on the relevant closed interval. Thus
max(h,k)=21​(h+k+∣h−k∣),min(h,k)=21​(h+k−∣h−k∣)
(2)
also lie in BR​. It is therefore a lattice of functions.
To see how the lattice gives density, fix a real continuous target f and a tolerance ϵ>0. For any two distinct points x,y, separation and constants provide hxy​∈BR​ with hxy​(x)=f(x) and hxy​(y)=f(y), by an affine rescaling of a separating function. For x=y, use the constant f(x).
Fix x. The sets where hxy​>f−ϵ cover Ω as y varies. A finite subcover and a pointwise maximum give gx​∈BR​ with gx​>f−ϵ everywhere and gx​(x)=f(x). The neighborhoods where gx​<f+ϵ now cover Ω as x varies. A second finite subcover and a pointwise minimum give h∈BR​ satisfying
f−ϵ<h<f+ϵeverywhere on Ω.
(3)
This is lattice approximation from two-point approximation. Using half the desired tolerance gives strict uniform error below the desired bound. Since BR​ is closed, it contains every real continuous function. Splitting a complex function into real and imaginary parts proves B=C(Ω)​.
The annulus test. For the closed annulus, the family holomorphic in the interior is not dense in the full continuous-function algebra. A uniform limit on the annulus is locally uniform in its interior, so locally uniform convergence of holomorphic functions makes that limit holomorphic there. Thus the proposed family is closed in C(Ω). It is proper because z↦z is continuous but not holomorphic on the annulus interior.
There is also an explicit continuous functional detecting the obstruction. Choose a small closed disk of radius r contained in the annulus interior and let L(h) be the integral of h(z)dz around its positively oriented boundary. The Cauchy integral theorem gives L(h)=0 for every member of the holomorphic family, while parametrizing the circle gives L(z)=2πir2=0. Since ∣L(h)∣≤2πr∥h∥∞​, no uniform approximation of z by that family is possible.
