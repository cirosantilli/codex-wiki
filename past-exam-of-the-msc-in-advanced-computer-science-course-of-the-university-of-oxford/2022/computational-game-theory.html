Let X denote the monetary reward. Its expected value and expected utility are different quantities:
EX=10089​+1010​=1.89​,E[u(X)]=10089​+10100​=1001089​=10.89.
(1)
Thus indifference to a sure nonnegative reward requires k2=10.89, giving k=3.30 dollars. This is the certainty equivalent, hence the lottery's monetary value in the question's comparison with a sure reward. The utility function u(x)=x2 is increasing and strictly convex on nonnegative monetary amounts. Its certainty equivalent exceeds its expected value, 3.30>1.89, demonstrating risk seeking; the same conclusion follows from the Jensen inequality.
The wording about paying an entry fee needs a qualification. A certainty equivalent compares a gross lottery with a sure gross reward; a literal utility indifference price also accounts for the fee's effect on wealth. If u instead describes final wealth, with initial wealth w and fee c, the comparison is E[(w−c+X)2]=w2, on a domain where the utility function is increasing. Here Var(X)=7.3179, so the nonnegative-wealth branch gives c=w+1.89−w2−7.3179​ whenever that branch is admissible. An initial wealth and an admissible payment domain are not specified. Therefore 3.30 is the determined certainty equivalent, while a literal wealth-dependent maximum fee is not uniquely determined. This is the distinction in certainty equivalent and willingness to pay. If negative rewards were allowed without requiring an increasing utility function, the equation for k also has the algebraic root −3.30; the usual nonnegative-reward interpretation selects the positive root.

Write A and B for the two deterministic outcomes. Under the expected utility hypothesis, indifference between them implies u(A)=u(B)=c. The expected utility of the fair lottery is consequently
U(21​A+21​B)=21​u(A)+21​u(B)=c.
(2)
It must therefore be indifferent to each deterministic outcome, contradicting the asserted strict preference. These preferences do not admit an expected utility representation over the stated outcomes. Equivalently, affine utility on probability measures preserves this indifference under mixing, as required by the independence axiom for lottery preferences.
A concern for the fairness of the procedure can be perfectly intelligible. It would require enlarging the outcome description to include the procedure; it cannot be represented by an expected utility over only the two final gift recipients while retaining the stated strict preference.

Reading the lower-left number in each source cell as player 1's payoff, against an opponent playing A the player's own payoffs from A,B are y,0; against B they are x,x/2. For x>0, identify A with aggressive defection and B with yielding cooperation. The standard payoffs then have values
T=x,R=x/2,S=0,P=y.
(4)
The prisoner's dilemma ordering T>R>P>S holds precisely for x>0 and 0<y<x/2 with this labeling. For example, x=4,y=1 gives T=4,R=2,P=1,S=0. Each player then has the strictly dominant strategy A, although (B,B) gives both a larger payoff than (A,A). Here 2R=T+S as well, so the optional weak aggregate-payoff condition is met with equality.
For the Chicken payoff ordering, the collision outcome must be worse than yielding to an aggressive opponent: T>R>S>P. This holds for x>0 and y<0, for example x=4,y=−1. Against B, the best response is A because x>x/2; against A, it is B because 0>y. Consequently (A,B) and (B,A) are the two pure strategy Nash equilibria, and mutual aggression is the worst outcome for both.
If action names may be exchanged, there is another prisoner's dilemma region: take A as cooperation and B as defection. Then (T,R,P,S)=(0,y,x/2,x), so x<0 and x/2<y<0 also work, for example x=−4,y=−1. This relabeling explains why the first displayed region is not a necessary condition without an action convention. Equality at any of these boundaries destroys the strict ordering.

Represent satisfied and unsatisfied goals by payoffs one and zero. The second player's goal is an exclusive or: it is true precisely when q=r, independently of p. The first goal is false only when all three Boolean variables are false. The resulting payoff matrix is below; each boxed entry marks that player's best response to the opposing pure strategy, including ties:
rrˉ​pq(1​,0)(1​,1​)​pqˉ​(1​,1​)(1​,0)​pˉ​q(1​,0)(1​,1​)​pˉ​qˉ​(1​,1​)(0,0)​​
(5)
For player 1, choosing r satisfies the goal against every opposing pure strategy. Choosing rˉ does equally well except against pˉ​qˉ​, where it does worse. Thus r is player 1's unique dominant strategy in the weak sense, and there is no strictly dominant strategy. The ties are why most first-player entries in both rows are boxed.
For player 2, either pure strategy with q=0 is a best response against r, while either with q=1 is a best response against rˉ. No one choice of q works against both rows, so player 2 has no dominant strategy, and there is no dominant-strategy equilibrium. The value of p does not affect player 2's own payoff.
The cells with both entries boxed give exactly four pure strategy Nash equilibria:
(r,pqˉ​),(r,pˉ​qˉ​),(rˉ,pq),(rˉ,pˉ​q).​
(6)
All four give payoffs (1,1). In particular, the weakly dominated choice rˉ can occur in a Nash equilibrium because player 1 is already satisfied there and cannot strictly improve.

c OurBigBook.com
The succinct input is the players' variable-control sets, their goal propositional formulas, and a complete candidate Boolean valuation; an exponentially expanded payoff matrix is not part of the input. This representation is essential to candidate equilibrium checking in a Boolean game.
For membership in co-NP, consider the complement: the candidate is not a Nash equilibrium. A complexity certificate consists of a player i and an alternative Boolean valuation of that player's controlled Boolean variables. Evaluate the current goal and the goal after this unilateral deviation, keeping every other player's variables fixed. A profitable unilateral deviation exists precisely when the current goal is false and the new goal is true. The certificate has at most the number of input variables plus the player index, and both propositional formula evaluations take polynomial time. Thus the complement belongs to NP, and the original decision problem belongs to co-NP.
For hardness, start with a propositional formula F(X) and introduce a fresh Boolean variable z. Construct a one-player Boolean game in which that player controls X∪{z} and has goal
γ=z∧F(X).
(7)
Choose the candidate pure strategy assigning every variable false. Its payoff is zero because z is false, regardless of F. If F has a satisfying assignment, changing X to that assignment and z to true is a profitable unilateral deviation. If F is an unsatisfiable Boolean formula, no unilateral deviation can satisfy the goal. Therefore
F is unsatisfiable⟺the constructed candidate is a Nash equilibrium.
(8)
The construction increases the formula length by only a constant and writes a candidate of linear length, so it is a polynomial-time many-one reduction. Unsatisfiability is co-NP-complete; combining hardness and membership proves that candidate Nash equilibrium checking for Boolean games is co-NP-complete. If a convention requires at least two players, add a player controlling a fresh unused variable and having the always-true goal; this changes neither direction of the proof.

There is no perfect recall. Player 1's later information set joins histories following both of her initial actions, so she has forgotten whether she chose L or R. It also hides player 2's intervening action, but failure to observe another player's action is imperfect information, whereas forgetting one's own earlier action is the particular violation of perfect recall. Player 2's two nodes form a single information set because player 2 does not observe the initial choice.
For a behavioural strategy, let p be player 1's initial probability of L and r her later probability of L. These are separate information sets, so p and r need not be equal; their random draws are independent. Name player 2's unlabeled branches left and right, and let q be the probability of taking the left branch. The only unit-payoff paths are left–left–left and right–right–right. Consequently
Pr(u1​=1)=qpr+(1−q)(1−p)(1−r).
(9)
Because this is a zero-sum game, the natural meaning of the claimed best behavioural strategy is its maximum guaranteed expected payoff. Player 2 can choose either branch, so the guarantee at (p,r) is
g(p,r)=min{pr,(1−p)(1−r)}.
(10)
For nonnegative numbers, their minimum is at most their geometric mean. Also p(1−p)≤1/4 and r(1−r)≤1/4, hence
g(p,r)≤p(1−p)r(1−r)​≤41​.
(11)
Taking p=r=1/2 makes both possible branch-conditioned success probabilities 1/4, so this upper bound is attained against every q. Thus the optimal guaranteed behavioural strategy success probability is 1/4.
A mixed strategy can instead select the complete plan LL with probability 1/2 and the complete plan RR with probability 1/2. The two choices within a sampled plan are correlated. If player 2 chooses left, exactly the sampled plan LL succeeds; if player 2 chooses right, exactly RR succeeds. Therefore the success probability is
21​q+21​(1−q)=21​​
(12)
against every opposing mixed strategy. It is optimal among mixed strategies too: player 2's uniform mixture gives success probability 1/2 to each of LL,RR and zero to LR,RL, so no mixture of the four plans can exceed 1/2 against that opponent. This establishes mixed and behavioural security levels without perfect recall directly.
The guarantee qualification matters: if player 2's branch were known to be left, the deterministic plan LL would succeed with probability one. The number 1/4 is not an upper bound against every fixed opposing choice separately.

Against Tit for tat, let at​ be an arbitrary deviator's action at round t, and set a−1​=C to encode the opponent's initial cooperation. The opponent then plays at−1​. Define h(C)=0 and h(D)=3. The four possible transitions give
at−1​CCDD​at​CDCD​u(at​,at−1​)3601​3+h(at​)−h(at−1​)3603​​
(13)
Thus every stage payoff is at most 3+h(at​)−h(at−1​). The telescoping sum yields the uniform payoff bound against Tit for tat:
∑t=0T−1​u(at​,at−1​)≤3T+h(aT−1​)−h(C)≤3T+3.
(14)
After division by T, no long-run average payoff can exceed three. The bound applies to every action sequence, so in particular it covers every finite-state machine representing a unilateral deviation. Mutual Tit for tat produces C,C in every round and gives each player three. Neither player can improve, proving (TFT,TFT) is a Nash equilibrium.
Another pair is (TFT,grim trigger). Here grim trigger initially cooperates and continues cooperating until the opponent first defects, after which it defects forever. Both strategies are two-state finite-state machines: Tit for tat records the opponent's previous action, while grim trigger records whether defection has ever occurred. Their prescribed play again consists entirely of cooperation and gives (3,3).
The player using grim trigger faces Tit for tat, so every unilateral deviation is bounded by the same telescoping sum and cannot improve on three. The player using Tit for tat faces grim trigger. If that player never defects, the payoff remains three. If the first defection occurs at a finite round τ, it gives a one-time payoff six; thereafter the opponent always defects, against which each subsequent payoff is at most one. Any finite initial prefix has zero contribution to the long-run average payoff, so such a unilateral deviation has limiting upper average at most one. It cannot improve on three either. Both players are therefore best responses, proving the second claimed Nash equilibrium.
For deterministic finite-state machines, the joint state path is eventually periodic, so the displayed averages have a limit. The first bound also controls the limiting upper average for arbitrary action sequences, making the no-improvement argument stronger than an enumeration of finite-state deviations. Notice that equality in the bound permits alternating exploitation to average three: equilibrium requires absence of a strict improvement, not uniqueness of a best response.

Write x(S)=∑i∈S​xi​. The core of a cooperative game is
C(v)={x∈Rn:x(N)=v(N),x(S)≥v(S) for every S⊆N}.
(15)
Take any x,y∈C(v) and α∈[0,1], and form the convex combination z=αx+(1−α)y. Its total payoff is z(N)=αv(N)+(1−α)v(N)=v(N). For every coalition S,
z(S)=αx(S)+(1−α)y(S)≥αv(S)+(1−α)v(S)=v(S).
(16)
Hence z remains in the core. The core is a convex set, even when the transferable utility game is not a convex cooperative game. An empty core is a convex set vacuously; the argument does not claim nonemptiness. Geometrically, the same conclusion follows because the constraints intersect an affine hyperplane with closed half-spaces.

Assume the standard empty-coalition normalization v(∅)=0. A convex cooperative game has increasing marginal contributions: whenever A⊆B and i∈/B,
v(A∪{i})−v(A)≤v(B∪{i})−v(B).
(17)
This follows from the supermodular inequality applied to A∪{i} and B. Fix a permutation π of all players, let Pi​ be the players preceding i, and form its marginal contribution vector
miπ​=v(Pi​∪{i})−v(Pi​).
(18)
Summing along the full permutation is a telescoping sum, so ∑i∈N​miπ​=v(N). For an arbitrary coalition S, list its members in their induced order i1​,…,ik​, and put Sj​={i1​,…,ij​}. Since Sj−1​⊆Pij​​, increasing marginal contributions give
∑i∈S​miπ​≥∑j=1k​(v(Sj​)−v(Sj−1​))=v(S).
(19)
Thus every marginal contribution vector belongs to the core. The vector of Shapley values is exactly their uniform average over the n! permutations, hence a convex combination. By the preceding part, that average is in the core. This proves that the Shapley value allocation belongs to the core of a convex cooperative game, including every coalition constraint. It is the result Shapley value belongs to the core of a convex game.
