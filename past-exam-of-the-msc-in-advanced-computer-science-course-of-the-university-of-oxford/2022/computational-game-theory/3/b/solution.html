After division by T, no long-run average payoff can exceed three. The bound applies to every action sequence, so in particular it covers every finite-state machine representing a unilateral deviation. Mutual Tit for tat produces C,C in every round and gives each player three. Neither player can improve, proving (TFT,TFT) is a Nash equilibrium.
Another pair is (TFT,grim trigger). Here grim trigger initially cooperates and continues cooperating until the opponent first defects, after which it defects forever. Both strategies are two-state finite-state machines: Tit for tat records the opponent's previous action, while grim trigger records whether defection has ever occurred. Their prescribed play again consists entirely of cooperation and gives (3,3).
The player using grim trigger faces Tit for tat, so every unilateral deviation is bounded by the same telescoping sum and cannot improve on three. The player using Tit for tat faces grim trigger. If that player never defects, the payoff remains three. If the first defection occurs at a finite round τ, it gives a one-time payoff six; thereafter the opponent always defects, against which each subsequent payoff is at most one. Any finite initial prefix has zero contribution to the long-run average payoff, so such a unilateral deviation has limiting upper average at most one. It cannot improve on three either. Both players are therefore best responses, proving the second claimed Nash equilibrium.
For deterministic finite-state machines, the joint state path is eventually periodic, so the displayed averages have a limit. The first bound also controls the limiting upper average for arbitrary action sequences, making the no-improvement argument stronger than an enumeration of finite-state deviations. Notice that equality in the bound permits alternating exploitation to average three: equilibrium requires absence of a strict improvement, not uniqueness of a best response.
