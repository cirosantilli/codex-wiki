Represent satisfied and unsatisfied goals by payoffs one and zero. The second player's goal is an exclusive or: it is true precisely when q=r, independently of p. The first goal is false only when all three Boolean variables are false. The resulting payoff matrix is below; each boxed entry marks that player's best response to the opposing pure strategy, including ties:
rrˉ​pq(1​,0)(1​,1​)​pqˉ​(1​,1​)(1​,0)​pˉ​q(1​,0)(1​,1​)​pˉ​qˉ​(1​,1​)(0,0)​​
(5)
For player 1, choosing r satisfies the goal against every opposing pure strategy. Choosing rˉ does equally well except against pˉ​qˉ​, where it does worse. Thus r is player 1's unique dominant strategy in the weak sense, and there is no strictly dominant strategy. The ties are why most first-player entries in both rows are boxed.
For player 2, either pure strategy with q=0 is a best response against r, while either with q=1 is a best response against rˉ. No one choice of q works against both rows, so player 2 has no dominant strategy, and there is no dominant-strategy equilibrium. The value of p does not affect player 2's own payoff.
The cells with both entries boxed give exactly four pure strategy Nash equilibria:
(r,pqˉ​),(r,pˉ​qˉ​),(rˉ,pq),(rˉ,pˉ​q).​
(6)
All four give payoffs (1,1). In particular, the weakly dominated choice rˉ can occur in a Nash equilibrium because player 1 is already satisfied there and cannot strictly improve.
