The method of characteristics replaces the first-order partial differential equation by the ordinary differential equations
x˙=x,y˙​=2y+βx,u˙=x+2.
(1)
An integrating factor for the second equation gives d(e−2sy)/ds=βCe−s, so their general solution of a differential equation is
x=Ces,y=De2s−βCes,u=Ces+2s+E.​
(2)
Here C,D,E are real constants. For C=0, eliminating s gives the characteristic projections
y=Kx2−βx,K=D/C2,​
(3)
each restricted to either x>0 or x<0. These are arcs of parabolas, with the straight-line exception K=0. Along an oriented characteristic curve, ∣x∣ increases with s.
For C=0, the characteristic projections lie on x=0: y=De2s describes either vertical half-line when D=0. For C=D=0 the projection is the single point (0,0), although u=2s+E still changes. This last lifted characteristic curve cannot be contained in the function graph of a classical solution: at (0,0) the original partial differential equation would require 0=2.

Parametrize the initial curve by (x0​(r),y0​(r))=(1,r), −1≤r≤1. Its tangent is (0,1), whereas the projected characteristic vector field there is (1,2r+β). The determinant
det(12r+β​01​)=1=0​
(4)
proves that these are Noncharacteristic Cauchy data: the initial curve is everywhere transverse to the characteristic curves.
There is a regularity qualification. The prescribed function g(r)=min{r,0} is continuous and Lipschitz continuous, but is not differentiable at r=0. Thus the data are Cauchy in this geometric sense, with data given by a smooth function on each of r<0 and r>0; the usual theorem giving a global classical solution from continuously differentiable data does not apply across r=0.

Use the initial conditions x(0,r)=1, y(0,r)=r, u(0,r)=g(r) in the characteristic equations for a transport equation. The resulting parametrized solution surface is
x(s,r)y(s,r)u(s,r)​=es,=(r+β)e2s−βes,=min{r,0}+es−1+2s,​s∈R, −1≤r≤1.​
(5)
The Jacobian determinant of (s,r)↦(x,y) is e3s>0. In fact the inverse is explicit:
s=logx,r=q(x,y):=x2y+βx​−β.
(6)
Consequently the explicit solution of a differential equation on the region reached from the data is
u(x,y)=x−1+2logx+min{x2y+βx​−β,0}.​
(7)
To verify it away from q=0, set D=x∂x​+(2y+βx)∂y​. Direct differentiation gives Dq=0 and D(x−1+2logx)=x+2. At x=1 we have q=y, giving precisely the prescribed boundary condition.
Across q=0 the formula remains continuous and locally Lipschitz, is a smooth function on each side, and solves the partial differential equation along every characteristic curve. It is also a weak solution, since its first distributional derivatives agree almost everywhere with its ordinary derivatives. It is not a classical solution throughout the region because of the inherited corner.

Every point with x>0 has exactly one backward characteristic curve meeting x=1, at the point (1,q(x,y)). It meets the specified data precisely when −1≤q≤1. Thus the whole region determined by these data, including the two bounding characteristic curves, is
Dβ​={(x,y):x>0, (β−1)x2−βx≤y≤(β+1)x2−βx}.​
(8)
Its interior is the corresponding open domain for the partial differential equation. Uniqueness here means uniqueness in the class of continuous functions satisfying the characteristic equations for a transport equation, including the piecewise smooth function defining the solution of a differential equation above. The endpoints of the initial segment determine the two boundary values by continuity.
This region is maximal for determination by the given data: on x>0, arbitrary extensions of g outside [−1,1] give different values on the characteristic curves with q∈/[−1,1]. No characteristic curve from x=1 reaches x≤0.
For β=1,
x>0,−x≤y≤2x2−x.​
(9)
The lower boundary is a straight line, the upper boundary is a parabola, and the characteristic carrying the corner is y=x2−x. All three approach the excluded origin as x↓0.

For β=1, q=(y+x−x2)/x2. On the two sides of the corner-carrying characteristic curve, the partial derivative is
uy​(x,y)={x−2,0,​−x<y<x2−x,x2−x<y<2x2−x,​x>0.​
(10)

The transport equation transports this corner along the characteristic curve from (1,0), which is y=x2−x; it provides no smoothing. This is consistent with Noncharacteristic Cauchy data and does not contradict the smooth-data theorem, whose differentiability hypothesis fails here.

In the convention Avxx​+2Bvxy​+Cvyy​=⋯, the coefficients of the principal symbol are
A=x,B=y+2βx​,C=0,B2−AC=4(2y+βx)2​.

The second-order differential operator factors as
xvxx​+(2y+βx)vxy​=(x∂x​+(2y+βx)∂y​)(vx​).​
(16)
Therefore setting u=vx​ turns the second-order partial differential equation into exactly the first-order partial differential equation in part (a). Conversely, given a sufficiently smooth solution of a differential equation u of that equation on a coordinate rectangle, define
v(x,y)=∫x∗​x​u(ξ,y)dξ+F(y).
(17)
The fundamental theorem of calculus gives vx​=u, so this solves the second-order equation, with an arbitrary twice continuously differentiable function F. For the Weierstrass M-test proof of the Picard-Lindelöf theorem, the required bound is
Mh≤k,with no additional restriction on Lh.​
(18)
To see this, use Picard iteration starting with y0​(x)=b and
yn+1​(x)=b+∫ax​f(s,yn​(s))ds.
(19)
If yn​ takes its values in [b−k,b+k], then ∣yn+1​−b∣≤M∣x−a∣≤Mh≤k. Thus every Picard iterate remains in the rectangle where the hypotheses hold. Successive applications of the Lipschitz condition and integration give the factorial estimate for Picard iteration
∣yn+1​(x)−yn​(x)∣≤(n+1)!MLn∣x−a∣n+1​.
(20)
For example, the n=0 estimate is M∣x−a∣, and integrating the nth estimate multiplied by L proves the next one. The sum of the uniform bounds is
∑n=0∞​(n+1)!MLnhn+1​<∞
(21)
for every finite Lh, by the exponential series. Hence the telescoping series of Picard iterates converges uniformly by the Weierstrass M-test. The uniform limit stays in the closed rectangle, and the Lipschitz condition permits passage to the limit in the integral equation. The fundamental theorem of calculus then gives the desired solution of a differential equation on [a−h,a+h].

For the direct contraction mapping theorem proof with the usual supremum norm, take the complete metric space
B={z∈C([a−h,a+h]):∥z−b∥∞​≤k}
(22)
and the Picard iteration map Tz(x)=b+∫ax​f(s,z(s))ds. The estimates are
∥Tz−b∥∞​≤Mh,∥Tz−Tw∥∞​≤Lh∥z−w∥∞​.
(23)
Thus this proof requires
Mh≤kandLh<1.​
(24)
The first inequality makes T preserve B; the strict second inequality makes it a contraction mapping. Its unique fixed point is the desired solution of a differential equation.
This is more restrictive than the preceding Weierstrass M-test argument: a single application of T must shrink every difference in the supremum norm, whereas repeated integration in the other proof produces the factorial (n+1)! and convergence even when Lh≥1. If L=0, the additional condition is automatic. These are conditions for the two stated direct proofs; they are not necessary bounds on the actual existence interval.

The rectangle avoids the singular line y=0 exactly when 0<k<1. There y>0, and the largest ∣x∣ on [1−h,1+h] is 1+h. Since 1+1/y decreases with y, the smallest feasible uniform bound is
M(h,k)=(1+h)1−k2−k​,h>0, 0<k<1.​

∣f(x,y)−f(x,z)∣=yz∣x∣​∣y−z∣≤(1−k)21+h​∣y−z∣.
(29)
The quotients approach this upper bound as x=1+h and y,z↓1−k, so the smallest feasible constant is

A(k)<B(k)⟺k<(1−k)(2−k)⟺k2−4k+2>0.
(32)

(33)

Where y=β, taking the quotient of the two ordinary differential equations gives
dxdy​=y−βx3−x​,2(y−β)2​=4x4​−2x2​+constant.
(47)
Choose the first integral
h(x,y)=2(y−β)2​+2x2​−4x4​.​
(48)
The chain rule checks conservation even at points excluded in taking that quotient:
h˙=(x−x3)(y−β)+(y−β)(x3−x)=0.
(49)
Thus each trajectory is contained in a connected component of a level set of h. At (0,β) the gradient vanishes and the Hessian matrix is the identity, so h has a strict local minimum there. To make the closed-trajectory argument explicit, put z=y−β and 0<E<1/4. The bounded component of h=E is
z=±2E−x2+x4/2​,∣x∣≤rE​:=1−1−4E​​<1.
(50)
The two arcs join at (±rE​,0) to form a simple closed curve that is a regular level set. The gradient cannot vanish on it: the only critical point of a dynamical system in ∣x∣<1 is the centre, whose level is 0.
The vector field is tangent and nowhere zero on this compact set, so its speed has a positive minimum. It therefore traverses the curve in finite time and returns to its initial point, giving a periodic orbit. These ovals shrink to the centre as E↓0. This is the planar first integral criterion for a centre and proves the required nonlinear assertion; it also gives Lyapunov stability without asymptotic stability.

One suitable autonomous planar system is
x˙=y2−1,y˙​=x2−1.​
(57)
Its equilibrium points are exactly the four combinations (x,y)=(±1,±1). Its Jacobian matrix is
J(x,y)=(02x​2y0​),λ2=4xy.
(58)
Thus (−1,−1) and (1,1) have eigenvalues ±2 and are saddle equilibria. At the other two points the eigenvalues are ±2i. To establish the nonlinear centre equilibrium classifications, use the first integral
H(x,y)=3x3​−x−3y3​+y,H˙=(x2−1)(y2−1)−(y2−1)(x2−1)=0.
(59)

Immediately to the right of (1,−1) it points up because x2−1>0, so that centre is counterclockwise. This verifies both the nonlinear classifications and the prescribed orientations, with exactly four equilibrium points.
