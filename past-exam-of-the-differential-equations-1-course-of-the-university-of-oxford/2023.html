Let y be any continuous function solving the integral equation on [0,∞), and retain B=∣b∣>0. A first-exit argument avoids assuming in advance that its supremum norm is finite. If ∣y∣ ever reached 2B, continuity would give a first such time T>0. On [0,T] we would have ∣y∣≤2B, so the kernel estimate would imply

An equilibrium point p=(x0​,y0​) of an autonomous planar system is a point where its vector field vanishes:
X(x0​,y0​)=Y(x0​,y0​)=0.​
(21)
For a locally well-posed ordinary differential equation, Lyapunov stability means that every prescribed neighbourhood can be protected by choosing a sufficiently small initial neighbourhood. More precisely, for every ε>0 there exists δ>0 such that every trajectory starting with ∥p(0)−p∥<δ exists for all t≥0 and satisfies
∥p(t)−p∥<εfor all t≥0.​
(22)
The number δ may depend on ε but not on the particular starting point. Asymptotic stability additionally requires convergence to the equilibrium point for all sufficiently close starting points.

A characteristic curve in (x,y,z) follows the vector field defining the first-order partial differential equation. Along such a curve the chain rule turns the equation into
dsdx​=xy,dsdy​=y2,dsdz​=−31​z4.
(33)
Its characteristic projection is the curve (x(s),y(s)) in the independent-variable plane.
Writing x(0)=ξ, y(0)=η, z(0)=ζ, separation of variables gives
x(s)=1−ηsξ​,y(s)=1−ηsη​,z(s)=(1+ζ3s)1/3ζ​.​
(34)
These formulas hold on the maximal interval through s=0 where the denominators remain nonzero, using the real cube root. They include η=0 (a stationary characteristic projection) and ζ=0 (the zero height). For ζ=0, the last formula follows from d(z−3)/ds=1. The alternative zero solution must be retained when deriving this by division by z4.

Parametrize the data by (ξ,η,ζ)=(cosθ,sinθ,−1). The determinant testing Noncharacteristic Cauchy data is
det(−sinθcosθ​cosθsinθsin2θ​)=−sinθ.
(35)
Thus the open upper and lower semicircles are noncharacteristic, but the entire circle is not a valid noncharacteristic data curve: the determinant vanishes at (1,0) and (−1,0).
There is a stronger obstruction at those two points. On y=0, the differential equation forces 0=−z4/3, and hence z=0. This contradicts the prescribed value z=−1 at both endpoints. Therefore
No classical solution satisfies the data on the entire circle.​
(36)
The obstruction is already present if the equation is imposed next to the circle and the solution and its first partial derivatives extend continuously to it.
Away from the endpoints, the characteristic projections are radial half-lines:
(x,y)=r(cosθ,sinθ),r=1−ssinθ1​>0.
(37)
For sinθ>0, r increases with s; for sinθ<0, r decreases with s. Each projection meets its own open semicircle exactly once and never crosses y=0. The endpoint projections themselves are single stationary points, although their lifted characteristic curves have varying height.

To check the parameter ranges explicitly, for η=sinθ>0 they are −∞<s<1, whereas for η<0 they are 1/η<s<1. At the latter lower endpoint the planar projection escapes to infinity. The inverse function theorem and uniqueness for the characteristic ordinary differential equations give a unique smooth solution on each region. Equivalently, each point has the unique foot (x/r,y/r) and parameter (r−1)/y.
A direct check is particularly short. For s=(r−1)/y, let D=xy∂x​+y2∂y​. Then Dr=yr, Dy=y2, and

(42)

The finite boundary where r=1+y, away from the axis, is the parabola y=(x2−1)/2 and corresponds to blowup. Points on y=0 cannot be added to a classical continuation of these regions: where they are approached from a data-determined region, the height behaves like a nonzero multiple of ∣y∣1/3, or has incompatible limits at the endpoints. The formula is therefore the complete classical answer for the compatible arcs, while the full-circle problem as printed has no classical solution.

(43)

u≤Athroughout Q.​
(44)
If A>0, equality at any point of Q gives the same contradiction with M=A, so u<A throughout Q. If A=0, equality is possible: u≡0 is an example. More precisely, write the inequality as Pu+cu≤0 with c=u2≥0. The strong parabolic maximum principle shows that an interior zero at time t0​>0 forces u≡0 on B×[0,t0​].
That conclusion need not extend into the future for a subsolution. For t0​<1, take a spatially constant function which is zero for t≤t0​ and equals −exp(−1/(t−t0​)) for t>t0​. It is smooth, nonincreasing and nonpositive, so Pu+u3≤0, but it becomes negative after t0​.
