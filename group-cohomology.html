Cellular free resolution from a contractible universal cover

Words: 105
Group cohomology OurBigBook.com
For a connected CW complex X with contractible universal cover, let Γ=π1​(X) and R=ZΓ. Lift each cell once and transport its orientation by deck transformations. Its translates form an R-basis of the cellular chains of the cover. The augmentation sends every vertex to one, and contractibility makes the augmented chain complex an exact free resolution of the trivial module Z. An R-linear cochain with trivial coefficient action is constant on each orbit of lifts, identifying HomR​(C∗​(X),Z) with the cellular cohomology complex of X. Consequently Hi(X;Z)=ExtRi​(Z,Z). The same argument with singular simplex lifts works for spaces with a contractible universal cover.

Integral cohomological dimension of a group (cdZ​G)

Words: 90 Articles: 1
Group cohomology OurBigBook.com
The integral cohomological dimension is the least length of a projective resolution of the trivial group ring module Z, equivalently the largest degree in which group cohomology with some module can be nonzero. A group containing torsion has infinite integral cohomological dimension. For Zr the dimension is r, realized by its torus classifying space and detected by its top cohomology.

Stallings-Swan theorem

Words: 33
Integral cohomological dimension of a group OurBigBook.com
A group has integral cohomological dimension at most one precisely when it is a free group, including the trivial free group in dimension zero. This result concerns integral coefficients; a rational dimension-one statement has different conclusions.

Poincare duality group (PDn)

Words: 397 Articles: 5
Group cohomology OurBigBook.com
A Poincare duality group of dimension n has a finite-length resolution of the trivial group ring module Z by finitely generated projective modules, and Hk(G,ZG) vanishes except in degree n, where it is infinite cyclic as an abelian group. The top module D can have an orientation-sign action. Reversing the dual projective resolution gives Hk(G,V)≅Torn−kZG​(D,V), equivalently Hn−k​(G,D⊗V) with the appropriate diagonal action. Untwisted duality requires trivial orientation action. In positive dimension H0(G,ZG)=0, since a finitely supported invariant element cannot exist for an infinite group.

Normal finitely presented subgroups of three-dimensional duality groups

Words: 91
Poincare duality group OurBigBook.com
Let N be a normal finitely presented group inside a dimension-three Poincare duality group G, with G/N containing an element of infinite order. Then N is either a free group or a dimension-two Poincare duality group. The Strebel infinite-index subgroup theorem gives cdN≤2. If the dimension is two, take the preimage of an infinite cyclic subgroup of the quotient. The cyclic-extension shift of group-ring cohomology gives dimension three, forcing this preimage to have finite index by Strebel. Its duality then gives H∗(N;ZN) concentrated in degree two with top group Z. The quotient is virtually cyclic, and surface automorphism realization gives a finite-index surface mapping-torus group.

Classification of two-dimensional Poincare duality groups

Words: 31
Poincare duality group OurBigBook.com
Every integral dimension-two Poincare duality group is the fundamental group of a closed aspherical topological surface. Orientable examples are the torus and closed surfaces of genus at least two. Nonorientable aspherical closed surfaces supply the orientation-twisted examples.

Strebel infinite-index subgroup theorem ([G:H]=∞⟹cdZ​H<dimG)

Words: 39
Poincare duality group OurBigBook.com
An infinite-index subgroup of an integral dimension-d Poincare duality group has integral cohomological dimension at most d−1. No finiteness assumption on the subgroup is needed for this inequality. In dimension three it places every infinite-index subgroup in cohomological dimension at most two.

Extension rule for Poincare duality groups (PDn-by-PDq⟹PDn+q)

Words: 77
Poincare duality group OurBigBook.com
For an extension with kernel a dimension-n Poincare duality group and quotient a dimension-q Poincare duality group, apply the Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence to the ambient group ring. Kernel cohomology is concentrated in degree n and is a regular quotient module with a rank-one orientation twist. Quotient cohomology is then concentrated in degree q with underlying group Z. The extension resolution supplies finiteness and gives cohomological dimension at most n+q, proving duality in that dimension. Orientation actions need not be trivial.

Finite-index invariance of Poincare duality for torsion-free groups

Words: 72
Poincare duality group OurBigBook.com
For a torsion-free group G and finite-index subgroup H, G is a Poincare duality group of dimension n if and only if H is. Finite-index induction and coinduction of ZH coincide and give ZG, so Shapiro's lemma identifies their group-ring cohomology as abelian groups. The finite-index finiteness and cohomological-dimension lemmas supply finite projective resolutions. Without torsion-freeness of G the converse fails: Z×C2​ contains the index-two PD1 subgroup Z but has infinite integral cohomological dimension.

Mod-p cohomology ring of a cyclic group (H∗(Cp​;Fp​))

Words: 120
Group cohomology OurBigBook.com
For a prime p, a classifying Eilenberg–MacLane space for Cp​ is the infinite lens space S∞/Cp​, using scalar multiplication by a primitive pth root on the complex-coordinate sphere. Its contractible universal cover is S∞. The standard two-periodic free resolution has differentials g−1 and 1+g+⋯+gp−1, both zero after applying cochains with trivial Fp​ coefficients. Thus every cohomology degree has dimension one. If p=2, the ring is F2​[t], ∣t∣=1. If p is odd, it is Λ(a)⊗Fp​[b], with ∣a∣=1, ∣b∣=2: graded commutativity gives a2=0 and the periodicity class b induces the degree-two isomorphisms. The Künneth theorem gives two copies of these generators for Cp​×Cp​; the dimension in degree d is d+1 for every p.

Dimension shifting in group cohomology (Hr(G,M′)≃Hr+1(G,M))

Words: 77
Group cohomology OurBigBook.com
Embed a module M over the group ring Z[G] in a module I with vanishing positive group cohomology, and write M′=I/M. The connecting map in the long exact group cohomology sequence of 0→M→I→M′→0 is an isomorphism Hr(G,M′)→Hr+1(G,M) for r≥1, since the groups involving I on both sides vanish. For a finite group and a module I with vanishing Tate cohomology of a finite group in every degree, the same argument shifts every Tate degree. Repeating this construction transports computations to a convenient resolution.

Tate cohomology of a finite group (Hr(G,M))

Words: 67
Group cohomology OurBigBook.com
For a finite group G, Tate cohomology joins group cohomology and group homology through the norm map NG​=∑g∈G​g. For r≥1 it equals ordinary group cohomology; for r≤−2 it equals H−r−1​(G,M). The middle terms are H0(G,M)=MG/NG​M and H−1(G,M)=ker(NG​)/IG​M, where IG​M is generated by gm−m. For the trivial module Z, H−2(G,Z)=Gab. With multiplicative coefficients the norm is the product of the translates.

Continuous cochains with topological coefficients (Cn(G,A)=Cont(Gn,A))

Words: 69
Group cohomology OurBigBook.com
For a topological group module, use continuous maps with pointwise addition and the inhomogeneous group-cohomology differential. The kernel modulo the image defines continuous group cohomology as an abelian group. With general topological coefficients the quotient topology need not be Hausdorff; the cohomology here is the algebraic quotient of continuous cocycles by continuous coboundaries. With trivial discrete coefficients in a prime field, its first cohomology is the space of continuous group homomorphisms to that field's additive group.

Tate cohomology of a cyclic group (H0(G,M), H−1(G,M))

Words: 270 Articles: 4
Group cohomology OurBigBook.com
For G=⟨σ⟩, set D=σ−1 and N=∑g∈G​g. The two periodic groups are MG/NM and kerN/DM. A short exact sequence of modules gives a six-term periodic exact sequence. For multiplicative modules the norm operator is a product and D(x)=σ(x)/x.

Cyclic cohomology of a regular lattice (H0(G,R[G])=H−1(G,R[G])=0)

Words: 70
Tate cohomology of a cyclic group OurBigBook.com
Let G be cyclic and let R[G] carry the regular permutation action. Invariants are constant coefficient vectors, which are the images of the norm operator. A vector with coefficient sum zero is the difference of a vector and its cyclic translate, by successively solving the coordinate differences. Thus the norm group kernel equals (σ−1)R[G]. Both cyclic Tate cohomology groups vanish. This applies to R=Z and R=Zp​, and to finite direct sums of regular lattices.

Herbrand quotient (hG​(M)=#H0(G,M)/#H−1(G,M))

Words: 157 Articles: 2
Tate cohomology of a cyclic group OurBigBook.com
When the two indicated groups are finite, their size ratio is multiplicative on short exact sequences. It is one for finite modules and therefore unchanged by finite-index modifications. A trivial integral module Z has quotient ∣G∣, while an induced regular lattice Zp​[G] has both Tate groups zero and quotient one. These facts permit norm-index calculations without explicitly finding every norm.

Herbrand quotient of a finite module (hG​(M)=1(∣M∣<∞))

Words: 52
Herbrand quotient OurBigBook.com
For a finite module acted on by a cyclic group, write D=σ−1 and N=1+σ+⋯+σn−1. The two Tate cohomology group sizes are ∣kerD∣/∣NM∣ and ∣kerN∣/∣DM∣. The equalities ∣NM∣=∣M∣/∣kerN∣ and ∣DM∣=∣M∣/∣kerD∣ show that these sizes are equal. The exact-sequence multiplication rule for the Herbrand quotient consequently makes it unchanged by finite-index modifications of lattices.

Herbrand quotient of the local multiplicative group (hG​(L×)=[L:K])

Words: 42
Herbrand quotient OurBigBook.com
For a cyclic extension of p-adic fields, sufficiently deep principal units are equivariantly isomorphic to an additive lattice by the p-adic logarithm. The normal basis theorem makes its Herbrand quotient one. Passing across finite unit quotients preserves it. The valuation exact sequence with quotient Z therefore gives the displayed formula.

Corestriction map in group cohomology (CorUG​:Hq(U,M)→Hq(G,M))

Words: 48
Group cohomology OurBigBook.com
For an open finite-index subgroup U of a profinite group G and a discrete G-module M, cohomological transfer gives CorUG​:Hq(U,M)→Hq(G,M). In degree zero it is the sum over cosets, or the norm for multiplicative coefficients. It is transitive and satisfies CorRes=[G:U] and the cup-product projection formula.

Continuous cohomology of a profinite group (Hcontq​(G,M))

Words: 262 Articles: 4
Group cohomology OurBigBook.com
For a profinite group and a discrete module with continuous action, continuous cohomology is computed by continuous cochains. On a compact domain these cochains have finite image. Positive-degree classes become zero on a sufficiently small open subgroup and hence are torsion by restriction followed by corestriction.

Cohomology continuity at closed subgroups (Hq(P,M)=lim​U⊇P​Hq(U,M))

Words: 59
Continuous cohomology of a profinite group OurBigBook.com
For a closed subgroup P of a profinite group G and a discrete G-module M, restriction gives Hq(P,M)=lim​U⊇P, U open​Hq(U,M). Thus a class restricting to zero on P already restricts to zero on one such open subgroup. The property follows by extending continuous finite-image cochains and their identities to a sufficiently small open neighborhood of P.

Cohomological dimension at a prime (cdp​(K)=cdp​(GK​))

Words: 159 Articles: 2
Continuous cohomology of a profinite group OurBigBook.com
The value cdp​(G) is the least n such that all cohomology in degrees greater than n vanishes for every discrete p-primary torsion module. Vanishing in degree n+1 for all such modules suffices by dimension shifting. Dimension does not increase on closed subgroups. For a field, use its absolute Galois group.

Trivial coefficients detect the cohomological dimension of a pro-p group (Hn+1(P,Fp​)=0 ⟺ cdp​(P)≤n)

Words: 71
Cohomological dimension at a prime OurBigBook.com
For a pro-p group P, vanishing of Hn+1(P,Fp​) implies vanishing in that degree for every discrete p-primary module. Finite such modules have composition factors equal to the trivial module Fp​, so the long exact sequence gives the result by induction. General modules are unions of finite stable submodules, and continuous cohomology commutes with filtered unions. Dimension shifting gives cdp​(P)≤n. The converse is immediate from the definition.

Characteristic p Galois dimension bound (cdp​(K)≤1)

A field of characteristic p satisfies cdp​(K)≤1. The Artin-Schreier sequence and vanishing of positive-degree cohomology of the additive separable-closure module yield the bound. It concerns discrete p-primary modules; it does not assert that the p-primary Brauer group vanishes.

Periodic group cohomology (Δ⌣−:Hi(G;M)≅Hi+n(G;M))

For a finite group, the usual integral notion asks for a class Δ∈Hn(G;Z) whose cup product gives the displayed isomorphisms for every coefficient module M and every i>0. Cohomology ring of a finite cyclic group over its prime field (H∗(BCp​;Fp​))

Words: 109
Periodic group cohomology OurBigBook.com
The alternating resolution maps g−1 and 1+g+⋯+gp−1 for a cyclic group Cp​ become zero after applying homomorphisms to its trivial prime field module. Thus there is one class in every degree. A two-step shift of the resolution represents a degree-two periodicity class u. For odd p, a degree-one class v squares to zero by graded commutativity, and
H∗(BCp​;Fp​)=ΛFp​​(v)⊗Fp​[u],∣v∣=1, ∣u∣=2.
(1)
One can take u=β(v) for the Bockstein homomorphism. For p=2 the two maps agree over F2​[C2​], so a one-step shift gives H∗(BC2​;F2​)=F2​[v] with ∣v∣=1. The Künneth theorem then gives dimension i+1 in degree i for B(Cp​×Cp​), obstructing periodic group cohomology and hence such a free sphere action.

Periodic group cohomology from a free sphere action (Δ∈Hn(BG;F))

Words: 94
Periodic group cohomology OurBigBook.com
Suppose a finite group acts freely on Sn−1 and trivially on its integral cohomology. The Borel construction gives a sphere fibration over BG whose total space is homotopy equivalent to the (n−1)-dimensional quotient. Its Serre spectral sequence has just two rows with trivial local coefficients. Transgressing the fibre generator gives Δ; the sole differential is multiplication by it up to sign. For i>0, both the source and target terms have total degrees above n−1, so their surviving kernel and cokernel must vanish. Hence multiplication by Δ gives period n in positive-degree cohomology over any field.

Torsion-free finite extension of a lattice (1→Zn→Γ→Q→1)

Words: 86
Group extension OurBigBook.com
For a specified finite group action on A=Zn, extensions are classified by e∈H2(Q,A). For an element q of prime order p, a lift g satisfies gp=a∈Aq; changing the lift changes a by Nq​v=(1+q+⋯+qp−1)v. Thus the extension is torsion-free precisely when every restriction to a prime-order cyclic subgroup is nonzero in Aq/Nq​A. Any finite-order element has a prime-order power, so this tests all torsion. An arbitrary finite quotient must not be confused with faithful holonomy on a maximal translation lattice.

Section normal form for a split group extension (g=i(π(g))n,n∈N)

Words: 61
Split group extension OurBigBook.com
If a group homomorphism section i:G/N→G satisfies πi=id, then each g∈G has a unique form i(q)n with q∈G/N and n∈N: take q=π(g) and n=i(q)−1g. Thus (q,n)↦i(q)n is a bijection. It is a homomorphism for the ordinary direct product of groups exactly when the section image centralizes N. In general it describes a semidirect product with a nontrivial conjugation action.

Mihailova subgroup (L={(u,v)∈F×F:π(u)=π(v)})

Words: 77
Fiber product of groups OurBigBook.com
For a finite group presentation H=⟨x1​,…,xm​∣r1​,…,rn​⟩, let F be the free group on its generators and π:F→H. The corresponding fiber product of groups is generated by (xi​,xi​) and (1,rj​). Conjugation by the diagonal pairs supplies every pair (1,r) with r in the normal closure of the relators, and (u,v)=(u,u)(1,u−1v) proves the generating claim. Membership of (1,w) is equivalent to w=1 in H, so this finitely generated subgroup can have undecidable membership even though F×F has soluble word problem.

Integral central extensions of a rank-two free abelian group (En​=⟨x,y,z: z central, [x,y]=zn⟩)

Words: 96

With a fixed identification of the kernel with Z and quotient with Z2, these central group extensions are classified by n∈Z. Choose lifts x,y of the quotient generators; their commutator is zn and is unchanged by central changes of lifts. Every element has unique normal form zmybxa, proving this invariant is complete. The zero parameter gives Z3 and parameter one gives the integer Heisenberg group. The Koszul resolution for a rank-two free abelian group independently computes H2(Z2,Z)≅Z.

f(gh)=f(g)+g⋅f(h).
(14)
Thus crossed homomorphisms are precisely one-cocycles.
Principal crossed homomorphism
Words: 34
Crossed homomorphism OurBigBook.com
For m∈M, the map g↦g⋅m−m is a principal crossed homomorphism. It is the group coboundary of the zero-cochain m, so first group cohomology is the quotient of crossed homomorphisms by principal crossed homomorphisms.

A group coboundary is a cochain in the image of the preceding coboundary map. Quotienting group cocycles by group coboundaries gives group cohomology.

An inhomogeneous group n-cochain with values in a G-module M is a function Gn→M. The standard alternating group coboundary turns these groups into the cochain complex whose cohomology is Hn(G,M).

Finite-group cohomology is annihilated by the group order

If G is finite and n>0, multiplication by ∣G∣ annihilates Hn(G,M). Indeed, restriction to the trivial subgroup is zero in positive degree, while the restriction-corestriction composite is multiplication by ∣G∣.

Restriction-corestriction identity in group cohomology

For a subgroup K≤G, restriction followed by corestriction acts on group cohomology as the sum over coset translates. For K trivial, inner automorphisms act trivially on cohomology, so this sum is multiplication by ∣G∣.

For a normal subgroup H◃G, quotient Q=G/H, and a G-module M, the Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence has
E2p,q​=Hp(Q,Hq(H,M))⟹Hp+q(G,M).

Cyclic-extension shift of group-ring cohomology (Hj+1(N⋊Z;Z[N⋊Z])≅Hj(N;ZN))

Words: 95
Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence OurBigBook.com
If N has a finite resolution by finitely generated projective modules, restriction of the semidirect-product group ring gives a direct sum of copies of ZN, indexed by the integers. On its kernel cohomology the cyclic generator shifts those copies, possibly twisting each copy by an automorphism. Invariants are zero because elements have finite support; coinvariants identify with a single copy. The two-column Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence therefore gives the displayed isomorphism of abelian groups. A group with such a finite resolution and cohomological dimension d has Hd(N;ZN)=0, so the cyclic extension has dimension d+1.

Inflation-restriction exact sequence (0→H1(G/N,MN)→H1(G,M)→H1(N,M)G/N)

Words: 73
Five-term exact sequence in group cohomology OurBigBook.com
For a normal subgroup N of G and a G-module M, the low-degree exact sequence begins 0→H1(G/N,MN)→H1(G,M)→H1(N,M)G/N→H2(G/N,MN). The first map inflates cocycles along G→G/N; the second restricts them. If the restriction is trivial, subtract a coboundary to make the cocycle vanish on N and descend to the quotient. Thus for a finite Galois extension, the kernel of restriction on Galois cohomology with finite coefficients is finite.
