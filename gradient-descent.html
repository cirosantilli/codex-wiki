Gradient descent (xk+1​=xk​−η∇f(xk​))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 42
Gradient descent is an iterative method for minimizing a differentiable function f: choose an initial vector x0​ and repeatedly move opposite its gradient.[ref]
With a fixed positive step size η, the update is
xk+1​=xk​−η∇f(xk​),k=0,1,2,….
(1)
The direction and the step size play different roles: the gradient supplies local information about increase, while η determines how far that information is trusted. A varying step size gives ηk​ instead; exact line search for a positive-definite quadratic chooses it by minimizing along the current direction.
Why is this the steepest direction? For a unit vector v in the Euclidean norm, the directional derivative is ⟨∇f(x),v⟩. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality bounds it below by −∥∇f(x)∥2​, attained by v=−∇f(x)/∥∇f(x)∥2​ when the gradient is nonzero. This is steepest descent relative to the Euclidean norm; changing the norm can change the steepest direction. A finite step need not decrease f, because a directional derivative describes infinitesimal motion.
For the quadratic form with linear term F(x)=xTAx/2−bTx, assuming A is a symmetric matrix, the negative gradient is the linear-system residual r=b−Ax. Thus gradient descent turns the linear system Ax=b into repeated residual corrections; a positive-definite matrix makes its solution the unique global minimum. This is useful when applying the linear map x↦Ax is affordable but directly solving the linear system is costly.
The attraction is that a gradient evaluation gives an entire update without solving a new linear system. The difficulty is choosing a step size that progresses along gently curved directions without overshooting steep ones. Fixed-step gradient descent on a positive-definite quadratic makes this tension exact: every eigenvector direction has its own multiplier, determined by its eigenvalue. The resulting condition number dependence explains why a narrow valley can slow even an inexpensive iterative method.
The simplicity of the gradient update and its sensitivity to the condition number of the Hessian matrix are central practical features.[ref]

Fixed-step gradient descent on a positive-definite quadratic (ek+1​=(I−ηA)ek​)

Consider the original two-dimensional example
f(x1​,x2​)=21​(x12​+9x22​),x0​=(2,1)T,∇f(x)=(x1​,9x2​)T.
(3)
The Hessian matrix is A=diag(1,9), a symmetric matrix and positive-definite matrix. Its eigenvectors are the coordinate directions, its eigenvalues are 1 and 9, and its Euclidean norm condition number is 9. The unique global minimum is x∗​=0, so the error ek​=xk​−x∗​ equals xk​. The linear map I−ηA, with I the identity matrix, gives
x1,k​=2(1−η)k,x2,k​=(1−9η)k.
(4)
For η=1/5=0.2, the two coordinate error multipliers are 0.8 and −0.8. Consequently x1​=(1.6,−0.8)T, x2​=(1.28,0.64)T, and the second coordinate changes sign at every step while both shrink. The Euclidean norm of the error contracts by 0.8 each step. The objective gap, which we call the quadratic energy, contracts by its square:
Ek​=f(xk​)−f(x∗​)=213​(2516​)k,E0​=6.5,E1​=4.16,E2​=2.6624.
(5)
The level sets of f are ellipses. Oscillation across their narrow direction is compatible with strictly decreasing energy.
For η=1/4=0.25, the coordinate error multipliers become 0.75 and −1.25, giving x1​=(1.5,−1.25)T. The first coordinate still tends to zero, but the second alternates and grows. Direct substitution gives
Ek​=2(169​)k+29​(1625​)k,E1​=32261​=8.15625.
(6)
Thus a larger positive step size can turn a strictly convex function into a divergent iteration. Energy and error are different quantities: coordinate errors carry signed multipliers, while each coordinate's contribution to energy carries the squared multiplier.
For the general quadratic form with linear term
f(x)=21​xTAx−bTx,A=AT>0,x∗​=A−1b,
(7)
the error obeys the same linear map ek+1​=(I−ηA)ek​. The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices supplies an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors qi​ with positive eigenvalues λi​. Writing e0​=∑i​ci​qi​ gives the exact solution and energy identity
ek​=∑i​ci​(1−ηλi​)kqi​,Ek​=21​∑i​λi​ci2​(1−ηλi​)2k.
(8)
Therefore convergence from every initial vector is equivalent to
∣1−ηλi​∣<1for every i,or equivalently0<η<λmax​2​.
(9)
At η=2/λmax​, a nonzero error component along a largest-eigenvalue eigenvector flips sign without shrinking. Above that threshold it grows. A particular start can still converge if it has zero components in every unstable direction; the interval is the condition for all starts. If ρ=maxi​∣1−ηλi​∣<1, then ∥ek​∥2​≤ρk∥e0​∥2​ and Ek​≤ρ2kE0​. These bounds follow by summing the squared components in the orthonormal basis.

Optimal fixed step for quadratic gradient descent (η∗​=2/(λmin​+λmax​))

Words: 257
Fixed-step gradient descent on a positive-definite quadratic OurBigBook.com
For a fixed symmetric matrix and positive-definite matrix A, what positive step size minimizes the largest coordinate error multiplier over all eigenvector directions? Let m=λmin​>0 and L=λmax​. Since ∣1−ηλ∣ is a convex function of λ, its maximum over the eigenvalues occurs at an endpoint:
ρ(η)=max{∣1−ηm∣,∣1−ηL∣}.
(10)
Balance the two extremes by solving 1−ηm=−(1−ηL):
η∗​=m+L2​,ρ∗​=L+mL−m​=κ+1κ−1​,κ=mL​.
(11)
Here κ is the Euclidean norm condition number of A. This balance is optimal: for η<η∗​ the endpoint 1−ηm exceeds ρ∗​, and for η>η∗​ the endpoint ηL−1 exceeds ρ∗​. The optimal worst-case energy factor is ρ∗2​, rather than ρ∗​. This optimizes a uniform one-step bound; a particular initial error supported on fewer eigenvectors can have a different best step size.
In the example m=1, L=9, so η∗​=0.2 and ρ∗2​=0.64. Both eigenvalue directions attain that energy factor, making the displayed history exact. The smaller step size 0.1 instead gives
Ek​=2(0.81)k+29​(0.01)k.
(12)
Its first step reaches E1​=1.665, below 4.16 for η=0.2, but subsequent progress is limited by the first direction's energy factor 0.81. By k=5, the energies are approximately 0.69736 and 0.69793, respectively; by k=6, the 0.2 iteration is ahead. A better first step need not yield a faster long-run history.
As the condition number grows, ρ∗​ approaches one: stability in the direction of the largest eigenvalue forces small movement along the smallest-eigenvalue direction. This is a precise reason to improve scaling or use a method such as the conjugate gradient method, rather than merely increasing the step size. When m=L, choosing η=1/m eliminates every error component in one step.

Gradient ascent (xn+1​=xn​+ηn​∇J(xn​))

Words: 58
Gradient descent OurBigBook.com
Gradient ascent seeks a maximum by stepping along the gradient of a differentiable objective. It is gradient descent applied to −J. Taylor expansion gives J(x+η∇J)=J(x)+η∥∇J∥2+O(η2), so sufficiently small positive steps improve the objective when the gradient is nonzero. Line search, projection onto admissible controls, and noise-aware gradient estimates are common practical modifications; a local stationary result need not be globally optimal.

Lipschitz gradient (∥∇f(x)−∇f(y)∥2​≤β∥x−y∥2​)

Words: 620 Articles: 4
Gradient descent OurBigBook.com
A differentiable function has a β-Lipschitz gradient on a set D if its gradient satisfies Lipschitz continuity there:
∥∇f(x)−∇f(y)∥2​≤β∥x−y∥2​(x,y∈D),β≥0.
(14)
All norms here are the Euclidean norm. The constant limits how fast the gradient changes; it is not a bound on the magnitude of the gradient. For f(x)=(x12​+9x22​)/2, the gradient is the linear map x↦diag(1,9)x. Its operator norm is 9, so β=9 works globally and is sharp by taking x−y=(0,1)T. The gradient itself is unbounded on the whole Euclidean space.
If f has a continuous Hessian matrix on an open convex set D and ∥∇2f(z)∥2​≤β throughout D, integrate that linear map along the line segment from x to y:
∇f(y)−∇f(x)=∫01​∇2f(x+t(y−x))(y−x)dt.
(15)
The operator norm bound proves the displayed Lipschitz continuity. Convexity of the domain keeps the entire line segment inside it. Bounding the Hessian matrix only at the endpoints is insufficient.

Block Lipschitz constant from coordinate smoothness (LB​≤∑i∈B​Li​)

For a continuously differentiable convex function on Rn with coordinate Lipschitz continuity constants Li​, its block coordinate gradient is Lipschitz continuous along each corresponding coordinate subspace, with constant at most SB​=∑i∈B​Li​.
To prove this without assuming a Hessian matrix, put δB​=∇B​f(x)−∇B​f(y). Sum the coordinate derivative bound by Bregman divergence over the block, then repeat with x,y interchanged and average:
∥δB​∥2≤SB​(Df​(x,y)+Df​(y,x))=SB​(∇f(x)−∇f(y))T(x−y).
(16)
If x−y is supported in the block, the dot product uses only δB​. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and cancellation of its Euclidean norm give the result, including the zero case separately. Taking the full block supplies a global Lipschitz gradient constant ∑i​Li​.

Descent lemma (f(v)≤f(u)+⟨∇f(u),v−u⟩+2L​∥v−u∥2)

Words: 253
Lipschitz gradient OurBigBook.com
Suppose f is a differentiable function on an open convex set D⊆Rn and has an L-Lipschitz gradient there, in the Euclidean norm. For u,v∈D, the descent lemma states
f(v)≤f(u)+⟨∇f(u),v−u⟩+2L​∥v−u∥22​.
(19)
A bounded continuous Hessian matrix gives one sufficient route to this estimate.[ref]
For a short proof, put d=v−u. The fundamental theorem of calculus along the line segment gives
f(v)−f(u)−⟨∇f(u),d⟩=∫01​⟨∇f(u+td)−∇f(u),d⟩dt.
(20)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the Lipschitz gradient bound make the integrand at most Lt∥d∥22​; its integral is L∥d∥22​/2. This proof needs no assumption that f is a convex function.
For the gradient descent update v=u−η∇f(u), provided v∈D, substitution yields
f(v)≤f(u)−η(1−2Lη​)∥∇f(u)∥22​.
(21)
For L>0 and 0<η<2/L, this ensures strict decrease whenever the gradient is nonzero. At η=2/L it guarantees only nonincrease; the quadratic example has a nonshrinking alternating component at this endpoint. At η=1/L the guaranteed decrease is ∥∇f(u)∥22​/(2L). A bound valid only on D cannot justify a step leaving D.
If a fixed admissible step size is used forever, every iterate stays in D, and f(xk​) has a finite lower bound, summing this inequality shows ∑k​∥∇f(xk​)∥22​<∞, hence ∇f(xk​)→0. That conclusion alone gives neither convergence of the iterates nor a global minimum. For example, the smooth polynomial f(x)=x4−x2 has f′(0)=0 and f′′(0)=−2: gradient descent started at zero never moves, although f(1/2​)=−1/4<0. On D=(−1,1), ∣f′′(x)∣≤10, so the Lipschitz gradient hypothesis holds on this domain. Convex functions and strongly convex functions provide additional structure; the exact quadratic convergence result above uses a positive-definite matrix.

Spectral weighting of conjugate gradient error (∥p(A)x∥A2​=∑i​αi2​λi​p(λi​)2)

Conjugate gradient method OurBigBook.com
For a symmetric positive-definite matrix, choose an orthonormal eigenbasis and write the exact solution as x=∑i​αi​vi​. Starting at zero, the conjugate gradient method chooses an admissible polynomial p(0)=1 of degree at most the iteration count that minimizes the displayed error. Nonzero vectors are conjugate directions with respect to a symmetric positive-definite matrix A when they are pairwise orthogonal in the inner product ⟨u,v⟩A​=uTAv. Finite termination of conjugate directions (xn+1​=A−1b)

Words: 91
Conjugate direction OurBigBook.com
Let p1​,…,pn​ be conjugate directions for a symmetric positive-definite matrix A. Starting from any x1​, minimize xTAx/2−bTx successively along those full lines, stopping early if the linear-system residual rj​=b−Axj​ vanishes. The quadratic minimization along a line uses αj​=pjT​rj​/(pjT​Apj​) and gives rj+1​=rj​−αj​Apj​.
This makes pjT​rj+1​=0. For i<j, conjugacy preserves piT​rj+1​=piT​rj​. By mathematical induction, rj+1​ is orthogonal to all preceding search vectors. The n conjugate directions are linearly independent and hence form a basis, so rn+1​=0. Thus the method reaches the unique solution of the linear system in at most n steps in exact arithmetic. The conjugate gradient method generates such directions without storing a complete prescribed basis in advance.

Krylov dimension from spectral components (dimK∞​(A,v)=#{λ:Pλ​v=0})

Krylov subspace OurBigBook.com
For a diagonalizable finite-dimensional operator, write v=∑j​uj​ with nonzero uj​ in distinct eigenspaces of eigenvalues λj​. Then Arv=∑j​λjr​uj​. The Vandermonde matrix of the first k powers is invertible for k distinct eigenvalues, so these powers span exactly the k independent components uj​. Thus the stabilized Krylov subspace dimension is k. It counts distinct spectral components, not the number of nonzero coefficients in an arbitrary eigenbasis: A=I, v=e1​+e2​ has Krylov dimension one. Equivalently it is the minimum number of eigenvectors needed when those vectors may be chosen freely.
