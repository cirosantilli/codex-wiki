Directional derivative (Dv​f)

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Calculus Multivariable calculus Partial derivative
OurBigBook.com Words: 494 Articles: 2
The directional derivative tests a function along a chosen straight line, including lines that follow neither coordinate axis. For a fixed vector v and a point a in an open subset of Rn, define
Dv​f(a)=limt→0​tf(a+tv)−f(a)​=dtd​f(a+tv)​t=0​,
(6)
if the limit exists. With a unit vector v, the parameter t measures signed distance in the input Euclidean space, so the directional derivative is change per unit distance. For a general v, it is change per unit parameter; the norm of v also sets the rate of travel.[ref]
For a differentiable real-valued function, the multivariable chain rule gives
Dv​f(a)=∇f(a)⋅v.
(7)
Thus one gradient predicts the local response in every direction. This formula requires differentiability, rather than merely separate partial derivatives.[ref]
Return to f(x,y)=x2+xy+2y2 at a=(1,1). Its gradient is (3,5). In the unit direction u=(3/5,4/5),
Du​f(a)=353​+554​=529​​.
(8)
One can check this without invoking the gradient formula: substitution gives
f(1+53​t,1+54​t)=4+529​t+2553​t2.
(9)
The difference quotient is 29/5+(53/25)t, whose limit is 29/5. This calculation separates the exact change along a finite path from its first-order rate at the starting point.
Changing the travel speed changes the parameter rate. Taking v=(3,4)=5u gives Dv​f(a)=29, because the input travels five distance units per parameter unit. For a differentiable function, Dcv​f(a)=cDv​f(a) for any real c: negative c reverses the orientation as well as scaling the rate. Normalizing v before comparing different slopes avoids confusing direction with speed.
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality bounds the rate in any unit direction by ∥∇f(a)∥. Here the largest rate is 34​≈5.831, attained in the direction (3,5)/34​; the chosen (3/5,4/5) gives the slightly smaller rate 5.8. The perpendicular unit direction (−5,3)/34​ gives zero. That means no first-order height change, rather than no change at all: along this direction the example has f(a+tu)=4+(28/34)t2.
This is useful for following a height or temperature scalar field, choosing a local search direction in mathematical optimization, and distinguishing a coordinate sensitivity from a response along an actual path. For a curved path, derivative along a curve uses its instantaneous tangent vector. Even existence of all directional derivatives does not guarantee one common linear approximation; directional derivatives do not imply differentiability gives a concrete counterexample.
