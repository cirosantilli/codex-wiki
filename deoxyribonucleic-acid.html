Deoxyribonucleic acid

 Home Chemistry Polymer Nucleic acid
OurBigBook.com Words: 453
Deoxyribonucleic acid, or DNA, is a nucleotide polymer that stores biological information in its nucleotide sequence. The crucial variable is order: two DNA molecules can contain the same numbers of each nucleobase yet carry different nucleotide sequences. This makes a physical molecule an inheritable record, rather than merely a mixture of ingredients. Genes are functional regions of that record; the whole DNA nucleotide sequence is not one long gene. See NHGRI: DNA fact sheet.
Each DNA strand has a backbone of alternating deoxyribose sugars and phosphate groups joined through covalent bonds. Attached nucleobases supply the familiar four-letter alphabet: adenine A, thymine T, guanine G, and cytosine C. In ordinary double-stranded DNA, base pairing matches A with T and G with C. The backbone connects successive nucleotides along a strand; base pairing connects partners across strands. Keeping these two connections distinct explains how strands can separate while each retains its own nucleotide sequence.
The two backbones have directions, conventionally labelled 5′ and 3′ after numbered carbon chemical atoms in deoxyribose. They form antiparallel nucleic acid strands: following one from 5′ to 3′ takes you opposite to the other's 5′-to-3′ direction. For the chosen DNA nucleotide sequence 5′-ATGCC-3′, write its partner directly underneath as 3′-TACGG-5′. Now read that partner from its own 5′ end: its reverse complement is 5′-GGCAT-3′. Complementing letters and reversing their order are separate operations. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: the structure and function of DNA.
The diagram's rails represent sugar–phosphate backbones and its coloured blocks represent nucleobases, not individual chemical atoms. Reading left to right gives five matched positions: A–T, T–A, G–C, C–G, C–G. There are two A–T-type base pairs and three G–C-type base pairs; the complete two-strand example contains ten nucleotides. Reversing the bottom row changes the reading convention, not the physical partners. Real double-stranded DNA winds into a nucleic acid double helix; the flattened ladder emphasizes base pairing and direction without pretending to show its three-dimensional geometry.
The motivating consequence is copying by a template. After strands separate, each existing DNA strand specifies which incoming nucleotides should accompany it through base pairing. DNA replication uses enzymes to build complementary strands, allowing the record to persist through successive biological cells. Base pairing supplies a selection rule, while enzymes perform the chemistry: a matching diagram alone is not a complete copying mechanism. The connection between structure and copying was already central to the model published by Watson and Crick on 25 April 1953. See Watson and Crick, Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids, Nature 171, 737–738 (1953).
Stored information needs interpretation. Some DNA regions specify proteins through RNA; others contribute to functional RNA or regulate gene expression, and some have no known function. Non-coding DNA means sequence that does not specify a protein's sequence of amino acids, not automatically useless sequence. Nor does a DNA nucleotide sequence alone determine an organism's fate: phenotypes also depend on biological context and environment. See NHGRI: non-coding DNA. Its double-stranded form is often modeled mechanically as a worm-like chain, with elasticity and effective excluded volume depending on solvent conditions.
