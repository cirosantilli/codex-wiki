Affine map (x↦Ax+b)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Affine geometry Affine space
OurBigBook.com Words: 495 Articles: 5
An affine map combines a linear map with a translation. After choosing coordinate origins, it has the form T:Rp→Rq, T(x)=Mx+b, where the q×p matrix M is its linear part of an affine map and b is a fixed vector. Equivalently, it preserves every affine combination. [ref]
An original planar example is
T(x,y)=(2x+y+1,y−2),M=(20​11​),b=(1−2​).
(25)
It stretches horizontally, applies a shear mapping, and then translates. The vertices A=(0,0), B=(2,0) and C=(0,2) become A′=(1,−2), B′=(5,−2) and C′=(3,0). A square grid becomes a grid of parallelograms; straight edges and their weighted points still fit together.
This map is not a linear map with respect to the displayed origins, because T(0,0)=(1,−2) rather than zero. It also is not a Euclidean similarity: AB has length 2 and A′B′ has length 4, while the right angle at A becomes 45∘. Its determinant is 2, so it doubles planar area. These metric changes explain why affine geometry studies straightness, incidence and weighted points rather than treating every transformation as an isometry.
The linear part is an invertible matrix, giving the concise inverse T−1(u,v)=((u−v−3)/2,v+2). More generally an affine map is bijective exactly when its linear part of an affine map is bijective. Between equal finite-dimensional spaces, this means an invertible matrix; the translation cannot repair a loss of rank of a linear map.
