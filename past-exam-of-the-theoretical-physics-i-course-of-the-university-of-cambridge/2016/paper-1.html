Write the bead position in Cartesian coordinates as xB​=(acosθB​,asinθB​,bθB​). Differentiation gives ∣x˙B​∣2=(a2+b2)θ˙B2​, while the free particle has ∣x˙∣2=r˙2+r2θ˙2+z˙2. Thus the kinetic energy is the sum of these squared speeds weighted by half the respective mass. The squared spring length is
ℓ2=∣x−xB​∣2=r2+a2−2arcos(θ−θB​)+(z−bθB​)2.
(1)
A spring obeying Hooke's law with zero natural length has potential energy kℓ2/2. Consequently L=T−kℓ2/2, and deleting the additive constant −ka2/2 gives
L=2m​(r˙2+r2θ˙2+z˙2)+2M(a2+b2)​θ˙B2​−2k​[r2−2arcos(θ−θB​)+(z−bθB​)2].
(2)
The spring length is the straight separation of its endpoints, not distance measured along the helix. The fixed frictionless constraint is already incorporated by using the generalized coordinate θB​; it supplies no extra potential energy.

The continuous transformation θ↦θ+ϵ, θB​↦θB​+ϵ, z↦z+bϵ, r↦r moves both endpoints by the same screw motion and preserves the fixed helix. Both Δ and u are unchanged, so δL=0. Noether theorem therefore gives
Q=pθ​+pθB​​+bpz​=mr2θ˙+M(a2+b2)θ˙B​+bmz˙=constant.​
(4)
This is the Noether conserved quantity for a mechanical point symmetry applied to helical symmetry. In physical Cartesian terms it equals total z angular momentum plus b times total z linear momentum: the bead contributes Ma2θ˙B​+b(Mbθ˙B​). Neither pure axial translation nor pure axial rotation preserves the fixed coil separately. Their conserved combination is a consequence of the helical symmetry, not two independently conserved momenta. Substitution of the four equations of motion verifies Q˙​=−karsinΔ+k(arsinΔ+bu)−bku=0.[ref]

In the negligible bead-mass limit the bead contribution to Q vanishes. Since the particle starts at rest, Q=0, and hence r2θ˙+bz˙=0. Assume b=0 and a regular massless limit. Integrating along the trajectory gives
Δz=−b1​∫0T​r2θ˙dt=−b1​∮C​(xdy−ydx),
(5)
where C is the closed projection onto the plane perpendicular to the coil axis. The given final r and θ ensure that this projection closes. Green's theorem identifies
A=21​∮C​(xdy−ydx)=21​∮C​r2dθ,Δz=−b2A​​.
(6)
Thus A is the signed area enclosed by the transverse projection, positive for counterclockwise traversal viewed from positive z. Self-intersecting projections contribute their winding-weighted areas; it is neither an unsigned area nor an area of a surface in three dimensions. For b>0, a positive signed area gives negative axial displacement. This is signed-area displacement under a helical constraint: returning to the same projected coordinates can leave a nonzero axial displacement.
The plotted loop illustrates the geometric integral only; it is not asserted to be a particular spring trajectory.

Use natural units and the Minkowski metric gμν​=diag(1,−1,−1,−1), with Aμ=(Φ,A) and Aμ​=(Φ,−A). Then F0i​=Ei​, Fi0=Ei​, and Fij=−ϵijk​Bk​. The electromagnetic field tensor is an antisymmetric tensor, which gives gives
δL=−21​FμνδFμν​=−Fμν∂μ​δAν​.
(7)
integration by parts in the action, with the variation vanishing on its boundary, yields δS=∫d4x(∂μ​Fμν)δAν​. Independent variations of the four components therefore give ∂μ​Fμν=0. The time component is ∇⋅E=0; the spatial components are ∇×B−∂t​E=0. These are the two source-free Maxwell equations obtained dynamically. The other two, ∇⋅B=0 and ∂t​B+∇×E=0, follow identically from F=dA, through the Electromagnetic Bianchi identity. They are not additional Euler-Lagrange field equations.[ref]

Hold ϕ,ϕ∗ fixed while varying Aν​. With the specified convention Dμ​=∂μ​+iqAμ​, the two matter variations are δ(Dμ​ϕ)=iqϕδAμ​ and δ(Dμ​ϕ)∗=−iqϕ∗δAμ​. Thus
δLmatter​=iq[ϕ(Dνϕ)∗−ϕ∗Dνϕ]δAν​=−JνδAν​.
(10)
Adding the Maxwell variation from part (a) gives δS=∫d4x[∂μ​Fμν−Jν]δAν​. Consequently
∂μ​Fμν=Jν,Jμ=iq[ϕ∗Dμϕ−ϕ(Dμϕ)∗].​
(11)
Expanding the gauge covariant derivatives supplies a useful convention check:
Jμ=iq(ϕ∗∂μϕ−ϕ∂μϕ∗)−2q2Aμ∣ϕ∣2.
(12)
In particular, the last sign is negative for the specified plus sign in Dμ​. Reversing that convention requires consistently changing the phase transformation and current conventions as well. This current is the matter source in scalar electrodynamics; its A dependence must not be dropped.

Take the constant-parameter subgroup of the local transformation: δϕ=−iqϵϕ, δϕ∗=iqϵϕ∗, δAμ​=0. The momenta conjugate to the complex scalar field gradients are Πϕμ​=(Dμϕ)∗ and Πϕ∗μ​=Dμϕ. Noether theorem gives
jμ=Πϕμ​ϵδϕ​+Πϕ∗μ​ϵδϕ∗​=iq[ϕ∗Dμϕ−ϕ(Dμϕ)∗]=Jμ.
(13)
The scalar field equations are Dμ​Dμϕ+m2ϕ=0 and their complex conjugate. Applying the product rule, the gauge-potential terms cancel to give
∂μ​Jμ=iq[ϕ∗Dμ​Dμϕ−ϕ(Dμ​Dμϕ)∗]=iq[−m2∣ϕ∣2+m2∣ϕ∣2]=0​.
(14)
This establishes conservation of the Noether current of scalar electrodynamics on shell, and conservation of its integrated electric charge when there is no boundary flux. An arbitrary function α(x) describes gauge redundancy; the associated Noether second theorem supplies an identity among Euler-Lagrange field equations, rather than a distinct physical charge for each function. Indeed its on-shell current can be written jμ[α]=αJμ−Fμν∂ν​α=−∂ν​(αFμν). The antisymmetric tensor makes its divergence vanish. Independently, differentiating the Maxwell equation also gives ∂ν​Jν=∂ν​∂μ​Fμν=0, confirming compatibility with the matter derivation.[ref]

Treat ϕ and ϕ∗ as independent variables during variation. With Minkowski metric (+−−−), differentiation with respect to ϕ∗ gives
∂(∂μ​ϕ∗)∂L​=∂μϕ+ieAμϕ,∂ϕ∗∂L​=−m2ϕ−ieAμ∂μ​ϕ+f(t).
(15)
The Euler-Lagrange field equation is therefore □ϕ+2ieAμ∂μ​ϕ+ie(∂μ​Aμ)ϕ+m2ϕ=f. The static purely temporal potential has ∂μ​Aμ=∂t​V=0, giving
(∂t2​−∇2+2ieV(r)∂t​+m2)ϕ=f(t).​
(16)
variation with respect to ϕ gives (□−2ieAμ∂μ​+m2)ϕ∗=f(t). This is the complex conjugate equation when the driving force is real, as is required for the supplied lagrangian density to be real. For a complex driving amplitude a real source term would instead require conjugate coefficients. The interaction supplied here is linear in the external potential: adding the e2Aμ​Aμ∣ϕ∣2 term from full minimal coupling would change the problem and its equation of motion.

Time independence of V makes the Green function depend on t,t′ through τ=t−t′. A spatially varying potential does not imply spatial translation invariance. Fix r′ and set x=r−r′; write the Fourier coefficient more fully as Gr′​(k;ω). The source position is an implicit parameter in the printed notation. Define
Vr′​(q)=∫d3xV(r′+x)e−iq⋅x=eiq⋅r′V(q).
(17)
The ordinary derivative terms transform into (−ω2+k2+m2)Gr′​. The product V∂t​G becomes a convolution, and 2ie(−iω)=2eω. More explicitly, insert both inverse spatial Fourier transforms into VG: the integral over x enforces q+k′=k. The transformed delta source is one, leaving
(−ω2+k2+m2)Gr′​(k;ω)+2eω∫(2π)3d3k′​Vr′​(k−k′)Gr′​(k′;ω)=1.​
(18)
This is a source-position-dependent Fourier Green function. Dropping the explicit r′ subscripts recovers the requested notation with exactly the potential transform defined in the source. No assumption of a homogeneous potential is needed. The factor (2π)−3 and the source-position phase are necessary for the stated Fourier convention.

The delta function in the specified convolution kernel collapses the k′ integral to −iγG. Set λ=eγ and ω02​=k2+m2. Then
G(k;ω)=−ω2−2iλω+ω02​1​,G(k;τ)=∫−∞∞​2πdω​−ω2−2iλω+ω02​e−iωτ​.
(19)
There is a change of assumptions here: the specified kernel is the transform of V=−iγ, which is not real. These later parts define a formal analytic continuation to an effective damped equation, [∂τ2​+2λ∂τ​+ω02​]G=δ(τ); they cannot simultaneously describe the earlier real external potential. The following pole analysis uses that continued operator.
Completing the square gives D(ω)=−(ω+iλ)2+(ω02​−λ2), so
ω±​=−iλ±k2+m2−λ2​.​
(20)
For e>0, hence λ>0, the underdamped regime has two poles at ±Ω−iλ, with Ω=k2+m2−λ2​>0. Both lie below the real axis. In the overdamped regime they lie on the imaginary axis at −i(λ∓κ), where κ=λ2−k2−m2​<λ for k2+m2>0; both are again below the real axis. At equality they coalesce into a double pole at −iλ. If m<∣λ∣, the crossover occurs at kc​=λ2−m2​; increasing k moves the two imaginary poles together and then apart horizontally. If m>∣λ∣, every k is underdamped; if equal, only k=0 is critical.
The sign of e is not specified: for e<0 all nonzero-frequency poles are instead in the upper half-plane, describing antidamping; for e=0 they lie on the real axis and a causal prescription is required. When m=k=0, one pole is at zero even for nonzero λ and a real-axis prescription is again needed. The figure uses illustrative λ=1, not supplied numerical data.

Let τ=t−t′ and Ω=k2+m2−λ2​>0. First take λ=eγ>0. Since ∣e−iωτ∣=eτImω, close the contour below for τ>0, and above for τ<0. On the chosen semicircle the exponential is bounded and the denominator grows as R2, so the arc integral tends to zero. The upper contour encloses no pole, giving G=0 for t<t′. The lower contour is clockwise, hence the real-axis integral divided by 2π is −i times the sum of residues. Since D′(ω+​)=−2Ω and D′(ω−​)=2Ω, that sum is
−2Ωe−i(Ω−iλ)τ​+2Ωe−i(−Ω−iλ)τ​=ie−λτΩsin(Ωτ)​.
(21)
Thus
G(k;t,t′)=H(t−t′)e−λ(t−t′)Ωsin[Ω(t−t′)]​(λ>0).​
(22)
This is the causal Green function of a damped oscillator. Its value is continuous through τ=0, and its derivative jumps from zero to one; integrating the differential equation through zero verifies the delta-source normalization and the residue sign. The limits are H(τ)τe−λτ at critical damping and H(τ)e−λτsinh(κτ)/κ in the overdamped regime.
For completeness, if the unspecified charge has e<0, the same real-axis integral instead encloses upper poles for τ<0 and gives −H(−τ)e−λτsin(Ωτ)/Ω, an advanced Green function. A causal growing response would require an inverse-Laplace contour above those poles, not the stated real-axis Fourier integral. At e=0 the real-axis integral needs a pole prescription; shifting both poles below it gives the retarded zero-damping limit. These distinctions are necessary because γ>0 alone does not fix the sign of damping.

a OurBigBook.com
For the isotropic zero-field classical Heisenberg model of a ferromagnet, the analytic vector Landau expansion uses the Cartesian components of the vector order parameter near m=0. rotational symmetry makes its scalar terms functions of m⋅m. Global spin inversion symmetry also requires f(m)=f(−m). Through fourth order this gives
a=c=0,f=bm2+dm4,d>0.​
(23)
A positive quartic coefficient stabilizes the unconstrained truncated free energy. The disordered phase has b>0 and its minimum at m=0; the aligned phase has b<0 and a minimum at nonzero m. At the continuous transition b=0. The attractive alignment interaction supplies an energetic tendency toward negative quadratic curvature; orientational entropy favors the disordered phase at high temperature, so the sign of b is a free-energy question rather than a consequence of the pair energy alone.
The analyticity assumption matters: the variable printed as m is a nonnegative magnitude, so reversing m does not send that scalar to −m. Terms proportional to ∣m∣ or ∣m∣3 would be rotationally invariant but nonanalytic at the vector origin. Standard analytic Landau theory excludes them. Without that assumption, the stated pair symmetry alone would not exclude odd powers of the magnitude.[ref]

Differentiation gives f′(m)=2m(b+2dm2). For b>0 the origin is the global minimum; for b<0, m=−b/(2d)​ has f′′=−4b>0 and f=−b2/(4d)<f(0). Therefore, close enough to the transition for the quartic expansion to apply,
meq​(T)={(Tc​−T)/(2dTc​)​,0,​T<Tc​,T≥Tc​.​​
(24)
The order parameter approaches zero continuously with mean-field critical exponent β=1/2: the transition is continuous. In the aligned phase a particular direction is chosen. The vector-spin rotation symmetry is spontaneously broken from SO(3) to SO(2), or from O(3) to O(2) including reflections. The manifold of vector order parameter directions is a sphere, so this is not just a two-state sign choice.[ref]
The plot uses illustrative d=1/2 and temperature measured in units of Tc​. A unit-spin average must satisfy m≤1; if the quartic model is used literally far below the transition, its constrained minimum is min{1,(Tc​−T)/(2dTc​)​}. In practice the near-transition expansion is not a controlled prediction of deep low-temperature saturation. Also, a finite zero-field statistical ensemble that averages all directions has ⟨m⟩=0 even below Tc​; the curve describes the magnitude in a selected ordered state, obtained by appropriate boundary conditions or the thermodynamic limit before removing a small magnetic field.

The independent head-tail reversal symmetry means that the squared dot product interaction energy is unchanged when any individual si​ is replaced by −si​. All configurations si​=σi​n, with σi​=±1 independently, attain the aligned ground-state pair energy. There is consequently no energetic preference for either end of a molecular axis. Averaging over these equally weighted signs gives ⟨si​⟩=0 and ⟨m⟩=0 even in the nematically ordered phase. The surviving order is the unoriented axis, represented by a nematic director with n∼−n. A second-moment tensor can detect this order while a polar vector cannot.[ref]
The word “always” needs the statistical ensemble or apolar interpretation: it is not an identity for each finite configuration. For example, choosing all σi​=+1 is also a ground-state representative and gives m=n. In an unbiased sign statistical ensemble the typical residual polar vector has size of order N−1/2, because ⟨∣m∣2⟩=1/N for these independent ground-state signs. This does not make polar order an equilibrium nematic order parameter. The tensor below is unchanged by each sign flip and thus removes the arbitrary labeling of rod ends.

Choose a basis whose first axis is n. The tensor with uniaxial nematic order then has eigenvalues 2Q,−Q,−Q. Hence TrS2=6Q2, TrS3=6Q3 and TrS4=18Q4. Substitution gives
f(Q)=6aQ2+6bQ3+18cQ4.​
(26)
For a,c>0, Q=0 remains a local minimum with curvature 12a. Its nonzero stationary points satisfy 2a+3bQ+12cQ2=0, so
Q±​=24c−3b±9b2−96ac​​.
(27)
They first appear at bs​=−32ac/3​ as a stationary inflection point; for more negative b, Q−​ is a local maximum and Q+​ a local minimum. The latter is initially metastable, becomes degenerate with Q=0 at bt​=−12ac​, and then becomes the global minimum as b decreases further. This is a first-order transition in a cubic-quartic Landau potential: the order changes discontinuously. The saddle-node bifurcation creating a local minimum is not the coexistence condition. The isotropic minimum never loses local stability in this fixed positive-a scan.
The illustrative plots set a=c=1 and show f/6. A cubic invariant is permitted because head-tail reversal leaves S unchanged; it does not implement S↦−S. The physical range of a uniaxial nematic order parameter imposes −1/2≤Q≤1; the usual small-order-parameter Landau result assumes the displayed minimum lies in that interval. The unrestricted quartic graphs express the intended Landau model, with the effect of enforcing the bound stated in part (f).[ref]

a+bQt​+3cQt2​=0,2a+3bQt​+12cQt2​=0.

bt​=−12ac​=−23ac​,Qt​=3ca​​.​
(29)
As b is decreased through bt​, the equilibrium scalar order jumps from 0 for b>bt​ to Qt​ just on the b<bt​ side. At b=bt​ both values coexist; the stable aligned branch below it is Q+​ from part (e). As a global-minimum check, at coexistence
f(Q)=18cQ2(Q−Qt​)2≥0.
(30)
There are two equal minima and a barrier at Qt​/2, of height a2/(8c). Comparing these minima, rather than finding only the stationary-point discriminant, determines the transition.
This is the unrestricted Landau answer, compatible with the physical range of a uniaxial nematic order parameter when a≤3c so that Qt​≤1. If one instead enforces Q∈[−1/2,1] for arbitrary positive coefficients, negative Q cannot win for b≤0, and bounded quartic nematic coexistence minimizes a/Q+3cQ over 0<Q≤1. No coefficients were supplied to choose this boundary case; the near-transition Landau approximation normally uses the interior result.
