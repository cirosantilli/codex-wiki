Mean-field theory replaces fluctuating interactions with the average field produced by the other degrees of freedom. For an interacting spin system, write Si​=S+δSi​ and neglect connected products δSi​⋅δSj​. This reduces the many-body partition function to independent single-site factors coupled through a self-consistency equation for S.
The approximation is most reliable when many effectively independent neighbors contribute, for sufficiently long-range interactions, or above the upper critical dimension away from the fluctuation-dominated critical region. It usually becomes least reliable near a continuous transition in low dimensions, where correlations extend over long distances. It can estimate ordering scales and reveal candidate broken symmetries while giving incorrect critical exponents or even a spurious transition. The two-dimensional isotropic model in the later parts is an especially important example of this limitation.

Near zero the Langevin function has expansion
L(x)=3x​−45x3​+O(x5).
(73)
For positive x, L(x) rises toward one and its ratio L(x)/x decreases from 1/3 to zero. Therefore a line τx has only the zero intersection for τ≥1/3, and also a nonzero positive intersection for 0<τ<1/3. In a fixed signed component there is a corresponding negative solution; in the isotropic vector problem the nonzero solutions have all spin-space orientations.
The mean-field transition temperature is
kB​TcMF​=3zc​J​=34J​.​
(74)
At the critical temperature the line is tangent at the origin. Just below it, S≃(5/3)(1−T/TcMF​)​, the usual mean-field square-root onset. The ordered minimum is stable below this temperature while S=0 becomes unstable.
This is a mean-field prediction, not a genuine finite-temperature ordering transition for the ideal two-dimensional short-range isotropic model. Long-wavelength spin fluctuations invalidate the predicted nonzero thermodynamic magnetization at every T>0. The relevant obstruction is the Mermin-Wagner theorem for continuous spin symmetry; anisotropy, long-range interactions or a finite sample change the assumptions. Its original paper establishes the absence of finite-temperature magnetic ordering for one- and two-dimensional finite-range isotropic Heisenberg models.[ref]

For uniform unconstrained boundary conditions, the positive gradient coefficient favors a spatially constant minimizer. Put u=∣m∣2≥0. The remaining free-energy difference is a(T−Tc​)u+bu2. Its minimum is
⎩⎨⎧​m=0,m=m0​n^,m0​=2ba(Tc​−T)​​,∣n^∣=1,​T≥Tc​,T<Tc​.​​
(75)
Below Tc​ the amplitude is fixed but its direction is arbitrary; the set of uniform minima is a two-sphere. The free-energy minimum is f0​−a2(Tc​−T)2/(4b) below Tc​ and f0​ above. The order parameter vanishes continuously, so this is a continuous, second-order transition within Landau theory.
The free energy has internal O(3) spin-rotation/reflection symmetry; a chosen nonzero vector leaves an O(2) subgroup invariant. Restricting to proper rotations, this is SO(3)→SO(2) spontaneous symmetry breaking. There are two transverse orientation directions and a massive amplitude direction below the transition. Spatially varying directions cost gradient energy, and imposed boundaries or defects can prevent a uniform minimizer. In strictly two-dimensional equilibrium with short-range interactions, the fluctuation caveat of part (d) still applies: minimizing this polynomial functional at mean-field level does not overturn the absence of finite-temperature spontaneous magnetization.
