Gas thermometer (P∝T(N,V fixed))

 ... Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Temperature Thermometer
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 1
A gas thermometer infers temperature from the equation of state of a confined gas. The constant-volume version measures pressure; the constant-pressure version measures volume. For an ideal gas,
PV=NkB​T=nRT,T0​T​=P0​P​(N,V fixed),T0​T​=V0​V​(N,P fixed).
(81)
Here N is particle number, n is amount of substance, kB​ is the Boltzmann constant, and R is the molar gas constant. The reference and test states must be internally equilibrated, and T0​ must be known on the absolute thermodynamic temperature scale. A pressure ratio cancels the unknown gas inventory and bulb size in the ideal constant-volume limit. This cancellation is the method's appeal: a mechanical measurement transfers an absolute temperature scale between different materials. The Maxwell speed distribution and ideal gas pressure from wall collisions explain the microscopic origin of the response. [ref]
For an original ideal-model calculation, choose T0​=300K, P0​=120kPa and P=160kPa. Then T=400K. These are stipulated values, not observations. Both pressures must be absolute, referred to vacuum. If the surrounding atmospheric pressure were 100kPa, the corresponding gauge pressures would be 20 and 60kPa; their ratio is three, which would incorrectly predict 900K. A pressure difference from the atmosphere cannot be substituted into the ideal gas law.
A motivating calibration experiment. Schooley's account of the NBS/NIST work describes a helium-filled metal bulb, a capillary, a capacitance diaphragm gauge, and a mercury pressure standard. Circulating bath liquid passed the bulb assembly and platinum resistance thermometers. The resistance probes checked the surrounding temperature; gas pressure supplied the independent thermodynamic comparison. In an early series, workers filled the cleaned bulb with purified helium at the highest test temperature, checked pressure over three hours, then measured at lower temperatures and near a reference at 0∘C. Six filling pressures allowed extrapolation toward zero pressure. [ref]
To turn this principle into a readable measurement protocol, use the following controls. These steps are a proposed teaching interpretation of the cited apparatus, rather than additional reported NBS observations:
  • Define the inventory. Evacuate and clean the bulb and connecting tube, fill with a known pure gas, close the filling valve, and keep the same total particle number during each reference/test comparison. Monitor pressure at a maintained temperature before and after a run. A leak changes N; a slow drift can also reflect contamination, sorption, or imperfect equilibration, so pressure stability alone does not identify its cause.
  • Measure the reference. Place the bulb in a controlled environment with independently assigned T0​. Record P0​ after thermalization, together with the independent thermometer readings and their spread. A reference near 0∘C has an absolute temperature near 273.15K, rather than zero temperature. Repeating reference readings tests whether the inventory or apparatus changed during the comparison.
  • Measure the test state. Change the bath setting, allow the bulb and gas to settle, and record P only when the surrounding thermometers and pressure are sufficiently stable for the intended accuracy. Compare readings at different positions and vary the settling time. Record the connecting tube's temperature profile too: uniform bath temperature does not make the entire connecting tube isothermal.
  • Measure the volume changes. Determine the bulb's thermal expansion and its deformation from differences between inside and outside pressure. Measure the connecting tube's dimensions and the effective valve volume. “Constant volume” is the ideal model; a real vessel expands. These measurements supply corrections, even though the absolute bulb volume cancels in the simplest pressure ratio.
  • Measure pressure against a reference. For a liquid-column pressure standard, measure the vertical column height, the liquid's temperature-dependent mass density, local gravitational acceleration, and the reference-side pressure. Include diaphragm offsets and the gas's hydrostatic pressure change between the sensor and bulb. The pressure needed in the equation of state is at the bulb, not automatically at the remote gauge.
The corresponding NBS apparatus used a protective case with an outer helium layer held close to the bulb pressure, reducing pressure deformation while aiding thermal contact. Thermal expansion was measured on bulb material. Gas in the capillary remained partly outside the hot region, requiring a measured tube temperature profile and geometry. Pure working gas also mattered: contaminants that sorb on surfaces can change the freely circulating inventory. These are physical effects to measure or bound, rather than scatter that disappears by averaging. [ref]
For illustration, neglect connecting-tube gas and describe a uniform bulb with a dilute virial expansion in particle number density ρN​=N/V:
PV=NkB​T[1+B2​(T)ρN​+O(ρN2​)],T0​T​=P0​P​V(T0​)V(T)​Z(T)Z(T0​)​.
(82)
Here Z=PV/(NkB​T) and B2​ is the second virial coefficient in the particle-density convention; a molar-density convention rescales the coefficient. The second relation follows exactly from the definition of Z for the same N, and shows the signs of the corrections. If the bulb expands by 0.1% and Z=1 at both states, the toy pressure ratio above gives 400.4K, rather than 400K. Improved expansion and helium nonideality models really changed the temperatures inferred from the 1989 NIST data: Harvey's 2023 reanalysis found expansion updates of roughly 1–3mK and nonideality updates of roughly −0.1 to −0.5mK. Those are reported corrections to historical results, not the much larger illustrative correction just calculated. [ref]
With equal corrected bulb volumes, negligible or corrected connecting-tube gas, fixed reference and test temperatures, and a dilute classical single gas phase,
P0​P​=T0​T​[1+(B2​(T)−B2​(T0​))ρN​+O(ρN2​)],limρN​→0​P0​P​=T0​T​.
(83)
Repeat the reference/test comparison for several sealed fills, reducing N between comparisons but keeping it fixed within each pair. The extrapolation varies density at fixed endpoint temperatures; it does not cool one fill toward zero temperature. Real gases have different finite-density corrections but share the classical ideal gas intercept under these assumptions. The limit does not remove leaks, bulb expansion, gauge offsets, or the gas thermometer dead-space correction. At very low pressures, thermal transpiration can make remote sensor and bulb pressures differ through a nonisothermal capillary; this must be corrected too. The NBS work measured this pressure correction using a separate comparison of narrow and wider tubes. [ref]
At x=1, the toy models give Tapp​=407.92K and 388.12K, respectively, although their imposed true test temperature is the same. Their zero-density intercept is the reusable quantity. Measuring several low-density points permits a fit and a check for curvature; asserting a straight line from one finite-density point supplies neither. A practical fit must balance nonideality against increasing relative pressure error and the capillary corrections, and keep its extrapolation uncertainty.
