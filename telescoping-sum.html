Telescoping sum (∑k=1n​(ak​−ak−1​)=an​−a0​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Arithmetic Addition Summation
OurBigBook.com Words: 634 Articles: 3
A telescoping sum cancels intermediate sequence terms, leaving boundary terms. For a finite sequence of real numbers a0​,…,an​, the displayed identity follows because every a1​,…,an−1​ occurs once with each sign. Applying it to an infinite series also requires checking the limit of the boundary terms; finite cancellation alone does not prove that the series is a convergent series.[ref]
For n≥1, expanding the finite sum gives
∑k=1n​(ak​−ak−1​)=(a1​−a0​)+(a2​−a1​)+⋯+(an​−an−1​)=an​−a0​.
(13)
No monotone sequence assumption is required. Consider the original sequence 4,9,2,7,5. Its successive finite differences are 5,−7,5,−2, so
5−7+5−2=1=5−4.
(14)
The large intermediate rise and fall disappear from the net change. This is useful whenever one knows successive changes rather than every absolute value: adding the changes reconstructs the final value from the initial one. It also checks a cumulative-update calculation without requiring all increments to be positive.
A finite difference Δak−1​=ak​−ak−1​ measures one discrete change. The identity ∑k=1n​Δak−1​=an​−a0​ is the discrete counterpart of the fundamental theorem of calculus: accumulation of changes returns the endpoint difference. The related summation by parts transfers a finite difference from one factor of a product to another, with explicit boundary terms.
