Stellar radiation and nebular ionization. The planetary-nebula spectral energy distribution is constrained by a shared radiation and thermal balance. The central star's effective temperature, luminosity and evolutionary state set its continuum and ionizing-photon supply. A hotter star shifts the ionizing spectrum and allows higher ionization stages; the total recombination output also depends on how many ionizing photons the gas actually absorbs. In an ionization-bounded nebula, approximate photoionization balance is

whereas a density-bounded nebula can leak photons. Dust absorption of ionizing photons changes this relation further. Stellar-atmosphere and wind effects make a blackbody an approximation, especially in the extreme ultraviolet. A changing core temperature and expanding envelope explain why nebular spectra evolve.

Case-B line ratios assume appropriate trapping of Lyman-series photons and escape of the lower-series radiation. Density, optical-depth effects and extinction can alter observed ratios. The free-free emissivity has the schematic dependence jνff​∝ne​ni​Te−1/2​exp[−hν/(kB​Te​)]gff​; the nebular free-bound continuum also carries ionization thresholds and recombination cross-sections. Two-photon emission depends on the population of the hydrogen 2s state and can be reduced by collisional mixing into 2p. Density clumping matters because these rates are weighted approximately by density squared, not simply by total gas mass.
Collisionally excited lines. Below a line's critical density of an atomic transition,
jul​≃ne​n(Xi)qlu​(Te​)hνul​,qlu​∝Te−1/2​exp[−ΔE/(kB​Te​)].

Elemental abundance, ionic fraction and electron temperature therefore all enter. The ionizing spectrum and local ionization parameter help determine which ion is present. Above the critical density of an atomic transition, collisional de-excitation competes with radiative decay and can suppress a forbidden line. Low-energy infrared fine-structure lines are generally less exponentially temperature sensitive than high-excitation optical/ultraviolet lines. Metal-line cooling also helps set the temperature, so changing an abundance changes both the number of emitters and the thermal balance; line luminosity need not be proportional to abundance at fixed everything else.
Dust and molecules. Nebular dust spectral features identify the carriers as well as contributing to the integrated luminosity. In an optically thin dust component,
Fν​∝∫κν​Bν​(Td​)dMd​.
(38)
The grain mass sets its normalization, while the radiation field, stellar distance, grain size and absorption efficiency determine its temperature distribution. For approximately grey heating, radiative equilibrium suggests Td​∝L∗1/4​r−1/2; non-grey grains change this scaling. Long-wavelength opacity is often approximated as κν​∝νβd​. Small aromatic grains undergo transient heating, so one equilibrium temperature is not adequate for every infrared feature. Carbon-to-oxygen chemistry, grain processing and shielding affect aromatic versus silicate bands. Molecular line emission further depends on molecule abundance, collisional density, kinetic temperature, ultraviolet pumping and shocks, while shielding and dissociation set whether molecules survive at all.
Finally, interstellar extinction preferentially suppresses ultraviolet and optical light, and dust reradiates some absorbed energy in the infrared. Aperture, angular resolution and distance must be treated consistently before comparing stellar, nebular and dust components. These dependencies make the combined spectrum an energy and abundance diagnostic, but also prevent a unique reconstruction from a single broad-band colour.
