Inverse Compton scattering transfers energy from fast electrons to lower-energy photons. In the Thomson scattering limit the scattering is approximately elastic in the electron's instantaneous rest frame, but the transformations into and out of that frame can greatly increase the photon energy in the original frame.
In a blazar, relativistic jet electrons can scatter their own synchrotron radiation, producing synchrotron self-Compton radiation, or scatter photons from an accretion disk and its surrounding reprocessing material. The two spectral humps constrain electron Lorentz factors, the electron distribution and, with assumptions about geometry and relativistic beaming, the magnetic field. For the same electron population in the Thomson regime, the ratio of inverse-Compton to synchrotron power scales as uph​/uB​ in the plasma rest frame. It does not determine the field without information about the photon energy density and beaming.
In a galaxy cluster, hot thermal electrons scatter cosmic microwave background photons and slightly raise their mean energy, producing the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect. The spectral distortion measures the line-of-sight integral of electron pressure, approximately y=σT​∫ne​kB​Te​dℓ/(me​c2) for nonrelativistic thermal electrons. Combined with X-ray emission, which weights density approximately quadratically, it constrains the gas density, temperature and mass distribution. These are distinct source populations and seed photon fields from the blazar example.
For the power derivation, use an isotropic incident radiation field of total photon energy density uph​ in the laboratory frame. Its stress-energy tensor has T00=uph​ and Tzz=uph​/3, with zero mean flux. Boosting to an electron moving at βc along z gives
T′0z=−γ2β(T00+Tzz)=−34​γ2βuph​.
(1)
The radiation energy flux in this rest frame is Sz′​=cT′0z. Elastic Thomson scattering exerts the drag Fz′​=σT​Sz′​/c. The parallel three-force has the same value in the laboratory frame, so the electron's positive energy loss rate is
PIC​=−Fz​βc=34​σT​cuph​β2γ2.​
(2)
This isotropic inverse-Compton drag assumes photon energies small compared with me​c2 in the electron rest frame, negligible recoil and an isotropic seed field in the specified frame. An anisotropic photon field requires its directional moments; the recoil-dependent differential scattering cross-section of Compton scattering becomes necessary at larger rest-frame energies.
There are two conventions to qualify in the printed expression. First, the density multiplying 4/3 must be uph​, not literally only the magnetic part of a propagating electromagnetic field. That field has equal electric and magnetic energy densities, so uph​=2umag​ and the corresponding formula is PIC​=(8/3)σT​cumag​β2γ2. Second, this is net electron energy loss, rather than all incident plus scattered photon energy. At β=0 an electron still scatters power σT​cuph​, but transfers no kinetic energy to the photons, so the inverse-Compton gain is zero.
For an individual incident photon of energy ε, the electron-frame energy is ε′=γε(1−βcosθ). For a head-on photon followed by forward emission in the laboratory,
εout​≃γ2(1+β)2ε≃4γ2ε.
(3)
Averaging an isotropic seed field gives ⟨εout​⟩≃(4/3)γ2ε for ultrarelativistic electrons. The original plot has an inverse-Compton maximum of order 108eV, a synchrotron maximum around 10−1eV and a disk component around 1eV. Thus
γ∼4εseed​3εout​​​∼9×103 to 3×104.​
(4)
The head-on maximum gives the same order of magnitude, about 5×103 to 1.6×104. These are approximate readings of a broad spectrum, not precise fitted parameters. Disk and synchrotron seed photons need not be scattered by the same part of the electron distribution, and bulk jet Doppler factors also enter the observed peak relation. The indicated rest-frame seed energies remain well below the electron rest energy in this estimate.

A gravitational wave is a propagating tidal perturbation of spacetime. In the weak-field limit its transverse polarizations alternately stretch and compress the separation of freely falling test bodies. A laser interferometer such as LIGO measures the differential optical phase of orthogonal arms. The mirrors approximate free test masses over the detection band; arm cavities enhance the phase response, while suspension, seismic isolation and optical control reduce instrumental disturbances. A strain h produces relative arm-length changes of order hL. Coincidence and waveform consistency between detectors distinguish candidate astrophysical signals from local noise.
Potential sources include black-hole and neutron-star binary inspirals and mergers, asymmetric core-collapse supernovae, nonaxisymmetric spinning neutron stars, and a stochastic background from many unresolved sources or early-Universe processes. Their detectability depends on frequency, strain, duration and detector noise; a source producing gravitational radiation need not be observable in a terrestrial interferometer's band.

An H II region is photoionized interstellar gas around young massive stars. Its optical spectrum contains hydrogen and helium recombination lines, collisionally excited forbidden metal lines, and weaker free-free, free-bound and two-photon continua. Typical hot main-sequence ionizing stars have effective temperatures of a few 104K; their ultraviolet output, age and metallicity determine the ionization balance. Gas and dust can also emit strongly outside the visible band.
A planetary nebula is expelled evolved-star material illuminated by a much hotter exposed stellar core. Its central star can reach temperatures of order 105K, allowing higher ionization stages and prominent He II emission. The gas may carry nucleosynthetic enrichment from its progenitor, while expansion lowers density and changes line strengths with age. Both object classes produce nebular spectra, but their ionizing sources and evolutionary contexts differ; neither spectrum is simply the blackbody spectrum of the exciting star.

The main evidence combines a large luminosity with a compact source and a massive central gravitating object. Rapid continuum variability bounds the emitting size by a light-crossing scale, while broad emission lines and reverberation delays constrain the gas orbital speeds and characteristic radii. Their virial masses, together with stellar and gas dynamics in galactic nuclei, imply compact central masses far larger than ordinary stars. Thermal optical/ultraviolet disk emission, a high-energy coronal component, jets and relativistically broadened inner-disk features are naturally associated with an accretion flow. No one signature alone is a unique proof, but the combined compactness, mass and energy requirements strongly favor accretion onto supermassive black holes. Gravitational accretion releases a much larger fraction of rest energy than ordinary stellar burning, without requiring the luminosity to come from the black-hole interior.
For a fully ionized pure-hydrogen plasma, each electron receiving Thomson radiation force is coupled electrostatically to a proton carrying almost all the mass. With spherical isotropic luminosity L, the outward force per electron is σT​L/(4πr2c), while gravity per electron–proton pair is approximately GMmp​/r2. Equality defines the Eddington luminosity:
LEdd​=σT​4πGmp​cM​.​
(34)
Here G is the gravitational constant, mp​ the proton mass, c the speed of light, M the central mass and σT​ the Thomson cross-section. The estimate assumes electron-scattering opacity, tight electron–ion coupling and no other dominant force. Geometry, beaming, other opacities and photon trapping can change the relevant luminosity limit.

Black-hole spin changes the innermost stable orbit and can raise or lower the efficiency. A core-collapse supernova occurs when the core of a massive evolved star loses stable pressure support and collapses. Iron-group nuclei no longer provide an exothermic fusion route, while electron capture and photodisintegration reduce electron pressure and consume energy. Collapse accelerates until nuclear-density matter stiffens the inner core and launches a shock. That shock loses energy to dissociation and neutrinos and can stall. Neutrino heating, aided by multidimensional flow, can revive it; sufficiently rapid rotation and magnetic stresses provide other possible channels. Most of the liberated compact-object binding energy escapes as neutrinos rather than optical light or ejecta kinetic energy.
Successful explosions can leave neutron stars, with additional fallback sometimes producing black holes. Failed explosions or very substantial fallback can form black holes more directly. Important theoretical uncertainties include progenitor structure and mass loss, nuclear equation of state, neutrino transport and interaction rates, convection and shock instabilities, rotation, magnetic fields and the relation between these effects and explosion asymmetry. A successful shock launch alone does not guarantee unbinding all overlying layers.

The ejecta binding energy of an initially uniform core is the negative gravitational energy of the outer shells in the original core, including their interaction with the material inside them. It is not the final neutron star's entire binding energy. With mc​=Mc​/M⊙​=1.4 and d=ΔM/M⊙​, the shell integral gives
Ebind,ej​≃6×1043[(1.4+d)5/3−1.45/3]J.

The calculation assumes the initial common density for the shell binding, all the indicated outer iron encounters the dissociating shock, and dissociation is charged as the stated energy cost. It does not subtract the much larger final compact-object binding energy as though all that energy were directly available to the ejecta. Later recombination can return some nuclear binding energy, and overlying noniron layers add their own energy requirements, so the table is a simplified shock-energy budget.
Compared with a characteristic 1044J explosion, ejecting a very thin remaining core layer is relatively easy in this estimate; 0.1M⊙​ is already demanding, and 1M⊙​ requires substantially more energy. More massive tightly bound cores therefore tend to make shock revival and complete ejection harder, increasing the possibility of fallback or failed explosions and black-hole remnants. A thin steep-gradient outer layer may allow a rapid, relatively weak explosion and a surviving neutron star.
A natal kick requires asymmetry of ejecta, neutrino emission or subsequent gravitational interaction, not merely a large scalar energy budget. Rapid relatively symmetric low-ejecta explosions can produce small kicks; a shock that develops strong asymmetries can give larger neutron-star kicks, while direct collapse need not do so. A schematic momentum estimate is vkick​∼ϵasym​2Mej​Ekin​​/Mc​, but neither the asymmetry nor its direction is fixed by the three core masses. The source data support tendencies, not a unique mapping from iron-core mass to remnant kick.
