An additional atomic input is needed: the total Case B recombination coefficient does not specify the number of H-alpha photons per net radiative recombination. Let yα​=αHαeff​/αr​, where αHαeff​ is the effective recombination coefficient. At the approximately 104 K temperature implicit in the supplied αr​, take yα​≃0.45. The coefficient 2.2 used later in the paper corresponds to yα​=1/2.2≃0.455, which we use consistently here.
For an unextinguished, fully filled Strömgren sphere with negligible central cavity, the H-alpha luminosity and observed radiative flux satisfy
4πD2FHα​=yα​λHα​hc​αr​ne2​34πrS3​​,rS​=2θD​.
(3)
Substitution eliminates rS​ and gives
D=yα​hcαr​ne2​θ324FHα​λHα​​.​
(4)
Convert the angular diameter to radians: θ=56×10−3/206265=2.7150×10−7. Also hc/λHα​=3.0268×10−19 J. Thus
D=1.5422×1019 m≃500 pc,rS​≃2.09×1012 m≃14.0 au.​
(5)
The implied hydrogen-ionizing photon production rate is Q≃1.00×1039 s−1. Using yα​=0.45 instead gives D≃505 pc. These values use the printed milliarcseconds and integrated line observed radiative flux. Assuming one H-alpha photon per recombination would give a different, unjustified distance. An appreciable cavity or a nonunit filling factor must also enter the emitting volume.
The Brackett series consists of transitions ending at n=4, at infrared wavelengths. They suffer much less interstellar extinction than H-alpha, so their corrected observed radiative fluxes can give a more reliable version of the same inference. At fixed measured angular size and number density, the formula has D∝F: uncorrected interstellar extinction therefore makes this particular distance estimate too small. Infrared lines are weaker and need their own effective recombination coefficients; their advantage is reduced sensitivity to dust.
