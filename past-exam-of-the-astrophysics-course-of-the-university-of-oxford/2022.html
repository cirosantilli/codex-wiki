The emission coefficient jν​ is emitted energy per unit time, volume, solid angle and frequency, whereas the absorption coefficient αν​ has units of inverse length. (1)

Assume an isolated, bound, nonrotating Newtonian gas cloud, negligible surface pressure, negligible magnetic and radiation stresses, no persistent bulk flow, and a stationary or time-averaged virialized state. The stellar virial theorem then reduces to
3∫P(x)dV+Ω=0,

(18)

For a gas-pressure-supported stellar core of fixed mass and composition, neglect boundary terms for the illustrative whole-core estimate. The virial theorem gives E=U+Ω=−U, and the monatomic gas gives U=3Mc​kB​T/(2μmu​). Thus the effective heat capacity along the equilibrium sequence is
dTdE​=−2μmu​3Mc​kB​​<0.​
(22)
This negative heat capacity of a virialized gravitational system means that net energy loss makes a self-gravitating core contract and heat. It is different from the positive local material specific heat capacity. On the main sequence, hydrogen burning supplies most of the radiated energy, so rapid contraction is arrested. Hydrogen depletion increases the mean molecular weight; slow structural readjustment usually raises the central temperature rather than making it fall.
After core hydrogen exhaustion, a massive stellar core contracts and heats more rapidly until a later nuclear fuel ignites. There is no general reversal to cooling simply upon leaving the main sequence. If radiation pressure becomes dominant, the internal energy becomes U=Ugas​+Urad​ with 3∫PdV=2Ugas​+Urad​. The virial theorem therefore gives E=−Ugas​; the radiation-dominated limit is marginally bound and its effective adiabatic index approaches 4/3.
Quasistatic contraction can still maintain virial equilibrium. It fails dynamically only when sufficient support is lost, for example if pair creation reduces the effective adiabatic index below 4/3, or when an iron core undergoes electron capture and photodisintegration. A fixed-composition gas-dominated contracting core with T∝1/R is already a counterexample to any assertion that leaving the main sequence alone reverses the heating or destroys virial equilibrium.

Primordial density contrasts perturb the intrinsic photon temperature, the gravitational potential, and the velocity of the last-scattering material. Intrinsic photon-density fluctuations obey δT/T=δγ​/4 because ργ​∝T4; adiabatic initial conditions relate these to matter density contrasts. Subsequent acoustic evolution, Doppler effect and gravitational frequency shifts determine the observed Cosmic microwave background anisotropy. There is no scale-independent identification of the final sky map with matter density contrast alone.
The spherical harmonic coefficients are random variables inherited from these perturbations. After subtracting the mean sky temperature, ⟨alm​⟩=0 for the anisotropy modes. Gaussian primordial perturbations give approximately Gaussian random variables; this extra Gaussian assumption is not needed for diagonal covariance. Reality of the sky map implies al,−m​=(−1)malm∗​.
For a statistically isotropic sky, its two-point function depends only on n^⋅m^. Rotations mix the m components at fixed l and do not mix different irreducible l spaces. A rotation-invariant covariance matrix is therefore a multiple of the identity in each l block:
⟨alm​al′m′∗​⟩=Cl​δll′​δmm′​.​
(23)
On our one sky, average the 2l+1 angular components at fixed l, or equivalently suitable rotated sky pairs, rather than treating different universes as observed samples. The CMB angular power spectrum is estimated by
Cl​=2l+11​m=−l∑l​∣alm​∣2.​

The estimator is unbiased, ⟨Cl​⟩=Cl​, but does not turn one realization into the exact ensemble mean.

Diffusive shock acceleration is viable in principle at 511 GeV: repeated scattering off magnetic irregularities on opposite sides of a strong nonrelativistic shock wave can reach such energies. Actual viability requires a sufficiently large and long-lived accelerator with an acceleration time shorter than its escape and loss times; γ alone does not establish those conditions.
Take positive velocity toward the upstream material, and let the front move at +vs​ in the upstream rest frame. For a strong adiabatic shock wave in a gas with adiabatic index 5/3, the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions give ρd​/ρu​=4 and the shock-frame inflow speeds satisfy u1​=vs​, u2​=vs​/4. The signed velocities in the three frames are
rest frameupstreamdownstreamfront​vu​0−3vs​/4−vs​​vd​3vs​/40−vs​/4​vfront​vs​vs​/40​​
(31)
Thus the two scattering media converge at V=u1​−u2​=3vs​/4. For a crossing particle with incident energy E in its local plasma rest frame, a Lorentz transformation to the approaching other plasma gives
E′=ΓV​(E+Vpμ)=E[1+cV​μ+O(V2/c2)],

One explanation is an upper electron energy imposed by the accelerator: finite age, finite confinement size, escape, or a balance of acceleration and losses limits γ to γmax​. Since the synchrotron critical frequency scales as νc​∝Bγ2sinα, the absence of higher-energy electrons produces a corresponding high-frequency cutoff, broadened by the single-particle spectrum.

The Jeans equations express local momentum conservation for a collisionless stellar system, just as the Euler equations do for a gas. The mean streaming velocity plays the role of fluid flow, the gravitational force is a body force, and random stellar motions supply a pressure tensor through ρσij2​. Taking moments smooths over individual stellar orbits and produces fields of density, mean velocity and random-motion stress; this is the sense of “stellar hydrodynamics”.
Ordinary gas pressure is usually nearly isotropic because collisions relax its local velocity distribution. A collisionless stellar system can retain an anisotropic velocity ellipsoid, so its pressure tensor is not generally a scalar times the identity. There are more unknown second moments than momentum equations, and their evolution introduces higher moments. The Jeans equations therefore require further dynamical assumptions or a specified one-particle distribution function for closure; they do not by themselves establish local thermal equilibrium or a gas equation of state.
