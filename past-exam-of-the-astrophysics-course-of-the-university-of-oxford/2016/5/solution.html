An H II region is photoionized interstellar gas around young massive stars. Its optical spectrum contains hydrogen and helium recombination lines, collisionally excited forbidden metal lines, and weaker free-free, free-bound and two-photon continua. Typical hot main-sequence ionizing stars have effective temperatures of a few 104K; their ultraviolet output, age and metallicity determine the ionization balance. Gas and dust can also emit strongly outside the visible band.
A planetary nebula is expelled evolved-star material illuminated by a much hotter exposed stellar core. Its central star can reach temperatures of order 105K, allowing higher ionization stages and prominent He II emission. The gas may carry nucleosynthetic enrichment from its progenitor, while expansion lowers density and changes line strengths with age. Both object classes produce nebular spectra, but their ionizing sources and evolutionary contexts differ; neither spectrum is simply the blackbody spectrum of the exciting star.

If hydrogen and helium dominate the mass, this corresponds to helium mass fraction Y≃4y/(1+4y)≃0.379, with y=He/H. It is an approximate elemental estimate under the ionization assumptions, not merely a ratio of two observed line fluxes.
An ordinary H II region would typically have He/H∼0.08–0.10 by number, with metallicity-dependent enrichment, rather than necessarily the larger value inferred here for an evolved-star nebula. Hydrogen ionization requires 13.6eV, neutral helium 24.6eV and He+ 54.4eV. Typical H II region stars often produce little He++, so He II 4686 can be extremely weak or absent despite substantial helium. He I 4471 relative to Hβ can still be useful, but neutral helium inside the H+ zone may require an ionization correction. A very hot planetary-nebula core is more favorable for observing both ionic stages, although ionization structure must still be checked.
