a OurBigBook.com
A hydrogen recombination line is a discrete spectral line produced when radiative recombination captures an electron into an excited bound level and a subsequent radiative cascade emits photons. The free-to-bound capture itself produces a continuum; the later bound-to-bound transitions produce the recombination lines. For example, H-alpha is the n=3→2 transition in the Balmer series.
For the neutral hydrogen absorbing an ionizing stellar continuum, the principal bound-free transition is photoionization from the 1s ground state into the continuum. Most neutral hydrogen is in its ground state, and the photoionization cross section is largest near its 13.6 eV threshold, falling approximately as ν−3 above it. Dominance of the population-weighted opacity is intended here; this does not mean that every excited-state microscopic cross section is smaller at every frequency.
In an ionization-bounded, optically thick nebula, direct radiative recombination into 1s emits an ionizing photon that promptly photoionizes another hydrogen atom. One ion disappears and another is created: there is no net removal of an ion from the global budget. Case B recombination therefore uses αB​=∑n≥2​αn​, with local recycling of ground-state recombination photons. This is the on-the-spot approximation, rather than an unconditional property of radiative recombination: if those photons escape, direct capture does produce net neutralization and a Case A treatment is appropriate.

The main intrinsic parameters for old globular clusters are age, metallicity, helium abundance and the distribution of envelope masses after red giant branch mass loss. Distance and foreground interstellar extinction alter the appearance and must first be removed before assigning intrinsic differences.
Age controls the main-sequence turnoff: a more massive star has a shorter main-sequence lifetime, approximately fuel divided by luminosity. An older coeval population therefore has a smaller turnoff mass and a fainter, generally redder main-sequence turnoff. The separation in astronomical magnitude between the main-sequence turnoff and horizontal branch is useful because a common distance modulus cancels.
At comparable age, larger metallicity increases stellar-envelope opacity and atmospheric line blanketing. This generally makes the red giant branch and main sequence redder; metal-poor populations tend to have hotter, bluer red giant branches. Metallicity also affects hydrogen burning through the CNO cycle, so a quantitative age inference needs composition-dependent stellar models.
The horizontal branch is especially sensitive to envelope mass: at similar helium-core mass, a thinner hydrogen envelope gives a higher effective temperature and hence a bluer location. Red giant branch mass loss, age and helium abundance all influence that envelope and produce the familiar “second parameter” complexity. Enhanced helium also changes mean molecular weight, luminosity and evolutionary lifetime, and can shift main sequences and horizontal branches.
Interstellar extinction dims the diagram, while wavelength-dependent interstellar extinction reddens B−V; distance primarily supplies a vertical distance modulus shift. A systematic bend or different horizontal branch colour distribution cannot be explained by distance alone. On the main sequence, central hydrogen burning converts four protons into helium, positrons, neutrinos and released energy. The net reaction is 4p→4He+2e++2νe​, with positron annihilation contributing to the total roughly 26.7 MeV release before subtracting escaping neutrino energy. In the low-mass main-sequence stars of an old globular cluster, the proton–proton chain generally dominates. The CNO cycle makes the same net conversion and becomes important in hotter burning regions.
After central hydrogen exhaustion, a subgiant contracts its helium core and establishes a hydrogen-burning shell. On the red giant branch, that shell supplies most of the luminosity, commonly through the temperature-sensitive CNO cycle, while the inert helium core grows and becomes supported mainly by electron degeneracy pressure. The envelope expands and its surface cools. The helium core is not yet powered by helium nuclear fusion.
Helium ignition in a sufficiently low-mass degenerate core produces the helium flash, removing the strong electron degeneracy pressure dominance. A horizontal-branch star subsequently burns helium in its core through the Triple-alpha process, 34He→12C+γ, and through 12C(α,γ)16O. A surrounding hydrogen-burning shell can continue simultaneously.
After central helium exhaustion, the Asymptotic giant branch has an inert carbon-oxygen core with a helium-burning shell and an outer hydrogen-burning shell. Alternation between quiescent burning and helium-shell thermal pulses drives mixing and substantial mass loss. Subsequent post-asymptotic giant branch evolution exposes a hot core that may ionize its expelled envelope as a planetary nebula. Finally a white dwarf cools mainly by loss of stored thermal energy, rather than sustained central nuclear fusion.

For ordinary single stars in these old globular clusters, no current core-collapse supernovae are expected, and no new black holes are expected from the normal plotted population. Their main-sequence turnoff masses are typically around 0.8M⊙​, far below the roughly 8M⊙​ initial-mass scale for advanced burning and core collapse. More massive original stars exhausted their much shorter main-sequence lifetimes long ago. The precise transition mass depends on composition and mass loss, but this large separation makes the conclusion robust.
The low-mass stars now leaving the main sequence evolve through the red giant branch, helium ignition, horizontal branch and Asymptotic giant branch. They lose their envelopes and leave mainly carbon-oxygen white dwarfs, typically around 0.5–0.6M⊙​ for current old-cluster progenitors, supported by electron degeneracy pressure. A visible planetary nebula is possible if the exposed core heats before the ejecta disperse; it is not guaranteed for every low-mass remnant. Lower-mass main-sequence stars repeat this evolution only much later, and the very lowest-mass isolated stars can ultimately leave helium-rich degenerate remnants without igniting helium.
The clusters may already contain neutron stars and black holes made by their original massive stars; these remnants need not appear on an optical colour-magnitude diagram.
