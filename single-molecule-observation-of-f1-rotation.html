Single-molecule observation of F1 rotation

 Home Biology Enzyme ATP synthase
OurBigBook.com Words: 286
In 1997, Hiroyuki Noji and colleagues attached a fluorescent actin filament to the central γ shaft of isolated F1​. With ATP supplied, the marker made sustained rotations. This exposed rotation directly, but used ATP hydrolysis: there was no membrane F0​ motor producing ATP from a proton-motive force in this assay.[ref]
A subsequent primary experiment made the optical readout much faster. Purified Escherichia coli F1​ was immobilized on glass in a 30μm-deep flow cell, with a 60nm gold bead attached to γ. With 2mM ATP and magnesium supplied, laser illumination and dark-field microscopy recorded bead positions at 4000 or 8000 frames per second. The position trajectory resolved turns and intervening dwells. This apparatus follows a shaft-attached marker, rather than imaging the shaft's chemical atoms.[ref]
Controls addressed perturbation and chemical activity: comparison of 40–200nm probes found similar rates for the two smallest sizes, supporting low drag; a separate coupled-enzyme assay followed ATP hydrolysis through NADH absorbance. But the rotational analysis excluded pauses longer than 0.1s, so it characterizes continuously active intervals, not total output including long inhibited periods. It neither counts transported protons nor measures simultaneous single-molecule ATP synthesis.[ref]
The experimental distinction is essential. Sustained directional marker motion tests a rotary mechanism. Demonstrating proton-driven synthesis additionally requires intact F0​F1​ in a membrane with a controlled proton-motive force and a chemical ATP-production readout; dependence on that driving force and inhibition or respiratory uncoupling controls then test the proposed energy coupling. A rotation movie alone cannot establish the complete material and free energy ledger.
