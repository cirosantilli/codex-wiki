Catalyst

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Catalysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 10
A catalyst makes a chemical reaction proceed faster by participating in an alternative reaction mechanism and being regenerated. This makes repeated conversion possible: the same catalyst can help many successive reactants become reaction products. An enzyme is a biological catalyst. The overall standard change in Gibbs free energy is unchanged; see the IUPAC Gold Book: catalyst.
Regeneration does not mean that the catalyst sits unchanged throughout every step. For example, a schematic reaction mechanism can temporarily bind A to a catalyst C in a reaction intermediate AC:
A+CACnet:A​⇌AC,⇌B+C,⇌B.​
(31)
Adding the steps cancels both the reaction intermediate AC and the catalyst C from the net chemical equation. AC is produced and later consumed; C is used and later recovered. These letters describe possible roles, not a measured reaction mechanism. The alternative route is useful only if its chemical kinetics permits faster conversion. See Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, §12.7: catalysts and alternative mechanisms.
For a worked barrier example, consider an idealized reversible A-to-B conversion at fixed temperature T=300K. Stipulate standard molar Gibbs free energy levels GA∘​=0 and GB∘​=−20kJmol−1, with effective transition state levels 80 without a catalyst and 50kJmol−1 with a catalyst. Use the same standard states and the same zero for both routes; the catalytic route includes the regenerated C at both endpoints. Unlike the separate potential-energy profile example, this illustration uses Gibbs free energy at one specified temperature.
The Gibbs energy of activation is measured from the starting endpoint in the chosen direction. Thus the four barriers are
ΔGf,uncat‡∘​ΔGr,uncat‡∘​​=80−0=80,=80−(−20)=100,​ΔGf,cat‡∘​ΔGr,cat‡∘​​=50−0=50,=50−(−20)=70,​all in kJmol−1.
(32)
Both directional barriers fall by 30kJmol−1. The endpoint difference stays Δr​G∘=−20kJmol−1.
To turn those barriers into rate constants, use the Eyring form of transition state theory for this effective first-order reaction pair, with a common prefactor and unit probability of successful passage:
k=νexp(−RTΔG‡∘​),ν=hkB​T​.
(33)
Here kB​ is the Boltzmann constant, h is the Planck constant, and R is the molar gas constant. The common prefactor cancels from the comparisons. These Gibbs energies of activation are not the empirical activation energy Ea​ of the Arrhenius equation. With the stipulated value R=8.314462618Jmol−1K−1, the speedup is
kf,uncat​kf,cat​​=kr,uncat​kr,cat​​=exp(8.314462618×30030000​)≈1.67248×105.
(34)
The exponent is a dimensionless quantity: convert 30kJmol−1 to 30000Jmol−1 before dividing by RT. The exponential function explains why a modest barrier reduction can have a dramatic kinetic effect. This number follows from the chosen free-energy barriers and common prefactor; it is not a universal catalytic multiplier or a prediction across temperatures.
Assume ideal thermodynamic activities with a common concentration standard state, and directional rate laws Jf​=kf​[A] and Jr​=kr​[B]. At chemical equilibrium, equal reaction rates give K=[B]/[A]=kf​/kr​ for this model. The barriers give
Kuncat​=e−100000/(RT)e−80000/(RT)​=e20000/(RT)=e−70000/(RT)e−50000/(RT)​=Kcat​≈3035.58.
(35)
The catalyst speeds up both directions, so it changes how quickly chemical equilibrium is approached without changing this equilibrium constant. For a general chemical reaction, the thermodynamic relation is K=exp[−Δr​G∘/(RT)] with consistent standard states; an arbitrary multistep reaction mechanism need not permit K=kf​/kr​ for two measured rate constants. At the same temperature, pressure, and thermodynamic activities, a catalyst also leaves the actual overall reaction change in Gibbs free energy unchanged. See 13.3 Shifting Equilibria: Le Châtelier’s Principle (§13.3: a catalyst does not shift equilibrium). To drive an unfavourable overall conversion, a process such as energy coupling must change the combined transformation.
