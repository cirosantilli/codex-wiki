First law of thermodynamics (dU=δQ−δW)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 7
The first law applies conservation of energy to a thermodynamic system. For a closed system with unchanged bulk kinetic energy and potential energy, write
dU=δQ−δW,ΔU=Q−W.
(1)
Here U is internal energy, Q>0 is heat entering the system and W>0 is work done by the system on its surroundings. Compression therefore gives negative W. Some references define work positive when done on the system; their plus sign represents the same balance with a different convention. If bulk kinetic energy or potential energy changes, include it in the total energy balance. [ref]
Supplying 1000J of heat while a gas does 300J of work increases its internal energy by 700J. Compressing it with 200J of input work in an adiabatic process instead gives Q=0, W=−200J and ΔU=200J.
The distinction between d and δ records state versus path: internal energy is a state function, whereas heat and work depend on the process. For an ideal gas expanding reversibly at constant temperature, W=NkB​Tlog(V2​/V1​) and Q=W, since internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature. Choose NkB​T=1000J and V2​=2V1​: then Q=W=693.15J. An insulated free expansion into vacuum between the same equilibrium endpoints has Q=W=0. Both have ΔU=0, yet their transfers differ. Their common entropy increase is NkB​log2. [ref]
For a simple compressible equilibrium system of fixed composition, combining the laws gives
dU=TdS−pdV.
(2)
Here p is pressure and V is volume. This identity relates neighboring equilibrium states. Identifying TdS with actual heat and pdV with actual work requires a suitable reversible thermodynamic process; an irreversible piston calculation uses the external resisting pressure for boundary work. This distinction prevents applying a reversible ideal-gas adiabat to an irreversible adiabatic piston compression.

Heat-flow reversal along a thermodynamic path (δQ=dU+pdV)

Words: 50
First law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
For a quasistatic simple compressible system with only pressure-volume work, the direction of heat transfer is determined by dU+pdV. A reversal occurs where this vanishes and changes sign. It need not coincide with a maximum or minimum of temperature, because the substance can still do work when its internal energy is stationary.

Adiabatic process

Words: 2k Articles: 5
First law of thermodynamics OurBigBook.com
An adiabatic process has no heat transfer across the chosen boundary throughout the process: δQ=0, rather than merely zero net heat after incoming and outgoing transfers cancel. It is a condition on energy transfer, whereas an isothermal process holds temperature constant. A sealed cylinder can approach an adiabatic process through insulation or through motion short compared with the time needed for appreciable heat transfer. Its temperature can nevertheless change because work crosses the boundary.[ref]
Take the trapped gas as a closed system, with fixed amount of substance and composition and no net change in bulk kinetic energy or potential energy between the endpoints. Define Qin​>0 as heat entering and Wout​>0 as work delivered to the surroundings. The first law of thermodynamics gives
ΔU=Qin​−Wout​,Qin​=0 ⟹ ΔU=−Wout​.
(3)
Here U is internal energy. An adiabatic compression normally supplies work, so Wout​<0; an adiabatic expansion against a resisting load delivers work, so Wout​>0. An adiabatic process need not preserve internal energy and need not make the closed system an isolated system. [ref]
For a reversible thermodynamic process, dS=δQrev​/T at positive absolute temperature T. A reversible adiabatic process therefore has dS=0: it is an isentropic process. Irreversible adiabatic processes can instead generate entropy; free expansion is the existing counterexample, with its calculation in entropy change in free expansion. Conversely, constant entropy alone does not certify an adiabatic process or reversibility: outward heat can export the entropy generated internally. For a closed system the macroscopic entropy balance is
ΔS=∫Tb​δQin​​+Sgen​,Sgen​≥0,
(4)
where Tb​ is the absolute boundary temperature where each heat transfer occurs and Sgen​ is internal entropy production. With no heat transfer, this reduces to ΔS=Sgen​; reversibility makes Sgen​=0. [ref]
A motivating experiment measures the gas temperature directly during compression and expansion, instead of inferring it from how warm a cylinder feels. PASCO's authored TD-8565 manual describes a sealed piston cylinder, a fine nickel-wire thermometer, an absolute pressure sensor and a position readout for volume. A computer records all three channels together. The wire's electrical resistance changes with temperature; its small size permits a rapid response. [ref]
Use ordinary trapped air and the apparatus's designed travel stops; mount the base securely, close the valve during a stroke, and record the actual volume limits. John Hart's university demonstration uses a large transparent cylinder with a low-heat capacity thermocouple, a pressure sensor, a travel gauge and a safety stop. It explicitly keeps the release valve closed during the demonstration. The basic observable is a rise in temperature on compression and a fall on loaded expansion, alongside the corresponding pressure and volume changes. Hart discusses wall thermal conduction, sensor response and leakage as reasons real records differ from the reversible model. [ref]
For a proposed rapid-versus-slow comparison, let the air and walls return to the same initial thermal equilibrium before each run, then compare the same starting and finishing volume values with different stroke durations. Read temperature, absolute pressure and position continuously, including the hold after motion stops. A rapid compression gives a temporary temperature rise; holding the smaller volume lets heat leave and the temperature approach the surroundings again. Slow compression in good thermal contact allows that heat transfer during the stroke and approaches an isothermal compression. Loaded expansion reverses the signs: the rapid stroke cools, while heat entering during slow motion reduces the cooling. PASCO explicitly supports rapid nearly adiabatic processes and slow isothermal processes; the matched-run controls here are a proposed extension, not a reported set of measurements. [ref]
Insulation suppresses thermal conduction; it does not prove that heat transfer vanishes. As proposed controls, repeat the matched strokes with and without a removable insulating jacket, record room and wall temperatures, measure fixed-position drift, and check valve closure and seal leakage. Calibrate the thermometer and position readout independently, including any connected dead volume; account for the heat capacity of the probe rather than treating it as a passive observer. Hart explicitly identifies the small air sample relative to the probe and walls, sensor inertia and seal leakage as limitations. A fast display cannot eliminate those physical effects. [ref]
There are two distinct timing requirements. Let τeq​ describe internal equilibration, τprobe​ the thermometer response, tmove​ the stroke duration and τheat​ the effective heat transfer time constant. A useful sufficient window for a resolved, nearly reversible adiabatic process is
τeq​,τprobe​≪tmove​≪τheat​,
(5)
with negligible friction. The left inequality permits a quasistatic process and a usable readout; the right limits heat leakage. A violent stroke may satisfy only the right inequality. PASCO reports a roughly 30–50ms lag of its temperature channel behind the other channels, so apparent simultaneous samples require care. If no such window exists, analyze equilibrium endpoints with the first law of thermodynamics, and regard the path as approximate. [ref]
The diagram's model is deliberately a hold after the stroke, when boundary work is zero. For constant whole-sample heat capacity at constant volume CV​ and effective thermal conductance K>0, it assumes
CV​dtdT​=−K(T−Tb​),τheat​=KCV​​,T(t)−Tb​=[T(0)−Tb​]e−t/τheat​.
(6)
This is Newton's law of cooling with one uniform gas temperature and a fixed surrounding temperature; actual wall and probe dynamics can require more than one time constant. At t/τheat​=0.1, the fraction of the initial temperature anomaly erased is 1−e−0.1≃0.0952; at one time constant it is about 0.6321. These are analytical fractions, not experimental tolerances or evidence that the preceding stroke was reversible. Measuring the hold helps estimate heat leakage independently of the motion.
The usefulness of an adiabatic process is that it separates mechanical work from heat transfer. Its reversible limit connects temperature changes, mechanical stiffness and entropy in one model: Rüchardt experiment and Clément-Desormes experiment already use that connection to measure heat capacity ratio. The contrast with free expansion explains the deeper point: suppressing heat does not determine how much work is exchanged or how much entropy is produced.

Adiabat

Words: 46
Adiabatic process OurBigBook.com
An adiabat is a curve representing a quasistatic adiabatic change. In a reversible description it is also a curve of constant entropy. For an ideal gas with constant heat-capacity ratio γ, it obeys pVγ=constant. A general irreversible adiabatic process need not follow this equilibrium-state curve and can produce entropy.

Adiabatic compression

Words: 433
Adiabatic process OurBigBook.com
Adiabatic compression decreases the volume of a sample without heat transfer across its boundary. For the fixed-composition ideal gas and endpoint assumptions in adiabatic process, compression work increases internal energy; positive heat capacity at constant volume then means a higher final temperature. The effect comes from supplied work, even though no heat enters. The amount of temperature increase depends on the work actually delivered to the gas, not on the adiabatic process condition alone.
The reversible ideal-gas adiabat supplies a particularly useful quantitative benchmark. Assume a fixed amount of substance n, a quasistatic process, negligible friction, only pressure-volume work, and constant heat capacity ratio γ=Cp​/CV​>1. Here Cp​ and CV​ are the whole sample's heat capacity at constant pressure and heat capacity at constant volume. With R the molar gas constant, Mayer's relation gives CV​=nR/(γ−1). The proof outline is CV​dT=−pdV from the first law of thermodynamics, followed by p=nRT/V from the ideal gas law and integration. The result is
pVγ=constant,TVγ−1=constant.
(7)
These finite power laws belong to this reversible, constant-γ ideal gas model. If heat capacity ratio varies, use the integral relation in heat capacity ratio; if the compression is irreversible, use the actual work balance, as in irreversible adiabatic piston compression. A short stroke by itself justifies neither power law. [ref]
Writing r=V1​/V2​>1 for the compression ratio, the reversible model predicts
T1​T2​​=rγ−1,p1​p2​​=rγ,Win​=−Wout​=CV​(T2​−T1​)=γ−1p1​V1​​(rγ−1−1).
(8)
All temperatures in these ratios are absolute. The larger pressure rise than in an isothermal compression, where p2​/p1​=r, explains the greater local stiffness described by isentropic compressibility.
For an original stipulated example, set γ=7/5, p1​V1​=nRT1​=1kJ and r=2. Then T2​/T1​=20.4≃1.31951 and p2​/p1​=21.4≃2.63902. The input work and internal energy increase are both 0.79877kJ, while Qin​=0. No initial temperature or apparatus performance is implied by the chosen energy scale. Independent integration gives the signed outward work
Wout​=∫V1​V2​​pdV=1−γp2​V2​−p1​V1​​,
(9)
which is negative during compression and agrees with −ΔU. This integral uses equilibrium gas pressure only because the path is reversible.
In the sensor experiment of adiabatic process, compare the pressure-volume integral with CV​ΔT as an energy check only when internal pressure is sufficiently uniform and piston dynamics permit that boundary-work estimate. Account for friction separately before comparing it with hand input work. PASCO's manual supplies a compression procedure and an illustrative logarithmic-slope result 1.39, compared with the model value 1.40 for a diatomic gas; that is its sample result, not new measurements here. [ref]

Adiabatic expansion

Words: 294
Adiabatic process OurBigBook.com
Adiabatic expansion increases the accessible volume without heat transfer. In a loaded piston expansion of the fixed-composition ideal gas considered in adiabatic process, positive outward work comes from internal energy, so the final temperature decreases. This provides a cooling mechanism without removing heat during the stroke. In the Carnot cycle, reversible adiabatic expansion connects two different temperatures between its isothermal processes.[ref]
For the same constant-γ reversible ideal-gas adiabat, put b=V2​/V1​>1. The scaling and first law of thermodynamics give
T1​T2​​=b1−γ,p1​p2​​=b−γ,Wout​=CV​(T1​−T2​)=γ−1p1​V1​​(1−b1−γ)>0.
(10)
With the figure's stipulated γ=1.4, p1​V1​=1kJ and b=2, the final ratios are T2​/T1​≃0.75786 and p2​/p1​≃0.37893. Outward work is 0.60535kJ and ΔU=−0.60535kJ. Reversing this particular path from its cooled endpoint would require the same work input; the figure's separate compression starts at the original state, so its input is different. Endpoint entropy supplies another independent check:
ΔS=CV​lnT1​T2​​+nRlnV1​V2​​=0.
(11)
The two logarithm terms cancel using CV​(γ−1)=nR, confirming the reversible path is an isentropic process. [ref]
PASCO's expansion demonstration first compresses trapped air and waits for thermal equilibrium before the rapid expansion. Its manual warns that friction-warmed cylinder regions contact the gas during expansion, compromising quantitative comparison. [ref] The hold separates cooling caused by expansion from simply undoing the earlier hot compression. As proposed controls, observe the temperature dip and subsequent warming, compare rapid and slow strokes from matched initial states, and assess wall heat transfer and probe lag before fitting a reversible model.
Cooling is not a universal consequence of adiabatic expansion: free expansion of an ideal gas has no outward work and returns to the same equilibrium temperature. Its entropy increases, so the reversible power laws do not describe it. Reuse free expansion, Joule's free-expansion experiment and temperature sensitivity of a free-expansion experiment for that apparatus, its energy budget and the real-gas qualifications. The useful distinction is the load: expansion cools this ideal gas when it must spend internal energy doing work.

Isentropic process

Words: 18
Adiabatic process OurBigBook.com
An isentropic process keeps the entropy constant. Every reversible adiabatic process is isentropic, while an irreversible adiabatic process can increase entropy.
