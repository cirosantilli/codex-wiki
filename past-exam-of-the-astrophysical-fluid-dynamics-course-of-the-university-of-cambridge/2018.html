Take positive Q˙​ to mean net removal of thermal energy per unit mass. The isobaric thermal stability of a heated ideal gas depends on the density change accompanying a temperature disturbance. For an ideal gas of fixed composition, p=ρkB​T/mˉ, so constant pressure gives ρT=constant. Therefore the net cooling can be written
L(T)=CTα−1−H,
(1)
where C>0 is constant for this pressure. At thermal equilibrium T=T0​, CT0α−1​=H.
The first law of thermodynamics at constant pressure includes the expansion work, so the relevant heat capacity is the specific heat capacity at constant pressure, cp​, rather than the constant-volume value. Linearizing its temperature equation gives
cp​dtdδT​=−(∂T∂L​)p​δT=−T0​(α−1)H​δT.
(2)
Hence
δT∝est,s=cp​T0​(1−α)H​.​
(3)
For optically thin Bremsstrahlung, α=1/2, so
s=2cp​T0​H​>0:the equilibrium is thermally unstable.​
(4)
A hotter parcel expands and becomes less dense. Its cooling per unit mass, proportional to T−1/2 at fixed pressure, decreases while its cosmic ray heating stays fixed, making it hotter still. A colder, denser parcel cools faster and runs away in the opposite direction. Here H has units Wkg−1 and cp​T0​ units Jkg−1, so s is an inverse time. The conclusion assumes the disturbance remains close to pressure equilibrium and neglects heat conduction and changes of composition. The relevant α is the temperature exponent in the supplied cooling law.

Use the nonrelativistic moving-conductor Ohm law in the form
E=σj​−u×B.
(5)
The Maxwell equations give Faraday's law of induction, ∂t​B=−∇×E. For a nonmagnetic conductor with constant permeability μ0​, the quasistatic Ampere-Maxwell law gives j≃(∇×B)/μ0​. This step neglects displacement current, requiring it to be small compared with conduction current; nonrelativistic velocity alone is not the complete justification.
For spatially uniform scalar electrical conductivity σ,
∂t​B=∇×(u×B)−μ0​σ1​∇×(∇×B).
(6)
Apply the identity ∇×(∇×B)=∇(∇⋅B)−∇2B and Gauss's law for magnetism, ∇⋅B=0. The resistive induction equation is
∂t∂B​≃∇×(u×B)+η∇2B,η=μ0​σ1​.​
(7)
The first term transports and deforms the magnetic field with the moving fluid; the second diffuses it. The magnetic diffusivity η has units m2s−1, so both terms have units tesla per second. No assumption of incompressible flow was needed. If σ varies spatially, the conductivity-gradient term in magnetic induction, −∇η×(∇×B), must also be retained.

For a plane-parallel slab, all equilibrium quantities depend only on height z. The vertical hydrostatic equilibrium equation and the Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity are
dzdp​=−ρdzdψ​,dz2d2ψ​=4πGρ.
(8)
With the polytropic equation of state p=Kρ2 and constant K,
2Kρdzdρ​=−ρdzdψ​.
(9)
In the gas interior, where ρ>0, divide by ρ and differentiate once:
2Kdz2d2ρ​=−dz2d2ψ​=−4πGρ.
(10)
Consequently
dz2d2ρ​+K2πG​ρ=0.​
(11)
The coefficient 2πG/K has units inverse square metres. The index-one plane-parallel polytrope illustrates the resulting structure: symmetry about z=0 gives ρ(z)=ρc​cos(kz), with k=2πG/K​. A slab bounded by vacuum ends at its first zero, ∣z∣=π/(2k); the negative continuation of the cosine is not physical mass density.

Supersonic communication. A point source moving at speed V>cs​ outruns its small acoustic waves. After a time t, the source has moved Vt, while a disturbance emitted at its old position has radius cs​t. The envelope of these spherical wavefronts is the Mach cone, with half-angle μ satisfying
sinμ=Vcs​​=M1​,M=cs​V​>1.​
(12)
Information is restricted to the downstream cone, so upstream gas cannot smoothly anticipate a supersonic obstacle. Finite compressions can also steepen because their characteristics converge. Such disturbances form a shock wave, across which the fluid variables change rapidly. A perfectly uniform supersonic flow need not contain a shock: the cone expresses the communication constraint, while a disturbance or compression supplies the reason for a discontinuity.
Conserved fluxes. Work in the shock frame, take velocities normal to the front, and integrate the conservation laws through a thin steady control volume of unit area. Mass conservation gives constant flux j=ρu, so
ρ2​u2​=ρ1​u1​=j.​
(13)
The normal momentum flux is the advected part ρu2 plus the pressure stress p. With negligible body-force impulse across the thin front,
p2​+ρ2​u22​=p1​+ρ1​u12​.​
(14)
For an ideal gas with constant adiabatic index γ, the specific internal energy is e=p/[(γ−1)ρ]. The energy flux is
j(e+21​u2)+pu=j(hsp​+21​u2),hsp​=e+ρp​=(γ−1)ργp​,
(15)
where hsp​ is the specific enthalpy. No radiation or other heat flux escapes in this adiabatic shock model. Conservation of the energy flux, divided by the nonzero mass flux, gives
(γ−1)ρ2​γp2​​+21​u22​=(γ−1)ρ1​γp1​​+21​u12​.​
(16)
These are the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas. The shock is adiabatic but irreversible: its entropy increases, so an isentropic relation cannot be imposed across the discontinuity.
Strong-shock compression. Put χ=ρ2​/ρ1​=u1​/u2​ and neglect p1​ and the upstream enthalpy compared with ρ1​u12​ and u12​. Momentum conservation gives p2​=ρ1​u12​(1−1/χ). Substitute this into energy conservation:
21​=γ−1γ​χ2χ−1​+2χ21​.
(17)
Thus
χ2−1=γ−12γ​(χ−1),(χ−1)(χ+1−γ−12γ​)=0.
(18)
The root χ=1 describes no shock. The compressive branch of the Strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions is
ρ1​ρ2​​=u2​u1​​=γ−1γ+1​.​
(19)
It also gives p2​=2ρ1​u12​/(γ+1). A monatomic gas has γ=5/3, hence χ=4.
The finite slab in its initial rest frame. Denote the shock's lab speed by Vs​. The upstream gas is initially stationary, so u1​=Vs​ and the downstream shock-frame speed is u2​=Vs​/4. Transforming to the initial slab frame gives
v2​=Vs​−u2​=43​Vs​,L2​=4L​.​
(20)
This is planar shock compression of a finite slab. In the uniform post-shock approximation, breakout occurs at tb​=L/Vs​. The original front face has then moved (3Vs​/4)tb​=3L/4, while the shock reaches the originally stationary far face at L, leaving thickness L/4. Equivalently, the fixed mass per area ρL becomes 4ρL2​. The result assumes a maintained approximately constant shock speed and no important boundary rarefaction during the crossing; an unconfined finite cloud need not remain uniformly compressed.
Escape estimate and the sound-speed convention. Let Mc​ be the slab mass, W>0 its binding-energy magnitude in the star's field, and Eth​ its initial internal energy. Treat the potential as nearly uniform across the slab. For the bulk-ballistic escape estimate, require
Ebulk​=21​Mc​v22​=329​Mc​Vs2​≳W,Eth​=23​ρMc​p​.
(21)
The Mach-number convention in shock unbinding estimates matters here. With the usual upstream adiabatic sound speed, cad2​=γp/ρ=5p/(3ρ), define Mad​=Vs​/cad​. Then
Eth​Ebulk​​=165​Mad2​,Mad2​≳516​Eth​W​.​
(22)
If instead the shock speed is normalized by the isothermal sound speed, cT2​=p/ρ, then MT2​=(5/3)Mad2​ and
MT2​≳316​Eth​W​≃5.3Eth​W​.​
(23)
The original PDF's “around five” is consistent with the latter convention or an order-of-magnitude sound-speed estimate. It is not the exact coefficient obtained with the conventional adiabatic Mach number and the stated bulk kinetic-energy criterion. Here W=−Egrav​ if the potential is zero at infinity; using negative gravitational energy directly on the right would be incorrect. Subsequent conversion of shock-generated internal energy to expansion, external pressure, or radiative losses would change the physical escape threshold, so this numerical factor is not universal.

Following a parcel. Let X(t) be a fluid pathline, with X˙=u(X,t). The multivariable chain rule gives the material derivative,
DtDQ​=dtd​Q(X(t),t)=∂t∂Q​+u⋅∇Q.​
(24)
The first term measures change at fixed position; the advection term accounts for the parcel moving through spatial gradients. The derivative is also called the Lagrangian derivative.
The forces on a parcel. A material volume dV has mass ρdV. The pressure forces on its boundary sum to −∇pdV, while the gravitational acceleration is −∇ψ. Newton's second law therefore gives the Euler equations for an inviscid fluid,
∂t​u+(u⋅∇)u=−ρ1​∇p−∇ψ.​
(25)
This neglects viscosity and other body forces. The left side is the parcel's acceleration, not just the time derivative at a fixed spatial location.
Bernoulli conservation. The claimed streamline result needs steady flow and a barotropic fluid, p=p(ρ), or an equivalent enthalpy relation along each isentropic streamline. These hypotheses are necessary; the unsteady Euler equation alone does not imply the stated conservation law. Define the barotropic enthalpy function
hb​(p)=∫pref​p​ρ(p′)dp′​,∇hb​=ρ∇p​.
(26)
Dot the steady Euler equation with u, using u⋅[(u⋅∇)u]=u⋅∇(u2/2). This gives
u⋅∇(21​u2+hb​+ψ)=0.
(27)
Thus the Bernoulli function is
H=21​u2+∫ρdp​+ψ=constant along each streamline.​
(28)
Every term has units of energy per mass, m2s−2. Different streamlines can have different constants.
The spherical accretion equation. Write the inward speed as u(r)>0, so the velocity vector is −u(r)r and the positive mass accretion rate is
M˙=4πr2ρu,drdlnρ​=−r2​−drdlnu​.
(29)
Assume a prescribed central potential ψ=−GM/r and negligible gas self-gravity. The steady radial Euler equation is
udrdu​=−ρ1​drdp​−r2GM​.
(30)
For a barotropic equation of state, dp/dr=cs2​dρ/dr, with cs2​=dp/dρ. Eliminate the density derivative to obtain
(u2−cs2​)drdlnu​=r2cs2​​−r2GM​=r2cs2​​(1−2cs2​rGM​).​
(31)
A smooth sonic point requires the right side to vanish where u=cs​, hence
rs​=2cs​(rs​)2GM​,u(rs​)=cs​(rs​).​
(32)
For a nonisothermal gas this is an implicit relation involving the local sound speed. In the isothermal case cs​ is constant and this is the Bondi sonic point.
The isothermal integral. For p=cs2​ρ, choose the enthalpy reference so that hb​=cs2​ln(ρ/ρ∞​). The reservoir is at rest at infinity and ψ(∞)=0, making its Bernoulli constant zero. Thus
21​u2+cs2​lnρ∞​ρ​−rGM​=0,
(33)
or
u2=2cs2​lnρρ∞​​+r2GM​.​
(34)
The logarithm has a dimensionless argument. Constant temperature during compression generally requires heat removal, so this is Isothermal Bondi accretion, rather than adiabatic accretion with γ>1.
The critical accretion rate. Select the smooth reservoir-connected transonic branch. Evaluate the last relation at us​=cs​ and GM/rs​=2cs2​:
cs2​=2cs2​lnρs​ρ∞​​+4cs2​,ρs​=e3/2ρ∞​.​
(35)
Mass conservation then gives the isothermal Bondi accretion rate,
M˙=4πrs2​ρs​cs​=πe3/2cs3​G2M2ρ∞​​.​
(36)
The units are kgs−1. Equivalently M˙=4πλG2M2ρ∞​/cs3​, with λ=e3/2/4≃1.12.
The inner-boundary selection of isothermal Bondi flow is important: a standard absorbing accretor inside the sonic radius selects this critical solution. The steady equations and ρ∞​ alone also admit subcritical subsonic branches supported by a different inner boundary; they do not by themselves fix a unique rate. The usual answer presumes transonic capture.

The background and the Jeans prescription. For a resting fluid, u0​=0 and continuity is automatic. The remaining hydrostatic equilibrium equations are
∇p0​=−ρ0​∇ψ0​,∇2ψ0​=4πGρ0​.​
(37)
An infinite uniform static medium with constant p0​ and ρ0​>0 would require ∇ψ0​=0 by force balance, but then its Laplacian would vanish, contradicting the Poisson equation. An isolated static uniform background is therefore not an exact equilibrium of this system.
The Jeans swindle suppresses the gravitational field of the uniform background and retains only the field sourced by perturbations. Formally it replaces the source by ρ−ρ0​ in the perturbation problem. This can be regarded as a specified compensating background or a local idealization; it does not prove that the original infinite isolated medium can be static.
Linearized equations. Put δρ=Δρ, δp=Δp, δψ=Δψ, and v=Δu. For uniform ρ0​,p0​, neglect products of perturbations. Continuity gives
∂t​δρ+ρ0​∇⋅v=0.​
(38)
The advective acceleration is quadratic, and the background pressure gradient vanishes, so the Euler equation becomes
∂t​v=−ρ0​1​∇δp−∇δψ.​
(39)
With the Jeans prescription and constant sound speed, the gravitational and pressure closures are
∇2δψ=4πGδρ,δp=cs2​δρ.​
(40)
The density correction to 1/ρ multiplying ∇δp is second order and is consistently omitted.
The longitudinal dispersion relation. Differentiate continuity in time and substitute the divergence of the linearized Euler equation:
∂t2​δρ=∇2δp+ρ0​∇2δψ=cs2​∇2δρ+4πGρ0​δρ.
(41)
For a plane wave proportional to ei(k⋅x−ωt), ∂t2​↦−ω2 and ∇2↦−k2. Therefore
ω2=cs2​k2−4πGρ0​=cs2​(k2−kJ2​),kJ​=cs​4πGρ0​​​.​
(42)
The physical Jeans length is
ℓJ​=kJ​2π​=cs​Gρ0​π​​.​
(43)
Since Gρ0​ has units inverse square seconds, kJ​ has units m−1 and ℓJ​ metres. This is the compressive density branch of the Jeans instability; transverse velocity disturbances have no pressure or gravity restoring force in this static nonrotating model and are neutral at linear order.
Which scales grow. For 0<k<kJ​, or wavelength ℓ>ℓJ​, ω2<0 and one temporal solution grows exponentially. The static Jeans growth-rate spectrum is
s(k)=4πGρ0​−cs2​k2​,δρ∝es(k)t.​
(44)
Its derivative is negative for 0<k<kJ​, so longer wavelengths grow faster. The supremum is s→4πGρ0​​ as k→0+: there is no selected finite fastest wavelength in this infinite-medium calculation. The exactly uniform k=0 density change is not an ordinary finite-potential Fourier perturbation with the fixed background subtraction. In a finite cloud, boundaries set the longest allowed scale.
For k>kJ​, the density perturbation oscillates as a stable acoustic wave. At k=kJ​ the linear equation is ∂t2​δρ=0, so the mode is marginal rather than exponentially growing.
Comparing acoustic and gravitational times. A sound-crossing estimate for a region of size ℓ is tsound​∼ℓ/cs​. Self-gravitational acceleration scales as Gρ0​ℓ, so collapse over a distance ℓ takes tgrav​∼(Gρ0​)−1/2. Equating these scales gives
ℓ∼Gρ0​​cs​​,
(45)
which agrees with the Jeans length up to its numerical factor π​. This is the physical competition between pressure communication and gravitational contraction.
The constants can be checked using the free-fall time of a uniform sphere. For a pressureless shell initially at radius a, enclosing fixed mass Ma​, energy conservation gives r˙2=2GMa​(1/r−1/a). Thus
tff​=∫0a​2GMa​(1/r−1/a)​dr​=22​π​GMa​a3​​=32Gρ0​3π​​,
(46)
where Ma​=4πρ0​a3/3. Consequently the Jeans length and gravitational time conventions satisfy
cs​ℓJ​​=Gρ0​π​​=332​​tff​≃3.27tff​.​
(47)
They agree as characteristic scales, not as identical exact times. Alternatively, using the length kJ−1​ gives 1/(cs​kJ​)=1/4πGρ0​​, exactly the gravitational inverse frequency entering the dispersion relation. The actual growth time of a marginal Jeans mode is infinite because pressure and gravity cancel there; it should not be confused with a pressureless free-fall time.
