Define the dimensionless right-hand side of part (d) by F(κ). Then ω2=−4πGρF(κ). Because I0′​(κ)=I1​(κ)>0 and I0​(κ)>0 for κ>0, its sign is the sign of I0​(κ)K0​(κ)−1/2. The original plot is positive for sufficiently long wavelengths and negative at short wavelengths; the nonzero crossing is approximately κc​≃1.07. Numerically solving the displayed equation gives κc​≃1.0667.
For 0<κ<κc​, ω2<0. Write ω=iσ with σ=4πGρF​>0. The time factor e−iωt then contains an exponentially growing solution. For κ>κc​, ω2>0 and the ideal inviscid modes oscillate stably, without damping. At the crossing the frequency is zero and the mode is marginal. In terms of the axial wavelength λz​=2π/k,
λz​>κc​2πR​≃5.89Rλz​<5.89R​:gravitationally unstable,:oscillatory and stable.​​

The growth rate tends to zero as kR→0+, so the very longest wavelengths are not the fastest growing. The source plot has its maximum near kR≃0.58, corresponding to λz​≃11R and σ≃0.254πGρ​. Thus a characteristic fragmentation spacing is several cylinder diameters; a quantitative nonlinear fragment mass is beyond this linear stability calculation.
For unstable modes the physical displacement must be interpreted as Re[ϵei(kz−ωt)] with complex amplitudes handled consistently throughout. The paper's real-part notation is harmless for the squared dispersion relation, but it should not be read as imposing real ω in a gravitational instability calculation. The exactly uniform mode k=0 is excluded from the finite-k derivation; the limit of the curve alone does not establish a separate volume-preserving uniform-radius mode.
