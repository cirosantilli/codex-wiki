Interpret “reach” first as remaining an energetic, supersonic blast wave, rather than as the arrival of any acoustic wave. An atmospheric blast-wave range estimate must account for the thin atmosphere: the distance D=8.5×106 m is much greater than its height H=104 m.
For an optimistic estimate, suppose the deposited energy E remains in a horizontally expanding atmospheric layer. If its representative mass density is ρa​, the swept mass is of order πD2Hρa​. Conservation of energy gives the self-similar blast wave scaling
E∼πρa​HD2U2,U(D)∼(πρa​HD2E​)1/2.
(7)
Using ordinary atmospheric estimates ρa​∼1 kgm−3 and speed of sound ca​∼300 ms−1, this gives
πD2Hρa​≃2.3×1018 kg,U(D)∼66 ms−1,U/ca​∼0.22.
(8)
These are scaling estimates, not an exact shock wave solution. The strong shock wave approximation would already have failed at
Ds​∼πρa​Hca2​E​​≃1.9×106 m.
(9)
Equivalently, keeping the disturbance strongly supersonic out to D would require substantially more than the sonic-transition energy scale
Es​∼πρa​HD2ca2​≃2.0×1023 J,
(10)
which exceeds 1022 J by about a factor of twenty. Reasonable atmospheric averages and order-unity geometrical coefficients do not reverse the comparison. Escape of energy through the top of the atmosphere makes the fully confined estimate optimistic; Earth's curvature changes its area estimate by an order-unity factor.
The strong atmospheric blast will not survive to Lansing in this model. A weakened acoustic wave can nevertheless travel there. The supplied energy and atmospheric height do not determine its detectable amplitude, detailed attenuation or damage: those require an atmospheric propagation model. Thus “no disturbance of any kind reaches Lansing” would be an unjustified stronger conclusion.
