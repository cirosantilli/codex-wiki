The first argument mixes a material fluid element with a fixed spatial volume. For a material volume element following the fluid, its volume is generally time dependent:
DtD​δV=(∇⋅u)δV.
(1)
Therefore the material derivative cannot be taken outside the product with δV as in the proposed cancellation. Instead, mass conservation states
DtD​(ρδV)=0,DtDρ​=−ρ∇⋅u.
(2)
The momentum balance for a material fluid element, using Newton's second law, is consequently
DtD​(ρδVu)=(ρδV)DtDu​=−∇pδV,ρDtDu​=−∇p.​
(3)
For a fixed control volume, fluid crosses its boundary and carries momentum; a momentum flux term must be included. Its local conservative form is ∂t​(ρu)+∇⋅(ρu⊗u)=−∇p, equivalent to the displayed Euler equations after using the continuity equation. The erroneous equation omits the term ρu∇⋅u needed to convert a material derivative of momentum density into this conservative form.
For the second task, take a homogeneous fluid with constant mass density ρ, prescribed gravitational acceleration g, incompressible flow and inviscid flow. Its equations are
DtDu​=−∇(ρp​)+g,∇⋅u=0.
(4)
Under the replacement ρ↦ρ​=Cρ, the same velocity solves these equations if p↦p​=Cp, up to a spatially constant pressure offset. This is density scaling invariance of incompressible Euler flow:
The velocity field alone cannot determine the uniform fluid density.​
(5)
The comparison assumes the same geometry and compatible velocity or gravitational free surface conditions; an independently specified nonuniform boundary pressure would supply additional information. The gravitational field is taken as prescribed. If the fluid's own self-gravity must be included, its field changes with mass density, so the invariance need not hold. A single freely moving particle's density-independent gravitational acceleration does not justify neglecting that distinction.

A highly conducting solar wind carries the Sun's magnetic field outward by magnetic flux freezing. This can open magnetic field lines that would form closed loops in a static dipole model. Beyond an opening radius ro​, approximate the field as radial. Constancy of the magnetic flux in a fixed solid-angle flux tube gives the radial solar-wind magnetic flux law
Br​(r)r2≃constant,Br​∝r−2,
(6)
which decays more slowly than the vacuum magnetic dipole field, proportional to r−3.
For example, if the field becomes radial already near the solar radius R⊙​, normalizing both estimates to the same field there gives
Bdip​(rE​)Bwind​(rE​)​∼R⊙​rE​​=0.0051​=200.
(7)
Thus a dipole estimate 10−11 T becomes of order 2×10−9 T, comparable to the stated hundredfold enhancement. More generally, matching dipolar decay inside ro​ to radial decay outside gives
Bdip​(rE​)Bwind​(rE​)​∼ro​rE​​;ro​∼2R⊙​ ⇒ B(rE​)∼10−9 T.​
(8)
This is a plausible scaling explanation, not a determination of the actual opening radius. The magnetic field need not be outward everywhere: opposite-polarity sectors can preserve zero net magnetic flux through a closed surface, consistent with Gauss's law for magnetism. The argument concerns the changed geometry of the field in a flowing plasma, rather than changing the dipole exponent by assumption.

a OurBigBook.com
A shock wave forms when a compressive flow steepens or when gas must be decelerated or turned faster than upstream acoustic waves can communicate the change. Astrophysical examples include a supernova remnant sweeping up surrounding gas, a fast stellar wind colliding with ambient material, and supersonic accretion onto an obstacle or onto a dense gas layer. A large Mach number by itself is not sufficient: a uniform supersonic stream need not contain a shock wave.
Choose the shock frame, and denote upstream and downstream normal velocities by u1​,u2​. Integrating the local conservation laws over a thin control volume spanning a steady normal shock wave gives equal fluxes on both sides. Mass conservation gives constant mass flux per unit area,
j=ρ1​u1​=ρ2​u2​.​
(13)
Pressure and advected momentum together give the normal momentum flux,
p1​+ρ1​u12​=p2​+ρ2​u22​.​
(14)
For a thin non-radiating layer, with negligible gravitational-potential change across it, conservation of energy gives equal fluxes j(h+u2/2). A perfect gas has specific enthalpy h=γp/[(γ−1)ρ], so
γ−1γ​ρ1​p1​​+21​u12​=γ−1γ​ρ2​p2​​+21​u22​.​
(15)
These are the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas. Dissipation inside the thin layer produces an entropy increase and selects the physical compressive branch. Efficient radiative cooling would change the energy relation and may instead lead to an isothermal shock.

Let U1​,U2​ denote the total flow speeds, keeping the normal components distinct from them. The original diagram measures β from the initial wall direction to the shock wave itself. The outgoing velocity is turned by θ, so it makes angle β−θ with the shock.
Resolve each velocity tangent and normal to the oblique shock. Tangential momentum conservation, with nonzero mass flux and no tangential surface force, makes the tangential velocity continuous:
U1​cosβ=U2​cos(β−θ).
(20)
The normal flow obeys the strong normal shock wave result from part (b):
U2​sin(β−θ)=γ+1γ−1​U1​sinβ.
(21)
Divide the second relation by the first to obtain
tanβ=γ−1γ+1​tan(β−θ).​
(22)
The approximation is controlled by the upstream normal Mach number Msinβ≫1, not just by M≫1. This distinction matters on the small-angle weak branch in part (d). The angle convention is also important: formulas using the angle to the shock normal instead would interchange sine and cosine.

Rewrite the relation from part (c) as
(γ−1)sinβcos(β−θ)=(γ+1)cosβsin(β−θ).
(23)
The angle-addition identities reduce this to
sin(2β−θ)=γsinθ.
(24)
For 0<θ<arcsin(1/γ), the right side lies strictly between zero and one. There are two physically admissible strong oblique-shock angle branches:
βweak​βstrong​​=21​[θ+arcsin(γsinθ)],=21​[θ+π−arcsin(γsinθ)].​​
(25)
Both lie between θ and π/2. They merge when γsinθ=1, proving the maximum deflection through a strong perfect-gas shock,
θmax​=arcsin(1/γ),β∗​=4π​+2θmax​​.​
(26)
For small positive θ, the formal strong-shock-limit asymptotics are
βweak​∼2γ+1​θ,βstrong​∼2π​−2γ−1​θ.​
(27)
The weak branch is normally observed for an unconfined supersonic stream turning through a modest compression corner. It has a smaller normal component of upstream velocity, hence a smaller downstream pressure increase; for small turns the downstream flow remains supersonic. The nearly normal strong branch can be selected by suitable downstream pressure conditions, so it is not mathematically forbidden.
The two limits must be ordered honestly. At finite M, the weak oblique shock approaches the Mach angle arcsin(1/M) as θ→0, rather than zero. The displayed weak-branch asymptotic applies when the large-M limit is taken first, or in the overlapping range 1/M≪θ≪1, where Msinβ≫1 remains valid.

Let M(r) be the enclosed mass. For spherical symmetry, hydrostatic equilibrium and mass conservation give
drdp​=−r2GM(r)ρ(r)​,drdM​=4πr2ρ.
(28)
The isothermal equation of state p=Kρ therefore implies
Kdrdlnρ​=−r2GM(r)​.
(29)
Multiply by r2, differentiate and eliminate M:
r21​drd​(r2drdlnρ​)=−K4πG​ρ,​
(30)
or equivalently r−2d[r2ρ−1ρ′]/dr=−4πGρ/K. This is the density equation for an isothermal sphere. Here K has units m2s−2 and equals the squared isothermal sound speed.
A regular isothermal sphere does not reach zero density at a finite radius. The finite body discussed later must therefore be truncated by a nonzero external pressure, as in a pressure-confined isothermal sphere. A finite vacuum boundary would not be compatible with this equation of state.

Along a family of equilibria at fixed temperature, K and the dimensionless function Ψ are fixed. The boundary mass density changes through both ρc​ and the boundary coordinate ξ:
δρ=e−Ψδρc​−ρc​e−ΨΨ′δξ=ρ(ρc​δρc​​−Ψ′δξ).​

0=NδN​=−21​ρc​δρc​​+ff′​δξ.
(39)
The isothermal Lane-Emden equation gives f′=ξ2e−Ψ, so the fixed-N relation is
ρc​δρc​​=Ψ′2e−Ψ​δξ.​
(40)
Substitute back into the boundary-density variation:
δρ​N​=ρ(Ψ′2e−Ψ​−Ψ′)δξ.
(41)
For a nontrivial variation at a finite positive radius, its vanishing gives the fixed-mass turning point of an isothermal sphere:
[Ψ′(ξ)]2=2e−Ψ(ξ).​
(42)
This derivative follows a fixed-mass sequence of equilibria; it is not merely the spatial derivative of density inside one sphere. The corresponding radial variation is
rδr​=(ξ1​−Ψ′e−Ψ​)δξ.
(43)
At small ξ, Ψ′≃ξ/3: the boundary density increases while the radius decreases along increasing ξ, giving the stated negative (∂p/∂V)N,T​ of the stable branch. The first density turning point marks its first loss of radial stability; it need not be confused with a turning point of radius itself.

Using the supplied first critical value ξc​≃6.5, the critical radius of a pressure-confined isothermal sphere is
rc​=6.54πGρc​K​​≃1.83Gρc​​cs​​,cs2​=K.​
(44)
No radius in metres can be specified without K and a central mass density. Here cs​ is the isothermal sound speed, consistent with the fixed-temperature variations in part (d). At a given central mass density, equilibria truncated at ξ<ξc​ lie on the first stable branch, while increasing the dimensionless outer radius beyond this first threshold gives an unstable configuration. Numerical integration gives ξc​≃6.451, consistent with the paper's rounded 6.5.
For a transparent comparison with the given Jeans length, set the uniform comparison density ρ0​=ρc​ and use the same speed of sound. Then
λJ​rc​​=2π6.5​≃1.03.​
(45)
Both scales express the competition between pressure support and self-gravity, with characteristic length cs​/Gρ​. Their numerical closeness under this density choice does not mean that the models impose the same threshold: rc​ is a boundary radius, whereas λJ​ is the wavelength of a perturbation. Comparing rc​ with λJ​/2 would instead give a factor about 2.07. Using the sphere's mean or boundary density instead of its central density changes the coefficient again, so the reference density must always be stated.
The pressure-confined isothermal sphere gives an actual finite, centrally concentrated hydrostatic equilibrium and an explicit confining pressure, making its radial stability threshold more self-consistent for a roughly spherical isothermal cloud. The uniform Jeans instability model gives a simpler local criterion, includes perturbations at different wavelengths, and does not require constructing a spherical equilibrium. Its ideal uniform static infinite background requires the Jeans swindle or an explicitly specified treatment of background gravity. Neither model includes rotation, magnetic fields, turbulent support or a realistic temperature evolution, so neither is a universal collapse criterion.

For a steady axisymmetric flow u0​=rΩ(r)ϕ​, incompressible flow is automatic. The only material acceleration is centripetal, −rΩ2r. The radial Euler equations therefore require
drdp0​​=ρrΩ(r)2.​
(46)
With no viscosity there is no tangential shear stress to redistribute angular momentum. Any sufficiently regular Ω(r) can therefore be a steady inviscid profile, with the radial pressure adjusted by integrating this equation. This is a statement of equilibrium, not of stability: some such profiles are centrifugally unstable.

For a homogeneous Newtonian fluid with constant kinematic viscosity ν>0, assume the same steady purely azimuthal flow. In cylindrical coordinates, the azimuthal Navier-Stokes equation has no pressure forcing or convective term in this state, and becomes
0=ν[dr2d2vϕ​​+r1​drdvϕ​​−r2vϕ​​],vϕ​=rΩ.
(47)
Equivalently, Ω′′+3Ω′/r=0, or (r3Ω′)′=0. Two integrations give the steady circular Couette profile,
Ω(r)=A+r2B​,vϕ​(r)=Ar+rB​.​
(48)
This is the basic Taylor-Couette flow between rotating cylinders. The constants depend on boundary angular velocities; for radii R<​,R>​ and prescribed angular velocities Ω<​,Ω>​,
A=R>2​−R<2​Ω>​R>2​−Ω<​R<2​​,B=R>2​−R<2​R<2​R>2​(Ω<​−Ω>​)​.
(49)
If the domain includes r=0, regularity requires B=0, leaving solid-body rotation. On an annulus a nonzero B is allowed and wall torques maintain the shear.
The restriction concerns a steady profile. An arbitrary initial angular velocity does not cease to exist when viscosity is introduced; within the purely azimuthal ansatz it evolves according to ∂t​Ω=ν(Ω′′+3Ω′/r) until compatible boundary conditions and angular-momentum constraints determine its long-time state.

Let ur​ be a nonzero admissible mode, multiply the equation of part (d) by its complex conjugate and integrate between the cylinders. The endpoint conditions remove the boundary term in integration by parts, giving the Rayleigh stability integral identity
∫∣ur′​∣2dr+ω2k2​∫Φ∣ur​∣2dr=−k2∫∣ur​∣2dr.
(60)
Rearrange to obtain a real Rayleigh quotient:
ω2=−∫∣ur′​∣2dr+k2∫∣ur​∣2drk2∫Φ(r)∣ur​∣2dr​.​
(61)
The denominator is strictly positive. Consequently, with the paper's convention,
Φ(r)<0 throughout the gap guarantees oscillatory normal-mode stability.​
(62)
The weak inequality Φ≤0 excludes exponentially growing axisymmetric modes, but permits marginal cases and possible algebraic growth when the restoring effect vanishes. Strict negativity avoids that degeneracy. Equivalently, the conventional Rayleigh discriminant is positive, or the squared specific angular momentum increases outward:
drd​(r2Ω)2>0.​
(63)
This is the Rayleigh centrifugal stability criterion. In the suggested time-independent Schrödinger equation analogy, a growing mode has ω2<0; if Φ≤0, its effective potential k2Φ/ω2 is nonnegative. The nonnegative kinetic term and that potential cannot have the required negative eigenvalue −k2, which gives the same exclusion.
As a sign check, for solid-body rotation κep2​=4Ω2 and Φ=−4Ω2. In a gap of width d=R>​−R<​, the radial modes ur​∝sin[nπ(r−R<​)/d] have
ωn2​=k2+(nπ/d)24Ω2k2​>0,
(64)
as expected for inertial waves. The argument establishes axisymmetric inviscid stability within the stated thin-gap model, rather than a claim about all perturbations of a viscous flow.
