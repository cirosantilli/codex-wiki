An accretion disk forms when infalling material has enough angular momentum to orbit before it can reach the central object. In a thin disc dominated by a central mass M, the orbital motion is approximately Keplerian rotation:
ΩK​=R3GM​​,ℓ=R2ΩK​=GMR​.
(17)
Vertical hydrostatic equilibrium gives the disk scale height H∼cs​/ΩK​; a geometrically thin disc has H/R≪1. The surface density is Σ=∫ρdz, and a steady inward mass flow rate is M˙=−2πRΣvR​>0.
Moving inward requires losing specific angular momentum. Internal stresses transport angular momentum outward; much of the mass can then accrete while an outer fraction spreads to larger radius. Molecular viscosity alone is generally inadequate for rapid astrophysical accretion. Turbulent and magnetic stresses, including those generated by the magnetorotational instability, provide effective transport. A common parametrization is ν=αcs​H for an effective kinematic viscosity, with α a dimensionless stress parameter.
For a thin Keplerian accretion disk, the evolution equation is
∂t∂Σ​=R3​∂R∂​[R1/2∂R∂​(νΣR1/2)].
(18)
This viscous evolution of an accretion disk occurs on the viscous timescale tν​∼R2/ν, much longer than an orbital period in a thin disc with moderate effective stress.
The orbital specific energy is −GM/(2R), so inward motion releases binding energy. In a steady, optically thick, radiatively efficient disc with zero torque at its inner edge, the emergent flux from each face is
F(R)=σSB​Teff4​=8πR33GMM˙​[1−(RRin​​)1/2].​
(19)
The Stefan–Boltzmann law identifies the local effective temperature. Far from the inner boundary, Teff​∝R−3/4, so the emitted spectrum combines hotter inner and cooler outer annuli. Integrating both faces gives Ldisc​=GMM˙/(2Rin​) under these assumptions. This is not a universal spectrum or efficiency: optical depth, inner torque, energy advection and outflows can change the radiation. The essential connection is outward angular-momentum transport, inward accretion, and release of binding energy.
