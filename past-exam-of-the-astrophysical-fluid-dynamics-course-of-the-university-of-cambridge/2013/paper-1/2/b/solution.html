A geometrically thin accretion disk around a central mass M has nearly Keplerian orbits, with Ω2≃GM/r3. Its vertical hydrostatic equilibrium gives H∼cs​/Ω≪r. The specific angular momentum ℓ=r2Ω∝r1/2 decreases inward, so accreting matter must transfer angular momentum to other matter. A viscous stress couples neighboring annuli rotating at different angular velocities.
Writing ν for effective kinematic viscosity and Σ for surface mass density, the outward viscous torque in an accretion disk is
G=−2πr3νΣdrdΩ​>0.
(9)
Steady mass conservation defines M˙=−2πrΣur​>0. If the inner radius rin​ has negligible applied torque, angular momentum conservation gives G=M˙(ℓ−ℓin​), hence
νΣ=3πM˙​[1−(rrin​​)1/2].​
(10)
Well outside that boundary, ur​≃−3ν/(2r) and the inward drift time is of order r2/ν, much longer than an orbital period in a thin disk with modest effective stress. Dissipation of orbital energy heats the disk. If that heat escapes locally from two faces, the radiative flux from either face is
F(r)=8πr33GMM˙​[1−(rrin​​)1/2].
(11)
Integration over both faces gives L=GMM˙/(2rin​) in this Newtonian zero-torque model. Material can lose angular momentum while the disk's outer parts spread outward: outward transport does not imply that most of its mass moves outward. In real ionized disks, magnetorotational instability and magnetic stresses often provide the effective transport; molecular viscosity alone is generally insufficient.
