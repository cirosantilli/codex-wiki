A fluid particle follows a Lagrangian trajectory, so its position satisfies x˙=u(x,t) rather than the velocity evaluated permanently at its initial position. Assume ω=0, and write
x(t)=X0​+u0​a(t)+u02​b(t)+O(u03​),a(0)=b(0)=0.
(1)
Set α=kX0​ and θ=α−ωt. A Taylor expansion of the velocity about X0​ gives
u0​a˙+u02​b˙=u0​sinθ+ku02​acosθ+O(u03​).
(2)
Thus a˙=sinθ, and
a(t)=ωcosθ−cosα​.
(3)
At the next order,
b˙=ωk​(cos2θ−cosαcosθ),
(4)
so the required second-order trajectory in a sinusoidal velocity field is
x(t)=​X0​+ωu0​​(cosθ−cosα)+ωku02​​[2t​+4ωsin2α−sin2θ​−ωcosα(sinα−sinθ)​]+O(u03​).​​
(5)
Both corrections vanish at t=0. All retained terms have units of length. The secular contribution gives the Stokes drift
x˙=2ωku02​​+O(u03​),​
(6)
although the Eulerian time average of the given velocity is zero. The particle samples the wave at its displaced position, producing this second-order drift. The expansion is in the dimensionless parameter ∣ku0​/ω∣≪1, for fixed times; it ceases to be uniform when the accumulated drift changes the wave phase by order unity.

Interpret “reach” first as remaining an energetic, supersonic blast wave, rather than as the arrival of any acoustic wave. An atmospheric blast-wave range estimate must account for the thin atmosphere: the distance D=8.5×106 m is much greater than its height H=104 m.
For an optimistic estimate, suppose the deposited energy E remains in a horizontally expanding atmospheric layer. If its representative mass density is ρa​, the swept mass is of order πD2Hρa​. Conservation of energy gives the self-similar blast wave scaling
E∼πρa​HD2U2,U(D)∼(πρa​HD2E​)1/2.
(7)
Using ordinary atmospheric estimates ρa​∼1 kgm−3 and speed of sound ca​∼300 ms−1, this gives
πD2Hρa​≃2.3×1018 kg,U(D)∼66 ms−1,U/ca​∼0.22.
(8)
These are scaling estimates, not an exact shock wave solution. The strong shock wave approximation would already have failed at
Ds​∼πρa​Hca2​E​​≃1.9×106 m.
(9)
Equivalently, keeping the disturbance strongly supersonic out to D would require substantially more than the sonic-transition energy scale
Es​∼πρa​HD2ca2​≃2.0×1023 J,
(10)
which exceeds 1022 J by about a factor of twenty. Reasonable atmospheric averages and order-unity geometrical coefficients do not reverse the comparison. Escape of energy through the top of the atmosphere makes the fully confined estimate optimistic; Earth's curvature changes its area estimate by an order-unity factor.
The strong atmospheric blast will not survive to Lansing in this model. A weakened acoustic wave can nevertheless travel there. The supplied energy and atmospheric height do not determine its detectable amplitude, detailed attenuation or damage: those require an atmospheric propagation model. Thus “no disturbance of any kind reaches Lansing” would be an unjustified stronger conclusion.

This is magnetic flux freezing in ideal magnetohydrodynamics. In addition to the supplied ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation, a physical magnetic field obeys Gauss's law for magnetism, ∇⋅B=0.
Parameterize the material surface by two particle labels a,b, so dS=(∂a​x×∂b​x)dadb and Dx/Dt=u. Differentiating these tangents gives the material surface element evolution law
DtD​dS=[(∇⋅u)I−(∇u)T]dS,
(11)
where (∇u)ij​=∂j​ui​. Thus the material magnetic-flux transport theorem is
DtD​∫S(t)​B⋅dS=∫S(t)​[DtDB​+B(∇⋅u)−(B⋅∇)u]⋅dS.
(12)
Expand the curl in the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation:
∂t​B=(B⋅∇)u−(u⋅∇)B−B(∇⋅u)+u(∇⋅B).
(13)
Since D/Dt=∂t​+u⋅∇ is the material derivative, the first three terms cancel the corresponding terms in the surface transport formula. The remaining integrand is u∇⋅B=0. Therefore
DtD​∫S(t)​B⋅dS=0.​
(14)
No incompressible flow assumption is needed: the changing surface area cancels the compression term. This proof assumes smooth velocity and magnetic field and a surface carried with the fluid; the ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation by itself, for an arbitrary non-solenoidal vector field, would leave the final divergence term.

The buoyancy frequency, also called the Brunt–Väisälä frequency, is the angular frequency of a small vertical displacement restored by buoyancy in a stably stratified fluid. Take z upward, gravitational acceleration −gz, and a background in hydrostatic equilibrium,
dzdp​=−ρg.
(15)
Displace a small gas element upward by ξ, sufficiently slowly for its pressure to match its surroundings but without heat exchange. For this adiabatic process, p/ργ is constant along the element's motion, so its mass density changes by ργ−1(dlnp/dz)ξ. The surrounding mass density changes by (dρ/dz)ξ. The element's excess mass density is consequently
Δρ=ρ(γ1​dzdlnp​−dzdlnρ​)ξ.
(16)
Its acceleration is ξ¨​=−gΔρ/ρ=−N2ξ. Thus
N2=g(γ1​dzdlnp​−dzdlnρ​).​
(17)
For a gas of fixed composition obeying the ideal gas law, ρ∝p/T. The adiabatic buoyancy frequency of an ideal gas is therefore
N2=g[T1​dzdT​−γpγ−1​dzdp​]=Tg​(dzdT​+cp​g​),​
(18)
where cp​=γR/(γ−1) is the specific heat capacity at constant pressure, and R=p/(ρT) is the specific gas constant. Here N2 has units s−2; N is an angular frequency, not a cyclic frequency. Positive N2 gives simple harmonic motion, negative N2 gives convective instability, and N2=0 is marginal. The temperature form assumes fixed composition; the preceding density form is more general.

These equations concern the incompressible model of part (b); its N2 does not contain the adiabatic compression correction needed in part (a).

For N2>0, write K=kx2​+kz2​​ and choose either signed branch ω=sNkx​/K, with s=±1. These two branches cover the roots of the squared dispersion relation. The group velocity of an internal gravity wave is
cg​=∇k​ω=K3sN​(kz2​,0,−kx​kz​).​
(27)
If instead the positive-frequency root is chosen for both signs of kx​, set s=+1 and multiply the displayed components by sgn(kx​), away from the cusp kx​=0.
Taking the dot product with the wave vector gives
k⋅cg​=0.​
(28)
Thus the group velocity, which describes the travel of a narrow wave packet and its energy, is perpendicular to the wave vector, and hence perpendicular to the phase velocity cp​=ωk/K2. This is the internal-wave phase and group velocity geometry. It also follows from phase-group orthogonality for degree-zero dispersion: the frequency depends only on the direction of k, not its magnitude. For kz​=0, the group velocity is zero, consistent with the stationary extremum ∣ω∣=N.

The Rankine-Hugoniot conditions follow by integrating conservation laws across a thin moving layer, then letting its macroscopic thickness tend to zero. For example, normal momentum conservation in one dimension has the conservative form
∂t​(ρu)+∂x​(ρu2+p)=0.
(29)
For a shock wave moving at speed V, integrate over a control interval whose endpoints move with the discontinuity. The Reynolds transport theorem gives
V[ρu]=[ρu2+p],​
(30)
where [f]=fR​−fL​ denotes the difference of limiting states. Mass conservation similarly gives V[ρ]=[ρu]. If v=u−V is the normal velocity in the shock frame, combining the two relations yields
[ρv]=0,[p+ρv2]=0.​
(31)
The same construction applied to conservation of energy, for a non-radiating normal shock wave with nonzero mass flux, gives [h+v2/2]=0, where h is the specific enthalpy. An equation of state closes these relations, and the physical compression branch has increasing entropy.
Finite dynamic viscosity replaces the ideal discontinuity by a thin smooth transition. A viscous stress contributes a derivative to the momentum flux inside the layer and converts directed kinetic energy into internal energy. If its gradients vanish in the uniform far states, the same limiting Rankine-Hugoniot conditions still hold there. Balancing nonlinear transport with viscous diffusion gives a thickness of order ν/∣uL​−uR​∣ in the viscous Burgers equation, where ν is the kinematic viscosity.

The pressure-free equation has the scalar conservation law form
∂t​u+∂x​f(u)=0,f(u)=21​u2.
(32)
A smooth travelling ansatz u=U(x−Vt) obeys (U−V)U′=0, so it cannot continuously connect two distinct constant states. The inviscid shock wave must be interpreted as a discontinuous weak solution. Its Rankine-Hugoniot condition is
V(u2​−u1​)=f(u2​)−f(u1​)=21​(u22​−u12​).
(33)
For u1​=u2​,
V=2u1​+u2​​.​
(34)

For a physical compressive entropy shock of the Inviscid Burgers equation, the left and right states satisfy uL​>uR​ and therefore uL​>V>uR​: characteristic curves run into the shock wave from both sides. The ordering uL​<uR​ instead produces a rarefaction wave.

For ν>0 and positive Ψ, set f=logΨ, so the Cole-Hopf transformation is u=−2νfx​. Substitution into the viscous Burgers equation gives
ut​+uux​−νuxx​=−2ν∂x​(ft​−νfxx​−νfx2​)=−2ν∂x​(ΨΨt​−νΨxx​​)=0.
(35)
Hence the most general equation implied by this substitution is
Ψt​−νΨxx​=C(t)Ψ.
(36)
The time normalization in the Cole-Hopf transformation accounts for the arbitrary function C(t): multiplying Ψ by a nonzero function of time leaves ∂x​logΨ and therefore u unchanged. Replacing Ψ by e−∫tC(s)dsΨ sets C=0. With this conventional normalization,
∂t​Ψ=ν∂x2​Ψ,​
(37)
the heat equation.
For each j=1,2, the function Ψj​=eνκj2​t+κj​x obeys ∂t​Ψj​=νκj2​Ψj​=ν∂x2​Ψj​. By the superposition principle for this linear partial differential equation,

Let aj​=νκj2​t+κj​x. Differentiating the logarithm gives
u=−2νea1​+ea2​κ1​ea1​+κ2​ea2​​.

For definiteness order κ1​<κ2​. Then ea1​ dominates as x→−∞, while ea2​ dominates as x→+∞. The limiting states are

Their exponent difference is

(41)
Consequently, the Viscous Burgers travelling shock moves at

(42)

u(x,t)=V−2Δu​tanh(4νΔuξ​).​
(43)

ℓ=Δu4ν​=∣κ2​−κ1​∣2​.​
(44)

Treat the cylinder as infinitely long, with a vacuum exterior and no surface tension. Let Φ0​ be its gravitational potential. Integrating the gravitational Poisson equation over a coaxial cylinder of radius r<R and length L, then applying the divergence theorem, gives
2πrLdrdΦ0​​=4πG(πr2Lρ),drdΦ0​​=2πGρr.
(45)
Thus the inward gravitational acceleration is −2πGρrr. Hydrostatic equilibrium requires
drdp0​​=−ρdrdΦ0​​=−2πGρ2r.
(46)
Impose zero pressure on the free surface, p0​(R)=0, and integrate inward:
p0​(r)=πGρ2(R2−r2),0≤r<R.​
(47)
This is the hydrostatic self-gravitating incompressible cylinder. Its central pressure is πGρ2R2, and the units of the expression are Pa. A nonzero external pressure would add that constant to p0​(r).

Write total pressure and gravitational potential as
p=p0​(r)+π(r,z,t),Φ=Φ0​(r)+ψ(r,z,t).
(48)
Here π is the Eulerian perturbation of a fluid variable applied to pressure, while ψ is the corresponding gravitational potential perturbation. They have units Pa and m2s−2, respectively. The constant interior mass density is ρ.
The Euler equations are
∂t​u+(u⋅∇)u=−ρ1​∇(p0​+π)−∇(Φ0​+ψ).
(49)
Subtract the hydrostatic equilibrium relation ∇p0​/ρ+∇Φ0​=0 and discard terms quadratic in the perturbation. Mass conservation gives incompressible flow, and the gravitational Poisson equation has no bulk density perturbation. Thus, strictly inside the fluid,
∂t​u=−ρ∇π​−∇ψ,∇⋅u=0,∇2ψ=0.​
(50)
The last equation is Laplace equation, not a statement that self-gravity has disappeared. The displaced surface carries a first-order surface mass density ρη, where η is its radial displacement, and this supplies the source and matching conditions for ψ at the moving interface. The axisymmetric gravitational potential of a displaced cylindrical interface, expressed using modified Bessel functions, incorporates that boundary source.
It is useful to define W=ψ+π/ρ. Taking the divergence of the momentum equation gives ∇2W=0. For an axisymmetric Fourier mode with axial wavenumber k>0, regularity at r=0 selects radial dependence I0​(kr), the Modified Bessel function of the first kind, since K0​(kr) is singular there. In what follows k=∣kz​∣ can always be chosen nonnegative.

Define the dimensionless right-hand side of part (d) by F(κ). Then ω2=−4πGρF(κ). Because I0′​(κ)=I1​(κ)>0 and I0​(κ)>0 for κ>0, its sign is the sign of I0​(κ)K0​(κ)−1/2. Numerically solving the displayed equation gives κc​≃1.0667.
For 0<κ<κc​, ω2<0. Write ω=iσ with σ=4πGρF​>0. The time factor e−iωt then contains an exponentially growing solution. For κ>κc​, ω2>0 and the ideal inviscid modes oscillate stably, without damping. At the crossing the frequency is zero and the mode is marginal. In terms of the axial wavelength λz​=2π/k,
λz​>κc​2πR​≃5.89Rλz​<5.89R​:gravitationally unstable,:oscillatory and stable.​​
(59)

Thus a characteristic fragmentation spacing is several cylinder diameters; a quantitative nonlinear fragment mass is beyond this linear stability calculation.
For unstable modes the physical displacement must be interpreted as Re[ϵei(kz−ωt)] with complex amplitudes handled consistently throughout. The paper's real-part notation is harmless for the squared dispersion relation, but it should not be read as imposing real ω in a gravitational instability calculation. The exactly uniform mode k=0 is excluded from the finite-k derivation; the limit of the curve alone does not establish a separate volume-preserving uniform-radius mode.
