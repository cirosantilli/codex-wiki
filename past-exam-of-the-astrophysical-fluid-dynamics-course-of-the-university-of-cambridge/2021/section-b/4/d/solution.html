Rewrite the relation from part (c) as
(γ−1)sinβcos(β−θ)=(γ+1)cosβsin(β−θ).
(23)
The angle-addition identities reduce this to
sin(2β−θ)=γsinθ.
(24)
For 0<θ<arcsin(1/γ), the right side lies strictly between zero and one. There are two physically admissible strong oblique-shock angle branches:
βweak​βstrong​​=21​[θ+arcsin(γsinθ)],=21​[θ+π−arcsin(γsinθ)].​​
(25)
Both lie between θ and π/2. They merge when γsinθ=1, proving the maximum deflection through a strong perfect-gas shock,
θmax​=arcsin(1/γ),β∗​=4π​+2θmax​​.​
(26)
For small positive θ, the formal strong-shock-limit asymptotics are
βweak​∼2γ+1​θ,βstrong​∼2π​−2γ−1​θ.​
(27)
The weak branch is normally observed for an unconfined supersonic stream turning through a modest compression corner. It has a smaller normal component of upstream velocity, hence a smaller downstream pressure increase; for small turns the downstream flow remains supersonic. The nearly normal strong branch can be selected by suitable downstream pressure conditions, so it is not mathematically forbidden.
The two limits must be ordered honestly. At finite M, the weak oblique shock approaches the Mach angle arcsin(1/M) as θ→0, rather than zero. The displayed weak-branch asymptotic applies when the large-M limit is taken first, or in the overlapping range 1/M≪θ≪1, where Msinβ≫1 remains valid.
