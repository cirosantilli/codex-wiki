The Rayleigh-Taylor instability extracts gravitational potential energy when a heavy fluid is supported above a lighter one. A small downward excursion of heavy material and upward excursion of light material can grow instead of experiencing restoring buoyancy. The Kelvin-Helmholtz instability instead extracts kinetic energy from a velocity difference across an interface or shear layer. The interface displacement and its induced flow reinforce one another by tapping the background shear; a reversed density ordering is not required.
A useful common model has two inviscid, incompressible, semi-infinite fluids separated at z=0, with lower density/velocity ρ1​,U1​, upper density/velocity ρ2​,U2​, downward gravity g, and surface tension σ. For a disturbance proportional to ei(kx−ωt), with k>0, the two-fluid interfacial gravity-shear dispersion relation is
(ω−kρ1​+ρ2​ρ1​U1​+ρ2​U2​​)2=ρ1​+ρ2​gk(ρ1​−ρ2​)+σk3​−(ρ1​+ρ2​)2ρ1​ρ2​​k2(U1​−U2​)2.

A negative right side gives exponential growth. With no shear or surface tension and ρ2​>ρ1​, the growth rate of the Rayleigh-Taylor instability is s=Agk​, where A=(ρ2​−ρ1​)/(ρ2​+ρ1​) is the Atwood number. With equal densities and no restoring force, the growth rate of the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability is s=k∣U1​−U2​∣/2. Heavy-over-light stratification drives Rayleigh-Taylor growth; velocity shear drives Kelvin-Helmholtz growth. A heavy lower fluid supplies gravitational stabilization, and surface tension stabilizes sufficiently short waves in this ideal interface model.
Finite shear-layer thickness, viscosity, compressibility and magnetic tension can change the growth rates and unstable wavelength range. In particular, unlimited growth with increasing k is a limitation of the zero-thickness, zero-surface-tension idealization. Density-interface overturning and jet-boundary mixing are appropriate physical applications of the two mechanisms, but the nonlinear mixed state is not determined by the linear growth rate alone.
