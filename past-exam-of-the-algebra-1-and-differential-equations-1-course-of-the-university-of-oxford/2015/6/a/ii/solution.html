Work with the L1 norm on R2. The scalar Lipschitz hypothesis implies
∥f(x,y)−f(x,z)∥1​​=∣y2​−z2​∣+∣F(x,y)−F(x,z)∣≤(1+L)∥y−z∥1​.​
(61)
We may take L≥0. The compact set S and continuity give a finite bound M=maxS​∥f∥1​. Choose
0<η≤min{h,M+1k​,2(1+L)1​}.​
(62)
Let I=[a−η,a+η]. The space Cη​ of continuous maps I→Bk​(b) is complete in the supremum norm: it is a closed subset of the Banach space C(I,R2). Define the Picard operator on a closed ball by
(Pg)(x)=b+∫ax​f(t,g(t))dt.
(63)
For any g∈Cη​, the integral triangle inequality gives
∥Pg(x)−b∥1​≤∣x−a∣M≤ηM<k.
(64)
The integrand is continuous, so the result is continuous and remains in the prescribed closed ball. Thus P is a self-map of the complete space. Moreover,
∥Pg−Ph∥∞​≤η(1+L)∥g−h∥∞​≤21​∥g−h∥∞​.
(65)
It is a contraction mapping. The contraction mapping theorem gives a unique fixed point y. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, the fixed-point identity makes y continuously differentiable and gives y′=f(x,y) and y(a)=b. Its first component is twice continuously differentiable, because its derivative is the second component.
Conversely, integrating any solution that remains in the ball makes it a fixed point, so it equals y. A solution starting at b cannot first reach the boundary of this ball during I: before such an exit the same estimate gives distance at most ηM<k, contradicting distance k at the first exit. This proves existence and uniqueness on ∣x−a∣≤η, not merely formal convergence of an iteration. It is the local Picard-Lindelöf theorem construction.
