In position-velocity variables (u,v), the scalar right-hand side is
F(x,u,v)=uv2​+2xv.
(66)
Its only relevant singularity is u=0. The initial state (x,u,v)=(0,1,1) is separated from it. For an explicit application of part (a), take h=1 and k=1/2 in the L1 norm. On the resulting closed ball, 1/2≤u,v≤3/2, and ∣x∣≤1.
The partial derivatives obey
∣Fu​∣=u2v2​≤9,∣Fv​∣=​u2v​+2x​≤8.
(67)
The ball is convex, so integrating these partial derivatives along a segment between two states gives the uniform Lipschitz condition
∣F(x,u,v)−F(x,u,v)∣≤9(∣u−u∣+∣v−v∣).
(68)
Continuity holds on the whole slab, and
∣F∣≤1/2(3/2)2​+2⋅1⋅23​=215​,∥(v,F)∥1​≤9.
(69)
Thus L=9, M≤9, and the choice η=1/20​ satisfies the self-map and contraction bounds in part (a). This proves a unique solution on [−1/20,1/20], with y bounded away from zero there.
There is also a useful exact representation. Where y>0, set q=y′/y, the logarithmic derivative. Differentiation cancels the quadratic term:
q′=yy′′​−y2(y′)2​=2xq,q(0)=1.
(70)
Solving this first-order linear differential equation gives q=ex2, and a second integration yields
y(x)=exp(∫0x​et2dt).​
(71)
This function is positive and smooth for every real x, satisfies both initial data, and verifies the original ordinary differential equation by direct differentiation. It therefore extends the unique local solution to the entire real line. The logarithmic-derivative reduction of a second-order equation explains why this apparently nonlinear example is explicitly integrable by integration.
