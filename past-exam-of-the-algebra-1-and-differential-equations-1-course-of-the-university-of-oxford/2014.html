The minimal polynomial mT​ is the monic polynomial of least polynomial degree such that mT​(T)=0. Here a polynomial p(x)=∑j​cj​xj is evaluated at the linear operator by p(T)=∑j​cj​Tj, with the constant term acting as c0​I.
An annihilating polynomial exists because I,T,T2,… lie in the finite-dimensional vector space EndF​(V) and are therefore eventually linearly dependent. Uniqueness follows by subtracting two monic annihilators of the same least degree: their difference has smaller degree and still annihilates T.
Equivalently,
mT​∣p⟺p(T)=0.​
(1)
For the forward implication evaluate the factorization at T. For the reverse implication divide p by mT​ using polynomial division; the remainder annihilates T, so minimality makes it zero. This also identifies the annihilating polynomials as the principal ideal generated by mT​.

The Picard-Lindelöf theorem gives local existence and uniqueness for an initial value problem when f is continuous on a rectangle around (a,b) and is Lipschitz continuous in its second variable there, with a constant uniform in the first variable. A sufficient hypothesis is that f and ∂f/∂y are continuous on that rectangle.
More quantitatively, if the rectangle is ∣x−a∣≤h0​, ∣y−b∣≤k, and ∣f∣≤M, a sufficiently small h>0 with h≤h0​ and hM<k yields a unique differentiable solution on [a−h,a+h] remaining inside the vertical strip. The proof uses the integral equation
y(x)=b+∫ax​f(t,y(t))dt
(41)
and Picard iteration, or the Banach fixed-point theorem after shrinking h to make the integral operator a contraction. The continuity and Lipschitz hypotheses must hold near the actual initial point.

For every a=0 and every real b, there is exactly one solution on the maximal connected interval containing a. The formula in part (i) proves existence on the entire appropriate half-line, and the initial condition fixes its sole constant uniquely. This agrees with the local Picard-Lindelöf theorem.
For a=0, there is no classical solution on an interval containing the initial point, whatever b is: the printed equation is not defined there. Even if one only imposes the equation for x=0 and asks for a finite continuous extension across zero, the negative side rules it out. For x→0−:

The maximum principle for subharmonic functions here states that if u∈C2(D)∩C(D) and Δu=f≥0 in D, then
Dmax​u=∂Dmax​u.​
(50)
If f≤0, apply this to −u to obtain the corresponding minimum principle. In particular a harmonic function has both its maximum and minimum on the boundary. Without a sign condition on f, this conclusion need not hold.

For the non-strict case put uϵ​=u+ϵ(x2+y2), with ϵ>0. Then Δuϵ​=f+4ϵ>0, so the strict case yields, for z∈D,
