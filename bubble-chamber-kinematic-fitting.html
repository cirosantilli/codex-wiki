Alvarez describes a progression from measured coordinates to momenta and angles, then conservation-constrained fits testing alternative track identities. Computer programs made the repeated tests practical; an insufficiently constrained event could retain several hypotheses.[ref]
A mathematical formulation explains what such a fit can establish. Let y collect measured momenta and angles, with positive-definite covariance matrix V. For a chosen reaction hypothesis, adjust them to x by minimizing a generalized least squares objective,
χ2=(x−y)TV−1(x−y),g(x)=0.
(39)
The constraints g express four-momentum conservation and any independently known daughter masses. For each proposed identity, calculate energy from the energy–momentum relation, Ei2​=mi2​c4+c2∣pi​∣2. A missing neutral particle adds unknowns; a measured decay vertex can add geometric constraints. Compatibility depends on the measurement errors and the independent constraints, not just the smallest raw χ2 among unrelated fits.
A simple independent check uses a single momentum conservation constraint. Suppose two measured one-dimensional momenta are 0.32 and −0.28GeV/c, each with independent standard deviation 0.02GeV/c, and a stipulated parent at rest requires their sum to vanish. Minimizing the two equal-weight squared changes gives fitted values 0.30 and −0.30GeV/c, with χ2=2. A fit repairs a mismatch according to a hypothesis; it does not prove that hypothesis. This one-component illustration is not a complete reaction fit.
