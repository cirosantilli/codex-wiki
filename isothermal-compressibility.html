Isothermal compressibility (κT​=−V−1(∂V/∂P)T,N​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Compressibility
OurBigBook.com Words: 316 Articles: 2
Isothermal compressibility is the compressibility measured at fixed temperature, particle number and composition. A slowly compressed sealed piston in contact with a thermal reservoir can approach this constraint. For an ideal gas, differentiating V=NkB​T/P at fixed temperature gives (∂V/∂P)T,N​=−V/P, hence κT​=1/P. A small pressure increase ΔP therefore gives ΔV/V≃−ΔP/P. For a finite change, use the whole isothermal process curve V2​/V1​=P1​/P2​ instead.
Take one mole of classical monatomic gas, with stipulated molar gas constant R=8.314Jmol−1K−1, initially at T0​=300K and P0​=100kPa. The initial volume is V0​=nRT0​/P0​=0.024942m3. Compressing to V0​/2 gives
Isothermal:Reversible adiabatic:​P2​=2P0​=200kPa,T2​=300K,P2​=25/3P0​≃317.48kPa,T2​=22/3T0​≃476.22K.​
(38)
The isothermal compression releases heat to the thermal reservoir; the reversible adiabatic compression retains the input work as internal energy. Reversing either curve gives the corresponding expansion, with adiabatic expansion cooling the gas.
The equilibrium curvature of Gibbs free energy provides another measurement of isothermal compressibility:
κT​=−V1​(∂P2∂2G​)T,N​.
(39)
This follows from V=(∂G/∂P)T,N​, with G denoting Gibbs free energy. Within a stable single thermodynamic phase, increasing pressure reduces volume and makes this curvature negative. At phase coexistence, changing the phase fraction can change the volume at constant temperature and pressure; the usual finite single-phase isothermal compressibility then need not exist. Near a critical point, it can diverge. [ref]
