Absolute magnitude (M)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Astronomical magnitude
OurBigBook.com Words: 367 Articles: 3
Absolute magnitude M, in the stellar convention used here, is the apparent magnitude a source would have at 10 parsecs, with interstellar extinction removed. The hypothetical observation uses the specified photometric passband and magnitude zero point, preserving the source's emitted spectrum and luminosity. Moving every source to this common distance lets us compare intrinsic brightness without confusing it with distance. [ref].
For a fixed source with isotropy of emission, in transparent space with negligible relativistic and cosmological effects, the inverse-square law gives the hypothetical measurement F10​ and absolute magnitude
F10​=F(d)(10pcd​)2,M=−2.5log10​(Fref​F10​​).
(19)
The factor applies in the same photometric passband, with unchanged emitted spectrum. Thus absolute magnitude compares luminosity in that response, rather than automatically the total bolometric luminosity over all wavelengths.
Consider a toy source with M=0. Its apparent magnitude is m=0 at 10 parsecs, m=5 at 100 parsecs, and m=10 at 1000 parsecs, neglecting interstellar extinction. Its absolute magnitude remains M=0 throughout: moving the observer changes received radiative flux, not emitted luminosity. Another toy source with M=−5 has 100 times its passband output under the same isotropy and calibration assumptions. Negative absolute magnitudes are allowed too; "absolute" names the reference-distance convention, not an absolute value.
A calibrated standard candle reverses this reasoning: its known absolute magnitude and measured apparent magnitude give a distance modulus. This is why estimating intrinsic brightness is so useful for measuring astronomical distances. [ref].
