Take the initial internal pressure as negligible and the external atmospheric pressure as p0​=1.01325×105Pa. The window has frontal area A=π(0.400m)2=0.50265m2. Before appreciable air has entered, its inward pressure force is approximately p0​A=5.09×104N. Newton's second law therefore gives the initial collective inward acceleration
a0​≃Mp0​A​=4.63×102ms−2≃47g.​
(11)
Here M=110kg and g≃9.81ms−2. Neglect gravity in this much larger pressure-driven acceleration, together with supports and elastic stresses once the glass has broken. The given thickness d=0.080m implies a plausible average glass mass density M/(Ad)≃2.74×103kgm−3. Equivalently, a fragment retaining the original thickness has acceleration p0​/(ρg​d), independent of its frontal area while it experiences the full pressure difference.
For a vacuum-window implosion energy estimate, the ideal pressure-work scale for filling vessel volume V=1.5m3 is W≃p0​V. Assigning all that pressure-volume work to the glass's kinetic energy gives
21​Mvest2​≃p0​V,vest​≃M2p0​V​​≃5.26×101ms−1.​
(12)
The same result follows from retaining acceleration a0​ over an effective swept distance V/A≃2.98m, so that vest2​=2a0​V/A. This is an ideal upper energy-budget estimate for the common speed, or mass-weighted root mean square speed, of all the glass. It assumes an atmospheric reservoir maintains the driving pressure and that the whole vessel's pressure-work budget can accelerate the glass. In practice, air motion, fragmentation, heating and collisions share that energy, the pressure difference falls, and fragments may hit the vessel after a much shorter path. The unspecified vessel shape and fragment trajectories prevent a unique actual maximum speed; concentrating energy into one small fragment also prevents interpreting this collective estimate as a strict bound on every individual fragment.
