For a particle of rest mass m0​ and velocity u, the relativistic energy and momentum are
E=γu​m0​c2,p=γu​m0​u,γu​=(1−u2/c2)−1/2.​
(36)
For a photon of wavelength λ travelling along n,
Eγ​=λhc​,pγ​=λh​n,Eγ​=c∣pγ​∣.​
(37)
Here h is Planck constant and c is the speed of light.
Let ε and ε′ be the incident and scattered photon energies. The stationary electron initially has energy me​c2. By four-momentum conservation, its final energy and momentum are
Ee′​=me​c2+ε−ε′,pe′​=cε​n−cε′​n′,n⋅n′=cosθ.
(38)
Use the electron's relativistic energy-momentum relation, rather than assuming its recoil is nonrelativistic:
(me​c2+ε−ε′)2=me2​c4+ε2+ε′2−2εε′cosθ.
(39)
Cancelling common terms gives
me​c2(ε−ε′)=εε′(1−cosθ),ε′1​−ε1​=me​c21−cosθ​.
(40)
Since λ=hc/ε, the Compton wavelength shift is
λ′−λ=me​ch​(1−cosθ).​
(41)
The word elastic here means that the final electron has the same rest mass as initially and the complete photon-electron system conserves total energy; the photon alone loses energy to recoil.
For backscattering, 1−cosθ=2, and hence
ε′=1+2ε/(me​c2)ε​=2.5110.511​MeV=0.2035MeV.​
(42)
Choose the incident direction as +x, so that the scattered photon travels in the −x direction. An observer moving with velocity +βcx meets that photon head-on. The Lorentz transformation of four-momentum gives the observed energy
εobs​=γβ​(ε′−βcpx′​)=γβ​ε′(1+β)=ε′1−β1+β​​.
(43)
This is the approaching-observer relativistic Doppler effect. Set D=ε/ε′=2.511/0.511 and solve:
β=D2+1D2−1​=0.9205 approximately.​
(44)
The observer must move opposite to the scattered photon, along the original incident direction, at approximately 0.9205c. Moving with the scattered photon would redshift it instead.
