For ordinary conservative classical mechanics, the Lagrangian is L(q,q˙​,t)=T−V, where T is the kinetic energy and V the potential energy. More generally it is the function whose action ∫Ldt satisfies the principle of stationary action. The canonical momenta and Hamiltonian are
pi​=∂q˙​i​∂L​,H(q,p,t)=i∑​pi​q˙​i​−L(q,q˙​,t).​
(1)
For a regular Lagrangian, the first equations can be inverted to express the generalized velocities in terms of q,p,t; this defines the Legendre transform on phase space.
Along a solution of the Euler-Lagrange equations, p˙​i​=∂L/∂qi​. Taking a total time derivative gives
dtdH​​=i∑​(p˙​i​q˙​i​+pi​q¨​i​)−i∑​(∂qi​∂L​q˙​i​+∂q˙​i​∂L​q¨​i​)−∂t∂L​=−∂t∂L​.​
(2)
Thus an autonomous Lagrangian has
dtdH​=0.​
(3)
For the usual time-independent mechanical coordinates and a velocity-independent potential energy, the kinetic energy is quadratic in the generalized velocities, so ∑i​pi​q˙​i​=2T and H=T+V. The conservation proof itself uses the Euler-Lagrange equations, rather than assuming this special expression for energy.

Choose the initial incident velocity as u1​x^. The centre-of-mass velocity in the laboratory frame is
V=m1​+m2​m1​u1​​x^.
(12)
In the centre-of-mass frame, the initial velocities are u1∗​=m2​u1​x^/(m1​+m2​) and u2∗​=−m1​u1​x^/(m1​+m2​). Conservation of momentum and kinetic energy in an elastic collision preserves these two speed magnitudes. Let θ∗ be the outgoing angle of particle 1 in that frame, measured from the initial positive x direction. Then
v1∗​=m1​+m2​m2​u1​​(cosθ∗,sinθ∗),v2∗​=−m1​+m2​m1​u1​​(cosθ∗,sinθ∗).
(13)
Adding V to transform back to the laboratory frame gives
v1​=m1​+m2​u1​​(m1​+m2​cosθ∗,m2​sinθ∗),v2​=m1​+m2​m1​u1​​(1−cosθ∗,−sinθ∗).
(14)
The nonnegative laboratory recoil angle θ2​ is measured below the incident axis in this choice of scattering plane. Hence the recoil angle in a two-body elastic collision obeys
tanθ2​=v2x​∣v2y​∣​=1−cosθ∗sinθ∗​=cot2θ∗​,θ2​=2π−θ∗​(0<θ∗≤π).​
(15)
The source identity therefore uses θ∗ for the projectile's outgoing direction in the centre-of-mass frame. If instead it denotes particle 2's outgoing direction, the corresponding expression has 1+cosθ∗ in the denominator. A signed laboratory angle for the drawn target trajectory would carry a minus sign. At zero deflection the target remains at rest, so its direction is undefined; θ2​→π/2 is a limit.

The parallel axis theorem relates the moment of inertia about an axis through the centre of mass to that about a parallel axis a perpendicular distance d away:
I=ICM​+Mtot​d2.​
(23)
To see the cross term vanish, write each perpendicular displacement as its displacement from the centre of mass plus the fixed offset. In the defining mass integral, the first moment about the centre of mass is zero.
For a thin planar rigid body in the xy plane, the perpendicular axis theorem says
Iz​=Ix​+Iy​,​
(24)
where all three axes pass through the same point, x,y lie in the plane, and z is perpendicular to it. This follows directly from x2+y2 being the squared distance from the z axis. The thin-planar condition and common intersection point are essential; the formula is not a general identity for three-dimensional bodies.

For a differentiable central potential, the force is F=−V′(r)r^. The angular momentum is J=mr×r˙, so
dtdJ​=r×F=0.
(32)
This proves conservation of angular momentum. For J=0, the trajectory lies in the fixed plane perpendicular to J. In plane polar coordinates, J=mr2ϕ˙​. Substituting this into the kinetic energy gives the radial energy equation
E=21​mr˙2+Veff​(r),Veff​(r)=V(r)+2mr2J2​.​
(33)
The second term is the centrifugal contribution to the effective potential. Its radial derivative reproduces mr¨=−V′(r)+J2/(mr3). Purely radial motion is the J=0 case.
For the satellite, write μ=GM, with M Earth's mass and m the satellite's mass. At apogee and perigee, the radial velocity is zero, so angular momentum conservation and conservation of energy give
rA​vA​=rP​vP​,21​vA2​−rA​μ​=21​vP2​−rP​μ​.
(34)
Eliminating vP​ yields
vA2​=rA​(rA​+rP​)2μrP​​.
(35)
For an elliptic orbit, rA​+rP​=2a. Therefore the vis-viva equation at apogee is
vA2​=GM(rA​2​−a1​).​
(36)
Substitution into the orbital energy gives E=−GMm/(2a). The circular case follows by continuity, or directly from the radial force balance.
An instantaneous tangential retrograde burn at apogee leaves the position unchanged. For the speed change v↦(1−ϵ)v, the orbital energy change is
ΔE=21​m((1−ϵ)2−1)v2=−ϵmv2+O(ϵ2).
(37)

A Lorentz invariant has the same value when the same physical system is described in any inertial frame. The components of its energy-momentum four-vector change, but their Minkowski norm does not. In particular the relativistic energy-momentum relation is
E2−c2∣p∣2=M2c4,​
(40)
where M is the rest mass. Individual total energy and momentum are frame-dependent; the combination on the left is invariant.
For the fixed-target annihilation, the initial total laboratory energy is E+Mc2 and its total momentum is the incident particle's p, with c2p2=E2−M2c4. The centre-of-mass energy available for new-particle creation is therefore
W=(E+Mc2)2−c2p2​=2Mc2(E+Mc2)​.​
(41)
In the centre-of-mass frame the total momentum vanishes, so W can supply the new particles' total rest energy at threshold. One does not subtract the annihilated particles' original rest energy. The excess laboratory energy beyond W is associated with motion of the whole final system in the laboratory frame.
For the subsequent elastic collision, the equal and opposite transverse components of final momentum imply equal final momentum magnitudes, and hence equal final energies, for the two identical particles. By conservation of relativistic energy, each has
Ef​=Mc2+2T​.
(42)
Their final momentum magnitude pf​ and the incident momentum magnitude pi​ obey
c2pf2​=TMc2+4T2​,c2pi2​=T2+2TMc2.
(43)
Longitudinal momentum conservation gives pi​=2pf​cosθ. It follows that the equal-angle relativistic elastic scattering relation is
cos2θ=4pf2​pi2​​=T+4Mc2T+2Mc2​.​
(44)
This derivation assumes nonzero incident kinetic energy and the stated symmetric outgoing paths. The angle between those paths is 2θ, which is evaluated in the two following parts.

For the elastic pendulum, set r=l+x>0 and measure θ from the downward vertical. In the vertical plane, Cartesian coordinates are X=rsinθ and Z=−rcosθ, with Z upward. Differentiating gives X˙2+Z˙2=x˙2+r2θ˙2. Thus the Lagrangian is
L=21​m(x˙2+(l+x)2θ˙2)−21​kx2+mg(l+x)cosθ.​
(50)
For x, the Euler-Lagrange equation gives
mx¨=m(l+x)θ˙2−kx+mgcosθ.​
(51)
For θ, the canonical momentum is pθ​=m(l+x)2θ˙. Its Euler-Lagrange equation is
dtd​[m(l+x)2θ˙]+mg(l+x)sinθ=0,
(52)
or, for positive length,
(l+x)θ¨+2x˙θ˙+gsinθ=0.​
(53)
At an equilibrium, all time derivatives vanish, so kx=mgcosθ and sinθ=0. Modulo full rotations, the two formal possibilities are
(x,θ)=(kmg​,0),(x,θ)=(−kmg​,π).​
(54)
The lower configuration always has positive length. The upper one is a physical straight-spring configuration only if l>mg/k. For example, l=mg/(2k) would give the purported upper state a negative length −mg/(2k); at l=mg/k its zero length makes the angular coordinate singular. Also, the straight compressed spring is an idealization; a real unsupported compressed spring can buckle.
The potential energy is V=kx2/2−mg(l+x)cosθ. At either formal stationary point its mixed second derivative vanishes, while
Vxx​=k,Vθθ​=mg(l+x)cosθ.
(55)
Both curvatures are positive at the lower state, making it a stable equilibrium; the upper state, when it has positive length, has negative angular curvature and is unstable.
To linearize about the stable state, define the small displacement ξ=x−mg/k, not x itself, and r0​=l+mg/k. Products such as θ˙2, ξ˙​θ˙ and ξθ¨ are second order, while cosθ=1+O(θ2) and sinθ=θ+O(θ3). The first-order equations of motion therefore decouple:
ξ¨​+mk​ξ=0,θ¨+l+mg/kg​θ=0.​
(56)
There are two independent normal modes. The radial one is vertical simple harmonic motion with angular frequency ωr​=k/m​. The transverse one is a simple pendulum oscillation at fixed, statically stretched length r0​, with angular frequency ωθ​=g/r0​​. Their decoupling is a first-order small-oscillation result, not an assertion about the nonlinear motion of the elastic pendulum.
