Take horizontal motion towards the second projectile as positive. In projectile motion, both bodies have the same downward gravitational acceleration, so it cancels from their relative motion. Their coordinates are
x1​=v1​t,z1​=h−21​gt2,x2​=v2​tcosθ,z2​=v2​tsinθ−21​gt2.
(1)
For a collision at t>0, equality of the horizontal coordinates requires v2​cosθ=v1​. Equality of the heights then requires v2​tsinθ=h. Hence
θ=arccosv2​v1​​,t=v22​−v12​​h​.​
(2)
The upward solution requires v2​>v1​. A collision strictly above the ground further requires t<2h/g​, or v22​−v12​>gh/2. The horizontal target distance does not independently affect the interception geometry. If the first shot is also required to hit that ground target, its parameters satisfy l=v1​2h/g​.

The centre-of-momentum frame is an inertial frame in which the system's total momentum is zero. For nonrelativistic particles with total mass MT​ and total momentum P, it moves with velocity V=P/MT​ relative to the original frame.
Under a Galilean boost by a constant velocity U, the total kinetic energy transforms as
K′=∑i​2mi​​∣vi​−U∣2=K−U⋅P+21​MT​U2.
(3)
For an isolated collision, momentum conservation gives ΔP=0, and nonrelativistic mass conservation gives ΔMT​=0. Therefore
ΔK′=ΔK.​
(4)
An elastic collision with ΔK=0 in one inertial frame has ΔK′=0 in every inertial frame. Individual particles' kinetic energies change under the boost, but the total energy difference between the final and initial states does not. This is the Galilean invariance of collision elasticity.

In the frame following the turn, the outward inertial force mv2/R acts at height h and tends to unload the inner rail. The two upward normal forces satisfy
Nout​+Nin​=mg,2d​(Nout​−Nin​)=Rmv2h​.
(5)
The second equation is moment balance about the midpoint between the rails. Thus
Nin​=2mg​−Rdmv2h​,Nout​=2mg​+Rdmv2h​.
(6)
Full contact requires a nonnegative inner normal force. Its limiting value is zero, giving the toppling speed on level rails
v0​=2hgRd​​.​
(7)
For strictly positive contact force on both rails, v<v0​. This ideal result assumes level rails, negligible suspension motion and enough lateral contact force to follow the curve; lateral sliding could impose a different limit.

Let ue​>0 be the constant rearward rocket exhaust speed, and let M denote the rocket's instantaneous mass. Apply momentum conservation over a short ejection interval: the remaining rocket gains Mdv, while the expelled positive mass −dM carries backward relative momentum ue​dM. To first order,
Mdv=−ue​dM.
(8)
Integrating gives the rocket equation
Δv=ue​lnM−mM​.​
(9)
This is a velocity increment, measured along the thrust direction, for motion in free space without external forces.
For the two-stage vehicle, take M1​ and M2​ to be the initial masses of the respective stages, each including its fuel m. The first burn changes the total mass from M1​+M2​ to M1​+M2​−m. Discarding the empty first-stage shell of mass M1​−m leaves mass M2​; ideal jettison without a relative impulse does not change the velocity. The second burn then changes M2​ to M2​−m. Adding the two rocket equation increments gives
Δvtwo​=ue​lnM1​+M2​−mM1​+M2​​+ue​lnM2​−mM2​​.​
(10)
Here M1​>m and M2​>m leave positive dry masses. Different stage exhaust speeds would multiply their respective logarithms. A multistage rocket gains its advantage by avoiding acceleration of the spent first-stage shell during the second burn.

Choose the cylinder's velocity to be vx^, and the ball's laboratory velocity to be u(cosθ,sinθ). By Galilean velocity addition, the ball's velocity relative to the cylinder is
u′=(ucosθ−v, usinθ).
(11)
For a suitably centred entry without hitting a wall, the cylinder's axis must be parallel to this relative velocity. Its oriented angle α to the cylinder's direction of travel is therefore
α=atan2(usinθ,ucosθ−v).​
(12)
Equivalently, tanα=usinθ/(ucosθ−v), with the quadrant chosen from the two components. For v/u≪1,
α=θ+uv​sinθ+O((v/u)2).​
(13)
Thus the extra tilt relative to the ball's original direction is approximately (v/u)sinθ. The use of atan2 also covers nearly transverse incidence, where taking an unqualified inverse tangent could select the wrong axis direction.

Let θ describe the direction towards the star, measured from the Earth's positive direction of motion. The arriving photon propagates in the opposite direction, so in the stellar frame its four-momentum has
px​=−cEγ​​cosθ,py​=−cEγ​​sinθ.
(14)
A Lorentz transformation into the Earth's frame, moving at βc along x, gives
Eγ′​=γEγ​(1+βcosθ),px′​=−cγEγ​​(cosθ+β),py′​=py​.
(15)
The observed stellar bearing is opposite to the transformed photon momentum. Since a photon satisfies ∣p′∣=Eγ′​/c,
cosθ′=1+βcosθcosθ+β​,sinθ′=γ(1+βcosθ)sinθ​.​
(16)
This is relativistic aberration. For small β, θ′=θ−βsinθ+O(β2): stellar directions shift towards the direction of the observer's motion. Keeping the distinction between stellar bearing and photon propagation direction explains the plus signs.

For a Lorentz boost along x, the Lorentz transformation is
t′=γ(t−c2vx​),x′=γ(x−vt),y′=y,z′=z,γ=(1−v2/c2)−1/2.
(17)
To calculate a photograph, compare photons that arrive together, which were generally emitted at different times. Put the lamina in the xy plane, moving along +x, and take the distant camera to lie along negative y. Let (X,Y) be a material point's coordinates in the lamina's rest frame. At a laboratory time t, its position satisfies x=X/γ+vt, y=Y.
The distant-camera approximation makes the variable part of the light travel time Y/c. Equal arrival time therefore requires t+Y/c=constant. Choose that constant to be zero. The point's photographed transverse coordinate becomes
ximage​=γX​−βY.
(18)
A stationary lamina rotated in its plane by θ would instead have the projected coordinate Xcosθ−Ysinθ. The two projections agree when
cosθ=γ1​,sinθ=β,θ=arcsincv​.​
(19)
This is Terrell rotation. The shape defined by simultaneous laboratory positions still has length contraction; the photographic rotation comes from combining that contraction with unequal emission times. Reversing the viewing or motion direction reverses the apparent rotation's sign.

The parallel axis theorem gives I=ICM​+Md2 for parallel axes separated by d, with the reference axis through the centre of mass. For a plane lamina, the perpendicular axis theorem gives Iz​=Ix​+Iy​ for mutually perpendicular axes meeting at one point, with x,y in the plane.
Treat the removed material as negative mass. With the hole's centre at bx^, its moment of inertia about O is m(a2/2+b2). The remaining moment of inertia and centre of mass are
I1​=21​MR2−m(21​a2+b2),rCM,1​=−M−mmb​x^.​
(20)
For a uniform original disk, m/M=a2/R2. Write d1​=mb/(M−m) and measure ϕ from the downward equilibrium direction of its centre of mass. The physical pendulum then has Lagrangian
L1​=21​I1​ϕ˙​2+(M−m)gd1​cosϕ=21​I1​ϕ˙​2+mbgcosϕ.
(21)
The Euler-Lagrange equation is I1​ϕ¨​+mbgsinϕ=0. For small oscillations, sinϕ≃ϕ, giving
ω1​=I1​mbg​​,f1​=2πω1​​.​
(22)
Now add the removed small disk on the opposite side, with its centre a distance c=R−a from O so that it is internally tangent to the large disk. The total mass returns to M, while its first moment about O is −m(b+c)x^. Thus
I2​=I1​+m(21​a2+c2)=21​MR2+m(c2−b2),d2​=Mm(b+c)​.
(23)
Replacing both the inertia and gravitational restoring moment gives
ω2​=21​MR2+m[(R−a)2−b2]mg(b+R−a)​​,f2​=2πω2​​.​
(24)
The same first-moment method applies to a general perforated-disc pendulum. The added disk is attached to the lamina and shares its rotation; treating it as a massless patch or updating only the inertia would omit its restoring torque.

Let x be the downward displacement of M from its initial position just beyond the table edge. The smaller mass moves towards the edge by the same distance, and the hanging chain length is x. All the chain and both endpoint masses move at speed x˙, so their total moving mass is M=M+m+ρL. The hanging chain's gravitational potential energy, relative to the initial configuration, is −ρgx2/2. Thus the Lagrangian for the chain sliding over a table edge is
L=21​Mx˙2+Mgx+21​ρgx2.
(25)
The Euler-Lagrange equation gives Mx¨=g(M+ρx). Put Ω=ρg/M​. With x(0)=x˙(0)=0, the solution is
x(t)=ρM​[cosh(Ωt)−1],x˙(t)=ρMΩ​sinh(Ωt).​
(26)
These are also the smaller mass's displacement and speed towards the edge. The formula applies until M reaches the floor, at tf​=Ω−1arcosh(1+ρy/M). Since y<L, the smaller mass has not yet reached the edge during this interval. As ρ→0, the result tends to x=Mgt2/[2(M+m)], as expected for a massless connector.
Let the spring's spring constant be k, and write its extension as e0​+x, allowing for an initial extension e0​. The new Lagrangian is
Ls​=21​Mx˙2+Mgx+21​ρgx2−21​k(e0​+x)2.
(27)
The specified equilibrium is xe​=y−y1​, with k(e0​+xe​)=g(M+ρxe​). Put ξ=x−xe​; the equation becomes
Mξ¨​+(k−ρg)ξ=0,ω=M+m+ρLk−ρg​​,f=2πω​.​
(28)
The spring-stabilized chain over a table edge oscillates only when k>ρg. More hanging chain increases its own gravitational pull, reducing the effective restoring stiffness. The equilibrium height alone does not determine k without information about the spring's natural length or preload.

For the Hohmann transfer orbit, the inner and outer circular radii are the ellipse's pericentre and apocentre distances, so a=(r0​+r1​)/2. The vis-viva equation gives the transfer speed at the inner tangent point,
vp​=r0​μ​​r0​+r1​2r1​​​.

In a quasi-circular spiral transfer, a small tangential thrust F supplies power Fvc​ while the orbit stays approximately circular. Differentiating E=−μm/(2r) and using vc​=μ/r​ gives
mF​=2r3/2μ​​r˙,Δvslow​=∫mF​dt=μ​(r0​​1​−r1​​1​)=v0​−v1​.​
(34)
This is the integrated thrust magnitude per unit constant mass, not the net vector change of orbital velocity.
For comparison, write u=r1​/r0​​>1. Adding the two Hohmann transfer impulses and subtracting the slow-transfer result yields
v0​ΔvH​−Δvslow​​=uu−1​[u2+1​2​(u+1)​−2]<0.
(35)
The bracket is negative because (u+1)2<2(u2+1) for u>1. Thus the Hohmann transfer uses less total impulse than the slow circular spiral for these ideal coplanar transfers. The Hohmann transfer compared with a slow spiral does not establish a global optimum over every possible transfer family or propulsion constraint.

Use the supplied planet mass 5.9×1024kg, G=6.67430×10−11m3kg−1s−2, and adopt mean Earth radius RE​=6371km. For a geostationary orbit, use one sidereal day, T≃86164s. The adopted radius and rotation period follow NASA's fact sheet.[ref]
r0​=RE​+300km=6.671×106m,r1​=(4π2μT2​)1/3≃4.1994×107m.
(36)
The slightly smaller radius than the usual terrestrial value follows from using the question's smaller Earth mass. For coplanar prograde orbits, the preceding formulas give
Δv1​≃2.410kms−1,Δv2​≃1.459kms−1,ΔvH​≃3.869kms−1.​
(37)
The escape transfer has a→∞, so its first tangential burn reaches the local escape speed 2μ/r0​​. No final circularization at infinity is needed:
Δvescape​=(2​−1)r0​μ​​≃3.182kms−1.​
(38)
Thus the geostationary transfer requires about 0.687kms−1 more total impulse, or about 22 percent more, despite leaving the spacecraft gravitationally bound. Reaching the outer radius and circularizing there are separate requirements. Changing the starting orbit's inclination would add a plane-change cost absent from this calculation.

Write me​ for the electron rest mass and ε=me​c2. The incident positron has total energy E and satisfies p2c2=E2−ε2. Adding the target electron's four-momentum, the squared total centre-of-momentum energy is
W2=(E+ε)2−p2c2=2ε(E+ε).
(39)
At the fixed-target production threshold, all three final particles are at rest relative to one another, so W=3M0​c2. Consequently
Emin​=2me​c29M02​c4​−me​c2.​
(40)
This is the positron's total energy, not its kinetic energy. It assumes 3M0​≥2me​ and addresses kinematic allowance; other selection rules could still forbid a proposed reaction. For M0​c2=135MeV and me​c2≃0.510999MeV, Emin​≃160.494GeV.
At threshold the three products share the centre-of-momentum frame's laboratory velocity. Their common Lorentz factor and speed are
γCM​=3M0​c2Emin​+ε​=2me​3M0​​≃396.283,v=c1−9M02​4me2​​​≃0.999996816c.​
(41)
For the subsequent two-photon decay, each photon has rest-frame energy Eγ∗​=M0​c2/2. A Lorentz transformation of a photon emitted at rest-frame angle θ∗ to the boost gives Eγ​=γCM​Eγ∗​(1+βCM​cosθ∗). Therefore the kinematically allowed laboratory range is
2γCM​M0​c2​(1−βCM​)≤Eγ​≤2γCM​M0​c2​(1+βCM​),​
(42)
or numerically
0.08517MeV ≲Eγ​≲ 5.3498×104MeV.​
(43)
The two photon energies in a single event are correlated: their sum is the parent's laboratory energy γCM​M0​c2. When one attains the upper endpoint, the other attains the lower endpoint. Angular distributions and detector acceptance can affect which part of this range is observed.
