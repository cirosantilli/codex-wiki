Neglecting the planets' masses compared with the Sun's mass, Newton's law of universal gravitation supplies the centripetal acceleration of each circular orbit:
r2GM⊙​​=T24π2r​.
(1)
Thus Kepler's third law, calibrated by the Earth's orbital period of one year and orbital radius of one astronomical unit, gives
1yrT​=(1AUr​)3/2,TJ​=5.23/2=11.86yr,TS​=9.53/2=29.28yr.​
(2)
Both orbits are taken to have the same direction. Their relative angular speed is 2π(1/TJ​−1/TS​), so the Sun-centred interval between successive conjunctions is the synodic period
Tsyn​=1/TJ​−1/TS​1​=19.93yr.​
(3)
Using the supplied rounded orbital radii explains the small difference from the commonly quoted approximately twenty-year interval.
From the Earth, the two lines of sight are displaced differently because Jupiter and Saturn are at different distances. The Earth's annual motion along its orbit therefore shifts the dates of apparent conjunctions by amounts of order months. The broad recurrence remains about twenty years, but successive apparent conjunctions need not be equally spaced: apparent retrograde motion can produce a group of three conjunctions separated by months during one encounter. The Earth's daily rotation adds a much smaller change of viewpoint. This is a geometrical comparison in the stipulated coplanar model; daylight and the horizon can prevent a particular conjunction from being visible at a chosen surface location.

Choose the vertical z axis downward, and measure the polar angle θ from it. With azimuth ϕ and fixed rod length r, the Cartesian coordinates are
x=rsinθcosϕ,y=rsinθsinϕ,z=rcosθ.​
(13)
The massless rod has no kinetic energy. Differentiating these coordinates and adding the squared components of velocity yields
v2=x˙2+y˙​2+z˙2=r2(θ˙2+sin2θϕ˙​2).
(14)
The gravitational potential energy is V=−mgz=−mgrcosθ, up to an arbitrary constant. The Lagrangian of this spherical pendulum is therefore
L=21​mr2(θ˙2+sin2θϕ˙​2)+mgrcosθ.​
(15)
Applying the Euler-Lagrange equation to the two generalized coordinates gives
mr2θ¨−mr2sinθcosθϕ˙​2+mgrsinθ=0,dtd​(mr2sin2θϕ˙​)=0.
(16)
Equivalently, away from the coordinate singularities at the poles,
θ¨=sinθcosθϕ˙​2−rg​sinθ,ϕ¨​+2cotθθ˙ϕ˙​=0.​
(17)
Since L is independent of ϕ, the azimuth is a cyclic coordinate. Its conjugate momentum

Orient the x axis along the displacement from the earlier event to the later one, so Δx=d=1000m and Δt=10−6s. For a parallel Lorentz transformation, simultaneity in the second inertial frame requires
Δt′=γ(Δt−c2vΔx​)=0,v=dc2Δt​=8.99×107ms−1≃0.300c.​
(19)
The second inertial frame moves towards the later event's location. The positive value d2−c2Δt2 establishes spacelike separation, which permits this change of simultaneity. For a general velocity V, the condition is V⋅Δr=c2Δt: only its component along the separation is fixed at the displayed value, and transverse components are allowed provided ∣V∣<c. Thus the parallel velocity has the smallest possible speed.
A physical inertial frame must have ∣v∣<c, giving
∣Δt∣<cd​=3.336μs.​
(20)
There is no attained maximum: d/c is the supremum. For the concrete boundary case Δt=1000m/c, the events have null separation and the formula would require v=c; no inertial frame can make them simultaneous.

Inertia is resistance to a change of motion. Translational inertia is measured by mass in Newton's second law; rotational inertia about a specified fixed axis is measured by the moment of inertia
I=∫r⊥2​dm,
(26)
where r⊥​ is perpendicular distance from that axis. For a thin uniform rod, take axes perpendicular to the rod. With linear mass density λ=m/l,
ICM​=lm​∫−l/2l/2​x2dx=12ml2​,Iend​=ICM​+m(2l​)2=3ml2​.​
(27)
The second result uses the parallel axis theorem; direct integration over 0≤x≤l gives the same value. The axis specification is essential: an ideal thin rod has zero moment of inertia about its own longitudinal axis.
During the short elastic collision, neglect the impulse from gravity. The pivot can exert an external impulse, so total linear momentum of rod plus particle need not be conserved. Its torque about the pivot is zero, so conservation of angular momentum about that point applies. Let w be the particle's signed outgoing horizontal velocity, and ω the rod's outgoing angular velocity, positive towards the incident motion. Then
mlv0​=mlw+Iend​ω,21​mv02​=21​mw2+21​Iend​ω2.
(28)
Substituting ω=3(v0​−w)/l into the kinetic energy equation gives
v02​=w2+3(v0​−w)2,(v0​−w)(v0​−2w)=0.
(29)
Discarding the zero-impulse branch w=v0​, the actual collision gives
w=2v0​​forward,ω=2l3v0​​.​
(30)
The rod's lower end initially moves forward at lω=3v0​/2, so the bodies separate. Afterwards the free particle undergoes projectile motion. With the pivot as origin and z downward, its trajectory is x=(v0​/2)t, z=l+gt2/2, where t=0 at the collision. The rod instead swings about the fixed pivot in a vertical plane as a physical pendulum.
The rod's initial rotational kinetic energy is Iend​ω2/2=3mv02​/8. Reaching the horizontal raises its centre of mass by l/2, increasing gravitational potential energy by mgl/2. Consequently,
v02​≥34gl​ to reach the horizontal,v0​=34gl​​ to just reach it at rest.​
(31)
For the threshold case, measure the rod's angle θ from the downward vertical and reset t=0 at its first horizontal turning point, so θ(0)=π/2 and θ˙(0)=0. The restoring torque about the pivot gives the exact equation of motion
θ¨+2l3g​sinθ=0.​
(32)
At the horizontal, θ¨=−3g/(2l), so the rod immediately swings back. Conservation of mechanical energy yields
21​3ml2​θ˙2+2mgl​(1−cosθ)=2mgl​,θ˙2=l3g​cosθ.
(33)
On the initial descent its explicit time dependence is specified by the definite integral
t=3gl​​∫θ(t)π/2​cosα​dα​.​
(34)
It continues through the vertical to −π/2, reverses, and repeats without damping. An alternative expression valid throughout the oscillation uses the Jacobi elliptic sine. Put k=1/2​, ωr​=3g/(2l)​, and let K(k) denote the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. Then
θ(t)=2arcsin[ksn(K(k)−ωr​t,k)],Tr​=43g2l​​K(2​1​).​
(35)
This is not simple harmonic motion at an amplitude of π/2. Its oscillation period exceeds its own small-amplitude value 2π2l/(3g)​ by the factor 2K(1/2​)/π≃1.18034.
A simple pendulum of length l/2 obeys θ¨+(2g/l)sinθ=0. At the same amplitude π/2, its oscillation period is Tp​=4l/(2g)​K(1/2​), so
Tp​Tr​​=3​2​>1.​
(36)
The rod swings more slowly. Its equivalent simple pendulum length is Iend​/[m(l/2)]=2l/3, not the distance l/2 to its centre of mass. The same oscillation period ratio holds in the common small-amplitude limit, but replacing the actual 90-degree motion by that limit would underestimate both oscillation periods.

For a satellite of mass m moving in the Newtonian gravitational field of a much larger, effectively fixed mass M, put μ=GM. Its conserved mechanical energy, with gravitational potential energy zero at infinity, is
E=21​mv2−rGMm​=−2aGMm​.​
(37)
Here r is the instantaneous centre-to-centre distance, v the satellite's speed, G the gravitational constant, and a the semi-major axis of its bound elliptic orbit. The kinetic energy and potential energy vary around the orbit, while their sum remains constant. Dividing by m and solving for v gives the vis-viva equation, v2=μ(2/r−1/a).
For this Hohmann transfer orbit, the periapsis and apoapsis distances are r1​=1AU and r2​=1.52AU, so
a=2r1​+r2​​=1.26AU,v1​=r1​μ​​.
(38)
The vis-viva equation gives the endpoint speeds
vp​=v1​2−1.261​​=v1​6376​​,va​=v1​1.522​−1.261​​.​
(39)
As a check, conservation of angular momentum at the two endpoints gives r1​vp​=r2​va​ because each velocity is perpendicular to its radius. Using 1AU=1.49598×1011m and μ=1.32712×1020m3s−2 gives v1​=29.78kms−1 and
vp​=32.71kms−1,va​=21.52kms−1.​
(40)
The departure impulse must increase the speed in the direction of the initial circular orbit:
Δv1​=vp​−v1​=2.93kms−1.​
(41)
These are Sun-centred speeds; leaving the Earth's own gravitational field would require an additional calculation beyond this idealized solar orbit transfer.
At r2​, the desired circular orbit speed is v2​=μ/r2​​=24.16kms−1. The arrival impulse is therefore
Δv2​=v2​−va​=2.64kms−1.​
(42)
The required thrust on the spacecraft is forward, tangent to its orbit, to accelerate it. The rocket ejects exhaust backwards, opposite to the spacecraft's velocity; this distinguishes the direction of thrust from the direction in which the exhaust is fired.
The Hohmann transfer time is half the orbital period of the transfer ellipse. From Kepler's third law, relative to the Earth's one-year orbital period,
tH​=21​(1.26)3/2yr=0.7072yr≃258days.
(43)
Meanwhile Mars's orbital period is TM​=1.523/2yr=1.8740yr. The spacecraft arrives π radians from its departure position. For a rendezvous, Mars must reach that same position after advancing through 2πtH​/TM​. Thus its initial lead angle over the departing spacecraft is
ϕ0​=π−TM​2πtH​​=π[1−(1.521.26​)3/2]=44.15∘.​
(44)
Mars must be ahead by this angle in the common direction of motion around their orbits. At departure the spacecraft and the Earth share the inner orbital position; arrival occurs on the opposite side of the Sun.

The laboratory is the parent's rest frame. In this relativistic two-body decay, conservation of momentum gives equal and opposite daughter momenta, of magnitude p. Their total relativistic energies satisfy
E1​+E2​=4mc2,E12​=p2c2+m2c4,E22​=p2c2+4m2c4.
(56)
Subtracting the relativistic energy-momentum relations eliminates p:
(E2​−E1​)(E2​+E1​)=3m2c4,E2​−E1​=43​mc2.
(57)
Solving the two linear equations gives
E1​=813​mc2,E2​=819​mc2,p=8105​​mc.
(58)
Since three-momentum obeys pc2=Ev, the daughter speeds are
v1​=13105​​c=0.7882c,v2​=19105​​c=0.5393c.​
(59)
Their directions are opposite; the initial state does not specify their common axis. The kinetic energies are K1​=5mc2/8 and K2​=3mc2/8. Their sum mc2 is the loss of rest energy when the total daughter rest mass is 3m instead of 4m.
The proper mean lifetime τ0​ is the mean lifetime measured in the unstable particle's own rest frame, or equivalently the mean elapsed proper time before decay. In the exponential decay model, its survival probability to proper time τ is e−τ/τ0​. For the daughter of rest mass m, the Lorentz factor is γ1​=E1​/(mc2)=13/8, so time dilation gives a laboratory mean lifetime of γ1​τ0​=3.25μs.
