The disc carries mass Md​=2M/3 and the thin rim carries Mr​=M/3. About the wheel's central normal axis, add the moment of inertia of a uniform disc and the thin rim's moment of inertia:
Iz​=21​Md​R2+Mr​R2=21​32M​R2+3M​R2=32​MR2.​
(2)
Treat the wheel as a thin planar lamina, as implied by the disc-and-thin-rim model. Interpret the requested perpendicular axis as perpendicular to the wheel's original axis and tangent to the rim: if the wheel lies in the xy plane, choose the tangent line x=R,z=0, parallel to the y diameter. By rotational symmetry, the two diametral moments of inertia are equal. The perpendicular axis theorem therefore gives
Ix​=Iy​=2Iz​​=31​MR2.
(3)
The parallel axis theorem then gives the tangent-axis inertia of an axisymmetric lamina:
Itangent,in plane​=Iy​+MR2=34​MR2.​
(4)
The axis direction matters. If “perpendicular” were intended to mean perpendicular to the wheel's plane, an axis through a rim point would instead be parallel to the original wheel axis, giving Irim,normal​=Iz​+MR2=5MR2/3. The 4MR2/3 answer uses a tangent axis perpendicular to the original axis; the 5MR2/3 value belongs to the different, explicitly identified normal axis. Finite wheel thickness would require additional information for the in-plane moment of inertia.

3 OurBigBook.com
Let r be the orbiting point mass's position relative to the attracting body, and let its velocity be v=r˙. Its angular momentum is
L=mr×r˙.
(5)
Conservation of angular momentum keeps both the magnitude and direction of L fixed. Since L is perpendicular to r, a nonradial orbit lies in a fixed plane. During a short interval dt, the radius vector sweeps a thin triangle of area
dA=21​∣r×dr∣=21​∣r×r˙∣dt.
(6)
Thus the areal velocity is constant:
dtdA​=2mL​,ΔA=2mL​Δt.​
(7)
Equal time intervals give equal swept areas: this is Kepler's second law. In plane polar coordinates, the same statement is L=mr2ϕ˙​ and A˙=r2ϕ˙​/2. The attracting body is at the relevant focus of the ellipse, not its geometric centre. The area argument needs conserved angular momentum, not specifically an ellipse; a central force provides this conservation because its torque r×F vanishes.

Choose the M ball's initial velocity as +v; the 3M ball then has velocity −v. Both have Lorentz factor γ=(1−v2/c2)−1/2. Their total relativistic energy and signed relativistic momentum are
Ei​=4γMc2,pi​=γMv−3γMv=−2γMv.
(8)
The laboratory frame is not the centre-of-momentum frame, despite the equal and opposite speeds. Four-momentum conservation and the relativistic energy-momentum relation give the invariant mass Mf​ of the combined incoming system:
Mf2​c4=Ei2​−c2pi2​=γ2M2c4(16−4β2),β=cv​.
(9)
The relativistic collision mass threshold for a final object of rest mass 5M is Mf​≥5M. At the threshold,
1−β216−4β2​=25⟹21β2=9,vmin​=c73​​≃0.65465c.​
(10)
At equality, the outgoing object has laboratory velocity V=c2pi​/Ei​=−v/2, directed towards the initially heavier ball's motion. Its nonzero final kinetic energy is why setting 4γMc2=5Mc2 would give the wrong threshold.
For the literal rest mass of a coalescing relativistic collision, if there is exactly one outgoing object of exactly fixed rest mass 5M and no radiation or other products, four-momentum conservation selects the equality above, rather than allowing every larger v. Speeds above this threshold require a larger final invariant mass or additional outgoing energy carriers.

Take the initial internal pressure as negligible and the external atmospheric pressure as p0​=1.01325×105Pa. The window has frontal area A=π(0.400m)2=0.50265m2. Before appreciable air has entered, its inward pressure force is approximately p0​A=5.09×104N. Newton's second law therefore gives the initial collective inward acceleration
a0​≃Mp0​A​=4.63×102ms−2≃47g.​
(11)
Here M=110kg and g≃9.81ms−2. Neglect gravity in this much larger pressure-driven acceleration, together with supports and elastic stresses once the glass has broken. The given thickness d=0.080m implies a plausible average glass mass density M/(Ad)≃2.74×103kgm−3. Equivalently, a fragment retaining the original thickness has acceleration p0​/(ρg​d), independent of its frontal area while it experiences the full pressure difference.
For a vacuum-window implosion energy estimate, the ideal pressure-work scale for filling vessel volume V=1.5m3 is W≃p0​V. Assigning all that pressure-volume work to the glass's kinetic energy gives
21​Mvest2​≃p0​V,vest​≃M2p0​V​​≃5.26×101ms−1.​
(12)
The same result follows from retaining acceleration a0​ over an effective swept distance V/A≃2.98m, so that vest2​=2a0​V/A. This is an ideal upper energy-budget estimate for the common speed, or mass-weighted root mean square speed, of all the glass. It assumes an atmospheric reservoir maintains the driving pressure and that the whole vessel's pressure-work budget can accelerate the glass. In practice, air motion, fragmentation, heating and collisions share that energy, the pressure difference falls, and fragments may hit the vessel after a much shorter path. The unspecified vessel shape and fragment trajectories prevent a unique actual maximum speed; concentrating energy into one small fragment also prevents interpreting this collective estimate as a strict bound on every individual fragment.

There is no external horizontal force, so momentum conservation keeps the student–punt centre of mass fixed if the system starts at rest. Let the student's walking direction be positive. If the punt's displacement relative to the water is X, the student advances X+L, where L=3m is measured relative to the punt. Constancy of the centre of mass gives
Mp​X+ms​(X+L)=0,X=−Mp​+ms​ms​L​=−150+8080×3​m≃−1.04m.​
(13)
The punt moves 1.04m opposite to the student's walking direction; the student's net displacement relative to the water is 3+X≃1.96m. These displacements are relative to the initial state, not distances travelled relative to the punt.
Under the same ideal assumptions, merely changing the walking speed or walking back more slowly cannot produce unlimited travel. Returning the student to the original place on the punt restores the original internal mass distribution and hence the punt's original position. A finite punt bounds the displacement available through internal rearrangement. Propulsion over large distances requires an external horizontal impulse, for example a push against the bed or momentum transferred to the water. Such an interaction changes the isolated, frictionless model rather than evading centre of mass conservation.

sBA2​=(Δx)2−c2(Δt)2≃36−9=27m2>0.
(14)

B cannot cause A.​
(15)

Descent equation. Take downward velocity v and underwater depth z as positive, with z=0 at the lake surface. For a sphere of radius r, the pebble mass is m=4πr3ρp​/3 and the frontal area is A=πr2. Newton's second law includes downward gravitational weight, upward buoyancy and upward quadratic drag:
mv˙=mg−ρw​34πr3​g−21​Cρw​πr2v2(v≥0).
(18)
Thus
v˙=g(1−ρp​ρw​​)−bv2,z˙=v,b=8ρp​r3Cρw​​.​
(19)
Here b has units of inverse length. For arbitrary signed velocity, the last term is −bv∣v∣, so drag always opposes motion. The displayed downward form suffices for this trajectory.
Limiting speed. At terminal velocity, the net force and v˙ vanish. For ρp​>ρw​,
vt​=3Cρw​8rg(ρp​−ρw​)​​.​
(20)
Use ρp​=2500kgm−3, ρw​=1000kgm−3, r=0.020m, C=0.7 and g=9.81ms−2. Then
b=5.25m−1,geff​=g(1−ρw​/ρp​)=5.886ms−2,vt​≃1.059ms−1.​
(21)
The ρp​−ρw​ factor is essential: omitting buoyancy would overestimate the limiting speed.
For the numerical descent, assume the pebble is released from rest, neglect air drag, take g and the water mass density as constant, and apply the given constant drag coefficient once it is submerged. Neglect the finite entry interval, entry energy losses and added mass, so its initial underwater speed is
u=2gHa​​=2g×5m​≃9.905ms−1>vt​.
(22)
It consequently slows down towards terminal velocity; a rest-starting underwater trajectory would describe a different initial condition.

Orbital energy. Treat the star as stationary to leading order in m/M, with Newtonian gravitational parameter μ=GM. For velocity components in plane polar coordinates, v2=r˙2+r2ϕ˙​2. The kinetic energy is mv2/2. Integrating Newton's law of universal gravitation with zero gravitational potential energy at infinite separation gives U(r)=−GMm/r. Thus
E=21​mr˙2+21​mr2ϕ˙​2−rGMm​=21​mr˙2+2mr2J2​−rGMm​,​
(32)
where J=mr2ϕ˙​ is the satellite's angular momentum. The first term is radial kinetic energy; the second is transverse kinetic energy, also the centrifugal contribution to the radial effective potential; the last is the attractive gravitational potential energy. These are not three independent sources of energy: the centrifugal term is already part of the ordinary kinetic energy.
For an elliptic Kepler orbit of semi-major axis a and orbital eccentricity e, the polar equation of a Kepler orbit r=p/(1+ecosϕ) has semilatus rectum p=J2/(GMm2)=a(1−e2). Substituting r˙=(Je/(mp))sinϕ into the preceding expression reduces it to
E=2pGMm​(e2−1),E=−2aGMm​.​
(33)
For a circular Kepler orbit, a=R, v02​=GM/R, K=GMm/(2R), and U=−GMm/R. Negative total energy describes a bound orbit.
Rapid, symmetric mass loss. Assume the mass is removed on a timescale short compared with the satellite's orbital period, without an impulsive kick or appreciable wind drag on the satellite. The post-loss gravitational parameter is μ′=G(1−f)M with f=0.01. At the change, r=R, r˙=0, and v=v0​=GM/R​ are unchanged. Spherical symmetry keeps the force central, so the specific angular momentum j=J/m=GMR​ is conserved. Orbital energy is not conserved across the changing gravitational field:
ε′=mE′​=21​RGM​−RG(1−f)M​=−2RGM​(1−2f).
(34)
The new semi-major axis is consequently
a′=−2ε′μ′​=1−2f1−f​R.
(35)
The original speed now exceeds the new circular speed at R, and the outward radial acceleration is v02​/R−μ′/R2=fGM/R2>0. Thus the release point is the new pericentre, not the apocentre. The impulsive orbital expansion under isotropic mass loss gives
rmin​=R,rmax​=2a′−R=1−2fR​,e′=1−ff​.
(36)
For the stated one-percent loss,
rmin​=R,rmax​=4950​R≃1.02041R,e′=991​.​
(37)
An independent angular momentum check is j2=μ′a′(1−e′2)=GMR. The impulsive orbit stays bound for f<1/2; loss of half the central mass is the zero-orbital energy limit.
What the wind timescale changes. The source specifies a spherically symmetric wind but does not give its duration. The preceding nearest and furthest distances require the rapid-loss assumption. In the opposite, slow limit, adiabatic orbital expansion under isotropic mass loss preserves an initially circular orbit to leading adiabatic order. Conserved specific angular momentum then gives j2=GM(t)r(t), hence M(t)r(t)=MR. After the same loss,
rmin​≃rmax​≃1−fR​=99100​R≃1.01010R(adiabatic limit).​
(38)
For a finite intermediate timescale, the orbit depends on the mass-loss history; the percentage alone does not select a unique pair of turning distances. Neglecting wind recoil and wind drag is common to both idealizations.

Use spherical coordinates about the fixed pivot, with fixed rod length l, polar angle θ measured from the downward vertical, and azimuth ϕ. The bob's position is
r=l(sinθcosϕ, sinθsinϕ, −cosθ)
(39)
when the Cartesian z axis points upward. Differentiation gives kinetic energy T=ml2(θ˙2+sin2θϕ˙​2)/2. Taking zero gravitational potential energy at pivot height gives V=−mglcosθ. The spherical pendulum's Lagrangian is therefore
L=T−V=21​ml2(θ˙2+sin2θϕ˙​2)+mglcosθ.​
(40)
The rod is light, rigid and of fixed length; pivot friction and air drag are neglected. A rigid rod, unlike a string, may sustain either tensile or compressive constraint force.

The azimuth ϕ is a cyclic coordinate, since ∂L/∂ϕ=0. Its Euler-Lagrange equation gives conservation of the vertical component of angular momentum:
h=pϕ​=∂ϕ˙​∂L​=ml2sin2θϕ˙​=constant.​
(41)
This h is the actual angular momentum, not specific angular momentum. The Lagrangian also has no explicit time dependence. The conserved energy is

(42)

Constant azimuth. If ϕ is constant, ϕ˙​=0 and the motion is a planar simple pendulum:
θ¨+lg​sinθ=0.​
(45)
For the small-angle approximation, sinθ≃θ, giving simple harmonic motion with
ωplane​=lg​​,Tplane​=2πgl​​.​
(46)
The harmonic angular frequency requires small amplitude. At finite libration amplitude θmax​<π, the exact oscillation period is 4l/g​K(sin(θmax​/2)), where K is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind; the nonlinear pendulum does not have the same amplitude-independent period.
Constant polar angle. If θ=θ0​ is nonzero and constant, the bob traces a horizontal circle: this is a conical pendulum. Setting θ¨=0 and dividing by sinθ0​ gives
Ω2cosθ0​=lg​,ϕ˙​=Ω=±lcosθ0​g​​,0<θ0​<2π​.​
(47)
The sign distinguishes the two senses of rotation. The exactly downward configuration θ0​=0 is stationary; its azimuth is undefined and it must not be obtained by dividing by sinθ0​. A nontrivial constant-angle orbit of this gravity-only system needs cosθ0​>0 even though the supporting rod can also push.

Energy–momentum transformations and invariant. Let the primed inertial frame move at signed velocity u along the unprimed x axis. With Γ=(1−u2/c2)−1/2, a Lorentz transformation of four-momentum gives
E′=Γ(E−upx​),px′​=Γ(px​−c2uE​),py′​=py​,pz′​=pz​.​
(54)
Use p2=px2​+py2​+pz2​. The longitudinal terms satisfy
E′2−c2px′2​=Γ2[(E−upx​)2−c2(px​−c2uE​)2]=Γ2(1−u2/c2)(E2−c2px2​)=E2−c2px2​.
(55)
The transverse momenta are unchanged, so
E′2−c2p′2=E2−c2p2.​
(56)
For one electron, the relativistic energy-momentum relation identifies this Lorentz invariant as E02​=me2​c4.
Symmetric electron scattering. Assume an isolated elastic collision of the two electrons, with no emitted radiation. The incident electron has total energy E and momentum magnitude p, so p2c2=E2−E02​; the target electron initially contributes rest energy E0​ and zero momentum. In the nondegenerate configuration with final track angles +θ and −θ, transverse momentum conservation gives
p1​sinθ=p2​sinθ⟹p1​=p2​=pf​.
(57)
Because both final particles have the same rest mass, they also have equal total energies. Conservation of energy gives
Ef​=2E+E0​​,pf2​c2=Ef2​−E02​=4(E−E0​)(E+3E0​)​.
(58)
Longitudinal momentum conservation is p=2pf​cosθ. Combining these expressions, with E>E0​, proves the symmetric relativistic equal-mass scattering angle relation:
cos2θ=(E−E0​)(E+3E0​)E2−E02​​=E+3E0​E+E0​​,θ=arccosE+3E0​E+E0​​​=arctanE+E0​2E0​​​.​
(59)
The low-kinetic energy limit has θ→45∘, recovering the familiar right angle between the outgoing tracks in nonrelativistic equal-mass scattering. Relativistic incident energies give θ<45∘, approaching zero at very high energy.
Accelerating potential. For θ=30∘, tan2θ=1/3=2E0​/(E+E0​), so E=5E0​. The acceleration from rest supplies kinetic energy, not total energy. If e>0 is the elementary charge, the required accelerating electric potential difference has magnitude
∣V∣=eE−E0​​=e4E0​​≃2.044×106V=2.044MV,​
(60)
using electron rest energy E0​≃0.511MeV. The electric potential difference is oriented to accelerate the negative electron; the result above specifies its magnitude.
