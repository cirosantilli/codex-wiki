The parallel axis theorem relates the moment of inertia about an axis through the centre of mass to that about a parallel axis displaced by perpendicular distance d:
I=IG​+Md2.​
(1)
The reference axis must pass through the centre of mass. To see why, write the displacement from it as r=R+d in the plane perpendicular to the axes. Expanding ∫∣R+d∣2dm gives IG​+Md2 because ∫Rdm=0.
For a thin planar mass distribution, let x,y be perpendicular axes in its plane and z the normal axis through their same intersection. The perpendicular axis theorem states
Iz​=Ix​+Iy​.​
(2)
Indeed, Iz​=∫(x2+y2)dm, while Ix​=∫y2dm and Iy​=∫x2dm. Their common intersection need not be the centre of mass, but an arbitrary three-dimensional body does not satisfy the planar equality.

Take the square to have uniform surface mass density σ=M/a2, and put its centre at x=y=0. For an in-plane axis through the centre parallel to a side, direct integration gives
Ix​=a2M​∫−a/2a/2​∫−a/2a/2​y2dxdy=12Ma2​.
(3)
The same holds for Iy​, and the mixed integral ∫xydm vanishes by reflection symmetry. The squared perpendicular distance to an axis making angle ψ with the x axis is (−xsinψ+ycosψ)2. Its integral therefore gives
Iψ​=12Ma2​(sin2ψ+cos2ψ)=12Ma2​.
(4)
Thus a diagonal through opposite corners and its perpendicular through the centre have
Idiagonal​=Icentral perpendicular​=12Ma2​.​
(5)
This is the in-plane inertia isotropy of a square plate.
Axis-location qualification: perpendicularity fixes a direction, not a line's position. If the second axis is instead shifted by distance d from the centre, the parallel axis theorem gives I2​=Ma2/12+Md2. A perpendicular through a corner on that diagonal has d=a/2​ and hence I2​=7Ma2/12, a concrete different value satisfying the stated directional description. If “through the corners” means an edge rather than a diagonal, that edge has I=Ma2/3; two adjacent edges both have that value. The general displaced-axis formula and these choices make the location convention explicit rather than silently assigning a unique value to an unspecified second axis.

In the centre-of-mass frame, the two equal incoming rest masses have opposite momenta and total energy 2E. The squared total four-momentum fixes the composite invariant mass M:
M2c4=Etot2​−c2∣Ptot​∣2=4E2,M=c22E​.​
(10)
A change of inertial frame, including going to either particle's rest frame, cannot change this invariant mass. It includes the internal kinetic energy and is generally larger than 2M1​.
There is a terminology convention worth making explicit. The older “relativistic mass” means frame-dependent total energy divided by c2. To stop particle 1 requires a boost with Lorentz factor γ=E/(M1​c2), so the system has total energy 2γE=2E2/(M1​c2) in that frame. Under that alternative convention, its relativistic mass would be 2E2/(M1​c4). This is not its invariant mass; both quantities coincide only when E=M1​c2 and the incoming particles already have zero relative motion.

Four-momentum conservation fixes the outgoing system's invariant mass to the same 2E/c2. In the rest frame of particle 3, let particle 4 have energy E4′​ and momentum p4′​. Each outgoing particle has rest mass M2​, so
4E2=(M2​c2+E4′​)2−c2∣p4′​∣2=2M22​c4+2M2​c2E4′​.
(11)
Consequently
E4′​=M2​c22E2​−M2​c2.​
(12)
Here E4′​ is the total energy, including rest energy. At the production threshold E=M2​c2, it reduces to M2​c2, as it should. Production is kinematically possible only for E≥M2​c2. The initial rest mass M1​ drops out because the conserved total centre-of-mass energy, rather than the identity of the incoming particles, determines this outgoing relative motion.

Let the source's proper emission period be T0​, and let it approach a stationary observer directly with speed v=βc>0. In the observer's inertial frame, consecutive emissions are separated by γT0​ through time dilation. The second crest starts vγT0​ nearer the observer, reducing the arrival interval to
Tobs​=γT0​(1−β).
(13)
Since light travels at c in each inertial frame, the longitudinal relativistic Doppler effect is
ν0​νobs​​=1−β1+β​​,λ0​λobs​​=1+β1−β​​.​
(14)
Recession reverses the sign of β. More generally, if Θ is the angle between the source velocity and the photon propagation direction in the observer's frame, a Lorentz transformation of the photon four-momentum gives ν0​=γνobs​(1−βcosΘ).
The common Doppler scaling of spectral-line separations follows because every line receives the same wavelength factor D. The supplied wavelengths have rest separation 50nm; the measured separation gives D=51/50=1.02. Defining βrec​>0 for recession, inversion gives
βrec​=D2+1D2−1​=5101101​=0.0198000,vrec​=5.93590×106ms−1.​
(15)
Source contradiction: a strictly approaching source has D<1 and must reduce the wavelength separation below 50nm, so no positive approach speed satisfies the stated 51nm. For example, approach at 0.02c gives a separation of 49.0098nm. The speed inferred from the numerical data is therefore about 0.0198c receding; retaining the word “approaching” would require a negative signed approach velocity. The two observed wavelengths consistent with the given separation are 357 and 408nm.

Measure the vertical displacement x downward from the spring's unstretched position. The kinetic energy is mx˙2/2, while the spring and gravitational potential energies are kx2/2 and −mgx. Thus the Lagrangian is
L=21​mx˙2−21​kx2+mgx.​
(16)
Applying the Euler-Lagrange equation, d(∂L/∂x˙)/dt−∂L/∂x=0, gives
mx¨+kx=mg.​
(17)
Gravity shifts the equilibrium to x∗​=mg/k. For displacement ξ=x−x∗​ from that equilibrium, the equation of motion becomes ξ¨​+(k/m)ξ=0, giving simple harmonic motion with angular frequency ω=k/m​. Choosing an upward coordinate instead reverses the gravitational term, but leaves the equilibrium-relative angular frequency unchanged.

Let the initial velocity be v and the two final velocities be u,w. In the stated nonrelativistic elastic collision, conservation of momentum and conservation of energy give
v=u+w,v2=u2+w2.
(18)
Squaring the first equation and comparing it with the second yields
v2=u2+w2+2u⋅w,u⋅w=0.​
(19)
If both final velocities are nonzero, their directions are perpendicular. If one is zero, the moving particle retains the initial velocity and a scattering angle for the stationary particle is undefined. This includes the zero-deflection and head-on exchange limits rather than incorrectly assigning a direction to a zero vector. Equal masses and a stationary initial target are essential to the dot-product cancellation.

Use coordinates s down the inclined plane and n along its upward normal, with the launch point as origin. Gravity has components gsinϕ and −gcosϕ. For launch angle θ above the downhill direction,
s(t)=v0​cosθt+21​gsinϕt2,n(t)=v0​sinθt−21​gcosϕt2.
(20)
The nonzero root of n(t)=0 is tf​=2v0​sinθ/(gcosϕ). Substituting into s(t) and combining the two trigonometric terms gives the distance along the slope:
Rslope​=gcos2ϕ2v02​sinθcos(θ−ϕ)​.​
(21)
This assumes 0≤ϕ<π/2, sinθ>0, and a downhill landing with cos(θ−ϕ)>0, as well as the neglected air resistance. It reduces to the horizontal projectile range v02​sin(2θ)/g at ϕ=0. The angle relative to the horizontal is θ−ϕ, which explains the shifted cosine. The original diagram uses stipulated illustrative angles; no examination figure is being reconstructed.

Take θ from the downward vertical. Under the conventional light-rod idealization, only the endpoint point mass contributes appreciably. Its speed is lθ˙, and its gravitational potential energy is −mglcosθ. Therefore
L=21​ml2θ˙2+mglcosθ.​
(22)
An additive constant in the potential energy has no effect on the Euler-Lagrange equation.
Rod-mass qualification: “thin” and “uniform” do not imply negligible mass. If the uniform rod has a finite unspecified mass Mr​, its pivot moment of inertia is Mr​l2/3 and its centre of mass lies l/2 from the pivot. For a pendulum with a uniform rod and an endpoint mass, the general answer is
L=21​Iθ˙2+Acosθ,I=l2(m+3Mr​​),A=gl(m+2Mr​​).
(23)
This formula covers either convention without inventing a rod mass. A result involving only the specified m,l,g uses Mr​=0 unless an additional relation for Mr​ is stated.

For the general Lagrangian just obtained, the Euler-Lagrange equation gives
Iθ¨+Asinθ=0.

Using the small-angle approximation sinθ≃θ, this is simple harmonic motion with ω02​=A/I. Thus
T=2πg(m+Mr​/2)l(m+Mr​/3)​​⟶T=2πgl​​ for a negligible-mass rod.​

Allowing azimuthal motion makes the endpoint speed satisfy v2=l2(θ˙2+sin2θφ˙​2). The light-rod spherical pendulum therefore has
L=21​ml2(θ˙2+sin2θφ˙​2)+mglcosθ.​
(26)
The azimuth φ is a cyclic coordinate, since the Lagrangian is independent of it. Its conjugate canonical momentum is the conserved vertical angular momentum:
pφ​=ml2sin2θφ˙​=Jz​=constant.​
(27)
For a finite uniform rod, replace ml2 by I and mgl by A from i. The same azimuthal symmetry then gives pφ​=Isin2θφ˙​.
The Hamiltonian is the Legendre transform of this Lagrangian. With pθ​=Iθ˙,
H=2Ipθ2​​+2Isin2θpφ2​​−Acosθ=2I​(θ˙2+sin2θφ˙​2)−Acosθ.
(28)
There is no explicit time dependence, so dH/dt=∂H/∂t=0. Here H also equals the total mechanical energy: the constraint is stationary, the kinetic energy is quadratic in generalized velocities, and gravity is conservative. The apparent singularity of the momentum expression at sinθ=0 is a spherical-coordinate singularity; trajectories reaching a pole must be described consistently, and nonzero Jz​ prevents reaching it at finite energy.

Let P=mp​c2 denote the proton rest energy and W=220GeV the desired particle's rest energy. For an antiproton of total energy Epˉ​​ striking a stationary proton, the incoming squared centre-of-mass energy is
s=(Epˉ​​+P)2−c2ppˉ​2​=2P2+2PEpˉ​​.
(29)
For production of the stated single particle, four-momentum conservation requires s=W2. Thus
Epˉ​fixed​=2PW2−2P2​≃25.7912TeV.​

The numerical value uses P=0.938272GeV. It is a total incident energy; the corresponding kinetic energy is Epˉ​fixed​−P≃25.7902TeV. Much of the laboratory energy supplies motion of the outgoing system rather than its invariant mass.

In the parent's rest frame, the identical daughters have equal and opposite momenta, and each has total energy 110GeV. With daughter rest energy Q=90GeV, each speed in this frame is
β∗​c=c1−1102Q2​​,β∗​=0.5749596.

(33)

Conservation of angular momentum means that the total angular momentum of a system about a chosen fixed origin in an inertial frame stays constant. Its dynamical condition is
dtdJ​=τext​=0.​
(34)
Internal torques cancel for the complete isolated mechanical system. If only the component of external torque about an axis vanishes, only that angular momentum component is necessarily conserved. The skater's upright spin uses this axial statement: vertical weight and support provide negligible vertical-axis torque, and frictional torque is neglected. The skater need not conserve rotational kinetic energy while changing the internal configuration.

Write the lost mass fraction as f, with f=0.02, and let μ=GM. Initially the circular orbit has v02​=μ/R, zero radial velocity, and specific angular momentum h2=R2v02​=μR. The stipulated rapid symmetric loss leaves the satellite's instantaneous position and velocity unchanged, but reduces the gravitational parameter to μ′=(1−f)μ. There is no impulsive kick from contact with ejecta in this model.
The new specific orbital energy is
ε′=2v02​​−Rμ′​=Rμ​(f−21​),
(39)
and h remains unchanged because the post-loss force is still central. At a radial turning point,
ε′=2r2h2​−rμ′​.
(40)
Inserting the known constants and factoring yields
(1−2f)r2−2(1−f)Rr+R2=(r−R)[(1−2f)r−R]=0.
(41)
For 0<f<1/2 the two radii are R and R/(1−2f). The initial radius is the pericentre: immediately after the loss, r¨=h2/R3−μ′/R2=fμ/R2>0, so the satellite begins moving outward. Therefore
rmin​=R,rmax​=0.96R​=2425​R.​
(42)
Equivalently, the new semi-major axis is 49R/48 and the orbital eccentricity is 1/49. These values also satisfy rmin​=a(1−e) and rmax​=a(1+e). For loss of half the original mass the ideal trajectory becomes parabolic, and larger loss makes it unbound. Slow adiabatic loss would give a different, nearly circular expansion; it is not the instantaneous limit stipulated here.
The figure retains the same angular momentum before and after the change. Once the expelled material is far away, a spherically symmetric outer shell exerts no interior gravitational force by the shell theorem; direct ejecta interaction and the shell's finite transit time are neglected as in the source idealization.
