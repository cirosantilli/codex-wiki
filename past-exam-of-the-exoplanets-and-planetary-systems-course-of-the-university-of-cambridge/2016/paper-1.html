Adopt circular orbits, negligible planetary masses, spherical bodies, uniform stellar brightness and no transit dilution. Interpret the measured duration as the total duration of a circular planetary transit, from first to fourth contact, T14​. These conventions matter especially for the off-centre crossing: finite planet size cannot automatically be ignored there. Unknown orbital eccentricity or limb darkening would require further information to infer unique stellar radii.
First convert the apparent magnitude difference into a fractional exoplanet transit depth, rather than treating a milli-magnitude as a part in a thousand:
δ=1−10−0.005/2.5=0.00459458,k≡R⋆​Rp​​=δ​=0.0677834.
(1)
Both crossings are non-grazing because their transit impact parameters satisfy b<1−k, so the full projected planetary disc can block the star. Equal depths determine equal radius ratios, not equal absolute planet radii.
For orbital inclination i measured from the sky plane, b=(a/R⋆​)cosi. Choosing mid-transit as orbital phase zero, the projected separation obeys
R⋆2​z2​=b2+[(R⋆​a​)2−b2]sin2(P2πt​).
(2)
At first and fourth contact, z/R⋆​=1+k. The full crossing duration therefore is
T14​=πP​arcsin[(a/R⋆​)2−b2​(1+k)2−b2​​].​
(3)
For the numerical scale use solar radius R⊙​=6.957×108m and nominal solar mass parameter (GM)⊙​=1.3271244×1020m3s−2, with one day equal to 86400s. These are stated conversion choices, not additional measurements of the two systems.[ref]
Kepler's third law gives a=[GM⋆​P2/(4π2)]1/3. For a solar-mass, solar-radius host, this gives a/R⊙​=12.3051 at five days and 6.68023 at two days. Substitution in the contact formula yields
T14,A,⊙​=3.32h,T14,B,⊙​=1.63h.​
(4)
The transit duration of a small planet instead takes both k→0 and R⋆​/a≪1, giving T≃PR⋆​1−b2​/(πa). It predicts 3.10h and 1.37h, respectively. The difference is appreciable for B because the fractional change in its crossing chord is larger. Durations defined by mid-ingress to mid-egress or by second to third contact should not be silently equated with T14​.
The original diagram uses a straight projected track to display the contact geometry; the duration formula above retains orbital curvature.
To infer the actual stellar radii, invert the contact formula before applying the supplied mass-radius scaling. Let A=a/R⋆​ and s=sin(πT14​/P); then
A=[b2+s2(1+k)2−b2​]1/2.
(5)
The measured durations imply AA​=8.34661 and AB​=7.55743. Normalize the stipulated constant mass-radius ratio to the Sun, so M⋆​/M⊙​=R⋆​/R⊙​≡r⋆​. Kepler's third law then gives
R⋆​a​=A⊙​r⋆−2/3​,r⋆​=(AA⊙​​)3/2.
(6)
Thus the stellar radius from transit duration and a linear mass-radius relation is
RA​=1.79R⊙​,RB​=0.831R⊙​.​
(7)
The same assumed scaling gives MA​=1.79M⊙​ and MB​=0.831M⊙​. A's longer crossing requires a lower mean stellar mass density, while B's shorter full-contact crossing requires a higher one. They are not both solar replicas under this model. Their planets have Rp,A​=0.1213R⊙​ and Rp,B​=0.05633R⊙​.
For a consistently applied point-planet approximation, the duration scales as T∝P1/3r⋆2/3​1−b2​ and the inferred radii would instead be RA​≃1.98R⊙​, RB​≃1.07R⊙​. The latter is close to solar. Consequently the assertion of a substantial non-solar radius for B depends on which duration convention and finite-size approximation are intended. The contact-based values above use the measured depth rather than discarding it. If eccentric orbits are allowed, the transit speed introduces an additional factor depending on eccentricity and orientation, so the two durations alone cannot establish those radii uniquely.
For an outer coplanar planet around B, the common orbital inclination gives b2​/bB​=a2​/aB​=(P2​/PB​)2/3. A point-planet centre must cross the stellar disc, requiring b2​<1. The usual coplanar transit limit is therefore
P2​<PB​bB−3/2​,P2,lim​=2.80days(point-planet geometry).​
(8)
This is a limiting value approached from below; a tangent path has no duration. For an outer planet of finite, unspecified radius ratio k2​, any overlap instead requires b2​<1+k2​, giving P2​<PB​[(1+k2​)/bB​]3/2. If it had the same ratio k2​=k, that geometric limit would be 3.08 days; requiring full overlap would give 2.52 days. Because neither its radius nor a photometric detection threshold is supplied, no unique finite-size practical maximum period can be inferred beyond this conditional expression. The stellar radius cancels from the point-planet period-ratio bound.
For B's inner planet, a reflected-light planetary phase curve has
F⋆​Fp​​=Ag​(aRp​​)2Φ(α),Φ(0)=1,
(9)
where Ag​ is geometric albedo and α is phase angle. Near exoplanet secondary eclipse the visible hemisphere is almost fully illuminated. Using the standard full-phase approximation and the contact-based AB​,
F⋆​Fp​​≃0.5(AB​k​)2=4.0222×10−5,δocc​≃40.2ppm.​
(10)
The exact lost fraction of combined stellar and planetary light is Fp​/(F⋆​+Fp​), which changes the result negligibly at this contrast. The corresponding magnitude depth is about 0.0437 milli-magnitudes. The circular orbit has the same impact distance at eclipse as at transit, and bB​<1−k, so the star can hide the entire planetary disc.
The inclination inferred for B is 83.92∘, putting closest approach to full phase at α≃6.08∘. A fully precise prediction would therefore also require the reflected-light phase function, which the question does not specify; the boxed value uses Φ≃1. The consistent point-planet duration approximation yields about 56.7ppm instead, through its different inferred a/RB​. It is important to keep the geometry used for the radius inference and the reflected-light calculation consistent.
Finally, the stellar radial-velocity semi-amplitude for a low-mass companion on a circular orbit is
K≃(P2πG​)1/3M⋆2/3​Mp​sini​.
(11)
The two stars' equal apparent magnitudes make a comparison at similar photon statistics plausible, though spectral-line widths, stellar variability and observing cadence can still differ. With the contact-based stellar estimates,
KB​KA​​=Mp,B​Mp,A​​(PA​PB​​)1/3(MA​MB​​)2/3siniB​siniA​​≃0.444Mp,B​Mp,A​​.
(12)
If the planets have comparable mean densities, A is easier: their equal depth then gives Mp,A​/Mp,B​≃(RA​/RB​)3≃9.99, so KA​/KB​≃4.44. The larger planetary mass outweighs A's longer orbital period and larger host mass. For equal planetary masses, B is easier by a factor 2.25 in amplitude. The supplied transit data give planet radii, not masses, so the ranking is conditional on a planetary mass-radius assumption; a unique unconditional ranking would invent information.

The principal feature is the abundance of planets with radii of roughly one to a few Earth radii compared with large gas giants within the stated short-period sample. The smallest few plotted bins are of broadly similar height, followed by a substantial decline at larger sizes. Thus the figure supports a high occurrence of small planets relative to giants, rather than an indefinitely steep rise toward arbitrarily small sizes. The horizontal scale is logarithmic: one should compare counts in the displayed bins with their widths in lnRp​, not mistake a bin count for a density per unit linear radius.
The vertical axis is an average number of planets per target star. This exoplanet occurrence rate need not equal the fraction of stars hosting a planet, because one star can host several. The restriction to periods below 85 days also matters: the figure is not a census of distant gas giants or of every planet in a system. Neither the detailed reduction procedure nor the raw detection counts are specified in the caption, so they cannot be reconstructed uniquely from the histogram.
Selection effects actually make the small-planet population harder to see. A full, undiluted exoplanet transit depth is approximately (Rp​/R⋆​)2. For independent photometric errors of fractional size σf​ per sample, an idealized detection signal-to-noise ratio is
SNR≃σf​(Rp​/R⋆​)2​Nin​​,
(13)
where Nin​ counts in-transit samples. At fixed star and orbital period, halving planet radius reduces this amplitude by four. Fainter hosts, correlated stellar noise, gaps and transit vetting reduce transit survey completeness further. Geometric transit probability also falls with orbital distance, ptr​≃R⋆​/a∝P−2/3 for fixed stellar mass. For a fixed observing baseline and cadence, the small-planet circular duration law gives Nin​∝P−2/3 and approximately SNR∝Rp2​P−1/3.
Recovering an intrinsic exoplanet occurrence rate therefore requires correcting for geometric alignments, incomplete recovery and false positives. An illustrative estimator for a radius-period bin is
fB​≃N⋆​1​∑j∈B​ptr,j​Cj​wj​​,
(14)
where Cj​ is the recovery probability over the relevant target sample and wj​ accounts for candidate reliability. Real analyses must average these probabilities over heterogeneous targets and measurement uncertainty; the formula indicates the required weights, not a reconstruction of the unpublished inputs to this figure. A primary Kepler space telescope analysis explicitly models both false positives and incomplete detection to infer occurrence.[ref]
Thus the apparent preference for small planets cannot be explained by their being photometrically easier to detect. Small transits are harder. A plateau in occurrence below about twice Earth's size was independently reported using synthetic-transit injection and recovery, though that study used a different 5–50-day selection and is not identified here as the source of the exam's plot.[ref]
The natural physical framework is core accretion in a protoplanetary disk. Most initial disk mass is gas, principally hydrogen and helium; a much smaller fraction is solid material. Protoplanetary dust coagulation first assembles aggregates through low-speed sticking. Settling and differential drag help concentrate solids, but bouncing, the fragmentation barrier to dust growth and radial drift of solids in a gas disk prevent a simple assumption that every collision grows a body smoothly to planetary size. Concentration in dust traps or through streaming instability, followed by gravitational collapse of a dense solid population, provides routes to planetesimals. Subsequent collisions must dissipate sufficient relative energy to bind material rather than disrupt it.
Once sufficiently large planetesimals exist, gravitational focusing enhances their accretion cross-sections:
σcoll​≃πR2(1+vrel2​vesc2​​),vesc2​≃R2GM​.
(15)
When background random velocities remain small, larger bodies grow disproportionately fast in runaway planetesimal growth. As they become massive enough to stir the surrounding population, oligarchic growth develops: several planetary embryos occupy neighboring feeding zones, while much of the remaining solid material resides in smaller planetesimals. Heating the planetesimal random motions weakens gravitational focusing, and local depletion drives the embryos toward an isolation mass. Collisions among embryos can continue assembly after gas dispersal.
This solid-building pathway naturally produces many modest cores. Producing a large gas giant requires an additional, time-sensitive step: a sufficiently massive core must acquire and cool a gaseous envelope while disk gas remains available. Gas captured by a low-mass core releases gravitational potential energy; the envelope's ability to radiate that energy governs its contraction and further accretion. A characteristic cooling estimate is
tcool​∼Lcool​∣Etot​∣​,
(16)
where Etot​ includes the envelope’s gravitational and internal energies. This states an energy budget rather than a calibrated accretion timescale. Increasing core mass generally facilitates faster gas growth in the appropriate envelope regime. The critical core mass for gas accretion depends on opacity, solid accretion heating and disk conditions; no one exact threshold follows from the histogram. If envelope cooling becomes rapid enough and gas supply persists, runaway gas accretion can produce a giant. If core growth or cooling is too slow, protoplanetary disk dispersal removes the reservoir first, leaving a rocky core or a smaller planet with a limited envelope.
The snow line changes the supply of condensed solids: where water ice can survive, additional solid material can help cores grow. Its location depends on the evolving disk temperature and pressure, so there is no universal orbital radius at which giants must form. Short-period planets need not have assembled entirely at their present locations. Planetary migration can carry cores and giants inward; scattering and collisions can further reshape their orbits. Pebble accretion is another way to supply solids rapidly to embryos, but its efficiency depends on particle coupling and the available pebble flow. These processes alter the distribution without requiring every core to become a giant.
Radius also is not a linear proxy for mass. A relatively small hydrogen-helium envelope can increase a planet's radius substantially, so some planets with a few Earth radii need not be purely rocky. Contraction as planets cool and irradiation-driven atmospheric escape can subsequently reduce their radii. Hence the final histogram combines solid growth, gas capture, migration and later thermal evolution. In particular, the smallest bins need not all share one composition or one formation location.
Direct fragmentation through gravitational instability of an astrophysical disk is an alternative route to massive gaseous objects. It requires a sufficiently self-gravitating disk, described locally by Toomre's stability criterion, together with sufficiently rapid cooling for bound fragments to survive. It does not naturally explain the dominant population of small close-in cores by itself, although migration or further evolution can change the locations of fragments. The observed abundance of small planets is therefore consistent with solid cores commonly forming while only a minority acquire a massive gas envelope before disk dispersal. It does not uniquely prove a particular prescription for each uncertain growth stage.
The physical explanation is widespread core formation combined with restricted access to runaway gas accretion. The strongest observational conclusion concerns small versus giant planets in the stated period range, after accounting for selection; the figure alone does not determine a universal core threshold, a unique mass distribution, or the population beyond the period cut.

A protoplanetary disk is a flattened, rotating reservoir of gas and solids around a young star. Its radial gravity is usually dominated by the host, making its rotation close to Keplerian rotation, ΩK​=(GM⋆​/r3)1/2. Gas pressure provides vertical support and a small radial correction to the orbital speed; it does not make the disk a nonrotating spherical atmosphere.
At height ∣z∣≪r, vertical hydrostatic equilibrium in the stellar gravitational field gives
∂z∂Pg​​=−ρg​ΩK2​z.
(17)
For a locally vertically isothermal ideal gas, Pg​=ρg​cs2​ with cs2​=kB​T/(μmH​), where μ is mean molecular weight. Integration gives
ρg​(r,z)=2π​HΣg​(r)​exp(−2H2z2​),H=ΩK​cs​​.​
(18)
Here Σg​ is the vertically integrated gas surface density of a disk, and H is the isothermal disk scale height. The disk aspect ratio H/r=cs​/vK​ is small in a geometrically thin disk. For T∝r−q it scales as r(1−q)/2, so q<1 produces disk flaring. This illustrative local vertical model does not determine a unique radial surface-density law or temperature normalization from the information given.
The gas is predominantly molecular hydrogen and helium. A much smaller component consists of heavier elements, partitioned between vapor, solid refractory grains and condensed volatile ices. A gas-to-solid mass ratio of order 100 is a common initial fiducial model, not a universal measurement applying at every disk radius or evolutionary stage. Solids can grow and settle while gas accretes or disperses, changing the local ratio substantially.[ref]
Thermal structure organizes composition. In the hottest inner region, grains can sublimate and much of their material remains in the gas. Refractory silicate minerals and metallic grains survive at lower temperatures; farther out, water and then other sufficiently volatile species can condense when their local condensation conditions are met. The water snow line can raise the solid inventory available to planet formation. Its location depends on stellar irradiation, accretion heating, opacity and gas pressure, and evolves with time; it is not fixed merely by naming a star or adopting a solar-system distance.
Stellar radiation heats the exposed surface of a passive disk. Beneath it, shielding can leave a cooler, molecule-rich interior with a higher mass density. In the inner actively accreting disk, dissipation can instead make the optically thick midplane the hottest region. These are distinct heating regimes; a vertically isothermal profile is an analytic approximation rather than a claim that every real layer has the same temperature. Dust opacity controls how radiation is absorbed and re-emitted. Infrared and millimetre emission probe solid material, while spectral lines probe gas; neither observable alone supplies a model-free inventory of the whole disk.
Dust settling in an astrophysical disk makes larger, less tightly coupled particles more concentrated toward the midplane, while turbulence and mixing oppose that settling. For an aerodynamic stopping time ts​ short compared with ΩK−1​, terminal vertical balance gives vz​≃−ΩK2​ts​z. Small grains remain better coupled to the gas. Radial drift also changes the distribution of solids and their access to snow lines and embryos.
The disk evolves through accretion and angular momentum transport, potentially involving turbulent stresses, magnetorotational instability or magnetically driven winds where conditions permit. Protoplanetary disk dispersal eventually removes gas through accretion and mass loss, including photoevaporation. In standard evolutionary pictures this occurs on a scale of a few million years, with substantial system-to-system variation. That finite reservoir lifetime is central to whether an embryo acquires a massive gaseous envelope.[ref]
The essential structure is a nearly Keplerian gas disk with vertical pressure support, radial and vertical thermal gradients, and an evolving solid population concentrated by settling, growth and drift. Its composition is set jointly by the original elemental inventory, local condensation conditions and transport.

Sub-Keplerian rotation means that the gas rotates more slowly than a pressure-free particle on a circular orbit at the same cylindrical radius. Let vK​=(GM⋆​/r)1/2. Neglecting radial acceleration, radial force balance in the gas is
rvgϕ2​​=r2GM⋆​​+ρg​1​drdPg​​.
(19)
An outwardly decreasing pressure, dPg​/dr<0, supplies part of the inward-gravity balance through an outward pressure force. Less centrifugal support is needed, so vgϕ​<vK​. Using cs2​=Pg​/ρg​, define the pressure-gradient parameter of a protoplanetary disk by
η≡−2ρg​vK2​r​drdPg​​=−21​(vK​cs​​)2dlnrdlnPg​​.
(20)
Then
vgϕ​=vK​1−2η​≃vK​(1−η).​
(21)
For a thin disk and a smooth radial pressure profile, η is small, of order (H/r)2. The factor of two is part of this explicit convention; changing the convention for η changes the drift formula correspondingly.
A solid particle feels essentially no continuum gas-pressure support. Left alone, it would orbit at vK​, so in the gas it feels a headwind. Drag removes angular momentum, causing radial drift of solids in a gas disk. Define Stokes number St=ΩK​ts​ using the aerodynamic stopping time ts​. In a dilute test-particle limit with linear drag, negligible radial gas flow and small departures from a circular Keplerian orbit, let uϕ​=vpϕ​−vK​. The steady local radial and azimuthal balances are
0=2ΩK​uϕ​−ts​vpr​​,0=−2ΩK​vpr​​−ts​uϕ​+ηvK​​.
(22)
Solving gives
vpr​=−1+St22ηvK​St​,vpϕ​−vK​=−1+St2ηvK​​.​
(23)
The sign is inward for the usual negative pressure gradient. Very small particles, St≪1, closely follow the gas and have a small relative drift vpr​≃−2ηvK​St. Very weakly coupled large bodies, St≫1, also drift slowly, with vpr​≃−2ηvK​/St. The fastest pressure-driven inward drift occurs at St=1, where ∣vpr​∣=ηvK​. The corresponding physical grain or aggregate size depends on gas density, orbital radius and the drag regime; “metre-size barrier” is a conventional description of some disk models, not a universal size.
The drift timescale in this model is
tdrift​=∣vpr​∣r​=2ηΩK​St1+St2​.
(24)
It can be much shorter than the disk gas lifetime, threatening loss of the material needed to make planetesimals. Growth through the vulnerable coupling range must compete with this loss, while differential drift generates collisions that may stick, bounce or fragment. Radial redistribution also changes solid-to-gas ratios and moves material across condensation fronts. These are consequences for planet formation, rather than merely a small correction to an orbital frequency.
The same mechanism can help concentrate solids. At a local radial pressure maximum, dPg​/dr=0, so this component of drift vanishes. On the inside, a positive pressure gradient gives outward drift; on the outside, a negative gradient gives inward drift. Particles therefore converge toward a dust trap. Turbulent diffusion and inward gas advection can limit retention. With a nonzero radial gas speed vgr​, the test-particle result generalizes to vpr​=[vgr​−2ηvK​St]/(1+St2), so gas advection need not vanish even at a pressure maximum.
Finally, if local solid loading becomes large, backreaction on the gas can no longer be neglected. Coupled gas-particle drift can support streaming instability and strong solid concentrations, potentially enabling gravitational collapse into planetesimals. The simple test-particle drift formula is a useful starting point, but not a description of that nonlinear high-loading regime. Pressure support creates both a drift obstacle to growth and opportunities for concentration where the pressure structure changes.

Oligarchic growth is the stage in which a relatively small number of planetary embryos dominate the dynamics of many smaller planetesimals. It follows runaway planetesimal growth. The embryos remain separated, grow to broadly comparable masses within similar local disk conditions, and regulate their accretion by stirring the population that supplies their material. The primary numerical study describes this embryo–planetesimal structure and typical separations of about ten individual Hill radii.[ref]
The key feedback is gravitational focusing. In a dispersion-dominated encounter with approach speed v and a small impactor, conservation of energy and conservation of angular momentum give the collision cross-section
σcoll​≃πR2(1+v2vesc2​​),vesc2​≃R2GM​.
(25)
At fixed internal mass density, R∝M1/3 and vesc2​∝M2/3. If the background random speed initially is controlled by small planetesimals and varies little with embryo mass, strong focusing gives σcoll​∝M4/3. For a disk with planetesimal thickness of order vz​/ΩK​ and a fixed ratio v/vz​, the associated estimate is
M˙∼Σs​ΩK​R2(1+v2vesc2​​),
(26)
up to geometric factors. With nearly fixed v, this gives M˙∝M4/3 and M˙/M∝M1/3: the largest bodies have the shortest fractional growth times, the essence of runaway growth.
As embryos grow, their gravitational encounters increasingly excite the random eccentricities and inclinations of nearby planetesimals. This is viscous stirring of planetesimals, not molecular fluid viscosity. Higher encounter speeds lower the focusing factor. The largest local embryo heats its feeding population most strongly and thereby suppresses its own relative growth advantage. Gas drag and dissipative collisions oppose stirring; dynamical friction tends to keep the more massive embryos' random motions relatively small while transferring random energy to lighter bodies. The balance between these processes controls the accretion rate.
For an illustrative self-regulated scaling v∝M1/3, the ratio vesc​/v is approximately fixed. The previous growth estimate becomes
M˙∝M2/3,MM˙​∝M−1/3,tgrow​=M˙M​∝M1/3.​
(27)
Larger embryos now grow more slowly in fractional terms, allowing neighboring embryos in comparable environments to approach similar masses. This scaling illustrates the feedback; it is not a universal exact exponent for every planetesimal size distribution, damping law or disk surface-density profile. In particular, a two-body focusing cross-section must be modified in the shear-dominated regime, where encounters are governed by the stellar tidal field.
The relevant gravitational interaction scale is the Hill radius,
RH​=a(3M⋆​M​)1/3,vH​=ΩK​RH​.
(28)
Close embryo encounters and orbital repulsion keep adjacent embryos separated by several Hill-scale lengths. The mutual Hill radius uses their combined mass; for equal neighboring masses it is 21/3RH​. Stating which definition is used matters when quoting a spacing coefficient. The spacing is a dynamical scale, not an exact universal integer.
Each feeding zone of a planetary embryo contains a finite solid reservoir. To estimate its isolation mass, let the full annular width be Δa=BRH​, where the dimensionless coefficient B includes the stated choice of width and Hill-radius convention. For local solid surface density Σs​,
Miso​≃2πaΔaΣs​=2πBa2Σs​(3M⋆​Miso​​)1/3.
(29)
Solving gives
Miso​=(3M⋆​)1/2(2πBa2Σs​)3/2​.​
(30)
Thus denser solid disks and wider feeding annuli allow larger embryos. If Σs​∝a−p, the estimate scales as Miso​∝a3−3p/2 at fixed host mass and B. This is a local exhaustion estimate, not an absolute maximum planet mass: radial delivery of solids, pebble accretion, migration and embryo mergers can replenish or enlarge a feeding zone. No solid surface-density normalization is given, so a numerical isolation mass would require an invented disk model.
As the local supply is depleted, growth slows further. After gas damping weakens, embryo orbits may cross and giant collisions can assemble larger terrestrial planets. Farther out, sufficiently massive cores may instead acquire gas before protoplanetary disk dispersal. Oligarchic growth explains why a few comparable embryos share a disk while many smaller planetesimals remain: gravitational stirring weakens focusing and makes accretion self-regulating.
