Oligarchic growth is the stage in which a relatively small number of planetary embryos dominate the dynamics of many smaller planetesimals. It follows runaway planetesimal growth. The embryos remain separated, grow to broadly comparable masses within similar local disk conditions, and regulate their accretion by stirring the population that supplies their material. The primary numerical study describes this embryo–planetesimal structure and typical separations of about ten individual Hill radii.[ref]
The key feedback is gravitational focusing. In a dispersion-dominated encounter with approach speed v and a small impactor, conservation of energy and conservation of angular momentum give the collision cross-section
σcoll​≃πR2(1+v2vesc2​​),vesc2​≃R2GM​.
(25)
At fixed internal mass density, R∝M1/3 and vesc2​∝M2/3. If the background random speed initially is controlled by small planetesimals and varies little with embryo mass, strong focusing gives σcoll​∝M4/3. For a disk with planetesimal thickness of order vz​/ΩK​ and a fixed ratio v/vz​, the associated estimate is
M˙∼Σs​ΩK​R2(1+v2vesc2​​),
(26)
up to geometric factors. With nearly fixed v, this gives M˙∝M4/3 and M˙/M∝M1/3: the largest bodies have the shortest fractional growth times, the essence of runaway growth.
As embryos grow, their gravitational encounters increasingly excite the random eccentricities and inclinations of nearby planetesimals. This is viscous stirring of planetesimals, not molecular fluid viscosity. Higher encounter speeds lower the focusing factor. The largest local embryo heats its feeding population most strongly and thereby suppresses its own relative growth advantage. Gas drag and dissipative collisions oppose stirring; dynamical friction tends to keep the more massive embryos' random motions relatively small while transferring random energy to lighter bodies. The balance between these processes controls the accretion rate.
For an illustrative self-regulated scaling v∝M1/3, the ratio vesc​/v is approximately fixed. The previous growth estimate becomes
M˙∝M2/3,MM˙​∝M−1/3,tgrow​=M˙M​∝M1/3.​
(27)
Larger embryos now grow more slowly in fractional terms, allowing neighboring embryos in comparable environments to approach similar masses. This scaling illustrates the feedback; it is not a universal exact exponent for every planetesimal size distribution, damping law or disk surface-density profile. In particular, a two-body focusing cross-section must be modified in the shear-dominated regime, where encounters are governed by the stellar tidal field.
The relevant gravitational interaction scale is the Hill radius,
RH​=a(3M⋆​M​)1/3,vH​=ΩK​RH​.
(28)
Close embryo encounters and orbital repulsion keep adjacent embryos separated by several Hill-scale lengths. The mutual Hill radius uses their combined mass; for equal neighboring masses it is 21/3RH​. Stating which definition is used matters when quoting a spacing coefficient. The spacing is a dynamical scale, not an exact universal integer.
Each feeding zone of a planetary embryo contains a finite solid reservoir. To estimate its isolation mass, let the full annular width be Δa=BRH​, where the dimensionless coefficient B includes the stated choice of width and Hill-radius convention. For local solid surface density Σs​,
Miso​≃2πaΔaΣs​=2πBa2Σs​(3M⋆​Miso​​)1/3.
(29)
Solving gives
Miso​=(3M⋆​)1/2(2πBa2Σs​)3/2​.​
(30)
Thus denser solid disks and wider feeding annuli allow larger embryos. If Σs​∝a−p, the estimate scales as Miso​∝a3−3p/2 at fixed host mass and B. This is a local exhaustion estimate, not an absolute maximum planet mass: radial delivery of solids, pebble accretion, migration and embryo mergers can replenish or enlarge a feeding zone. No solid surface-density normalization is given, so a numerical isolation mass would require an invented disk model.
As the local supply is depleted, growth slows further. After gas damping weakens, embryo orbits may cross and giant collisions can assemble larger terrestrial planets. Farther out, sufficiently massive cores may instead acquire gas before protoplanetary disk dispersal. Oligarchic growth explains why a few comparable embryos share a disk while many smaller planetesimals remain: gravitational stirring weakens focusing and makes accretion self-regulating.
