Lunar phase

 ... Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Planetary science Natural satellite Moon
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 6
A lunar phase is the appearance of the sunlit portion of the Moon as seen from Earth. Outside a lunar eclipse, the distant Sun illuminates approximately one hemisphere of the Moon, but an observer sees a changing overlap of that hemisphere with the hemisphere facing Earth. The lunar phase therefore changes even though synchronous rotation keeps approximately the same near side of the Moon facing Earth. [ref].
The cycle runs through new Moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full Moon, waning gibbous, last quarter and waning crescent. “Waxing” means that the lunar illuminated fraction is increasing; “waning” means it is decreasing. A crescent has less than half its apparent disk sunlit, and a gibbous lunar phase has more than half. First and last quarter each show half a disk: “quarter” refers to progress around the cycle, rather than a quarter of the disk being lit. [ref].
An instructive circular orbit model puts Earth at the origin, the Sun far away along +x, and the Moon at R(cosθ,sinθ). The sunward position x>0, θ=0, gives new Moon; the opposite position x<0, θ=π, gives full Moon. Counterclockwise motion gives first quarter at θ=π/2 and last quarter at θ=3π/2. The Sun's direction is fixed during this snapshot of the cycle. Every small globe in the orbital view is illuminated on its +x half; the adjacent apparent disks are separate views from Earth, rather than globes viewed from above.
The disk views choose north up, with the upward direction perpendicular to the orbital plane. In this convention waxing lunar phases are bright on the right and waning lunar phases on the left. Local horizon orientation and viewing from different locations on Earth can rotate the apparent disk; its lit edge always points toward the projected direction of the Sun. The lunar illuminated fraction is unaffected by this rotation.
The useful angle is the phase angle α=∠SME, with S the Sun, M the Moon and E the observer at Earth's center: its vertex is at the Moon. Full Moon has α=0, quarter has α=π/2, and new Moon has α=π in the ideal model. By contrast, the elongation ϵ=∠SEM has its vertex at Earth. For a distant Sun, its rays are parallel, so
α=π−ϵ,k=21+cosα​=21−cosϵ​.
(4)
Here k is the lunar illuminated fraction: a fraction of projected disk area, not a fraction of the Moon's total surface area or its received radiant flux relative to full Moon. The phase angle and unsigned elongation each lie between 0 and π, so either alone gives the illuminated amount without distinguishing waxing from waning. The orbital direction resolves that ambiguity.
The lunar illuminated fraction has an elegant geometric derivation. Model the Moon as a sphere of unit radius and view it along the z direction. Take the Sun's unit direction to be (sinα,0,cosα). A visible surface point above apparent disk coordinates (x,y) has z=1−x2−y2​. Direct illumination requires the outward normal's dot product with the sunward direction to be positive. The day-night terminator therefore obeys xsinα+zcosα=0. At a fixed y, set q=1−y2​; the visible day-night terminator is xt​=−qcosα, and the illuminated strip extends from xt​ to the right edge q. Integrating its width gives
Alit​=∫−11​(1+cosα)1−y2​dy=2π​(1+cosα),k=πAlit​​.
(5)
The integral is the area of half a unit circle. Thus a three-dimensional visibility problem reduces to a circle's area and a cosine. The derivation assumes parallel sunlight, a distant observer and a sphere, and ignores a lunar eclipse. Real observed brightness additionally depends on surface scattering of light and observing geometry, so k=1/2 does not promise half the full-Moon radiant flux.
For a worked example, elongation ϵ=60∘ gives phase angle α=120∘ and k=(1+cos120∘)/2=1/4: a crescent with 25% of its apparent disk sunlit. At elongation 120∘, the phase angle is 60∘ and k=3/4: a gibbous lunar phase. At 90∘, k=1/2. In each case the same k occurs once while waxing and once while waning in the ideal cycle.
Ordinary lunar phases are not caused by Earth's shadow. A lunar eclipse occurs only when the Moon actually enters that shadow near full Moon. The orbital inclination of the Moon is about 5∘ relative to Earth's orbital plane around the Sun; most full Moons pass above or below the shadow. This is why full Moon does not imply a lunar eclipse every month. [ref].
The phase cycle is a synodic month, about 29.5 days, whereas the Moon's orbital period relative to distant stars is a sidereal month, about 27.3 days. During one sidereal month, Earth advances around the Sun, so the Moon must move farther to repeat the sunward alignment. For coplanar circular orbits traversed in the same direction, subtract their mean motions: nsyn​=nsid​−nyear​, with n=2π/P. Hence
Psyn​1​=Psid​1​−Pyear​1​,Psyn​=Pyear​−Psid​Psid​Pyear​​.
(6)
Using Psid​=27.321661days and Pyear​=365.256days gives Psyn​=29.530591days, consistent with the mean synodic month of 29.53059 days. After one sidereal month, the sunward direction has advanced about 26.93∘; catching up takes about 2.21 more days. These are mean-period estimates: orbital eccentricity and changing geometry make individual synodic months vary. [ref]. [ref].
The lunar phase is also a practical clock for observing. Full Moon is opposite the Sun and rises roughly at sunset; first quarter is about a right angle from the Sun and is convenient in the evening, while last quarter is convenient before dawn. These are rough guides, not exact rise-time predictions for every place and season. [ref]. Historically, repeated lunar phases provided the month in lunar calendars, while half-lit disks gave Aristarchus of Samos a way to infer astronomical distances. [ref].
