Orthogonal polynomial (⟨pn​,pk​⟩w​=hn​δnk​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 26
An orthogonal polynomial family chooses polynomials that do not overlap under a specified inner product. For real polynomials on an interval (a,b), use the weighted inner product
⟨p,q⟩w​=∫ab​p(x)q(x)w(x)dx,∥p∥w2​=⟨p,p⟩w​.
(1)
Assume a<b, the weight w is measurable and positive almost everywhere, and its absolute moments ∫ab​∣x∣kw(x)dx are finite for every integer k≥0. These conditions make the integrals finite and give every nonzero polynomial positive squared norm. A sequence p0​,p1​,… is an orthogonal polynomial family when pn​ has real coefficients and polynomial degree exactly n, and ⟨pn​,pk​⟩w​=0 for n=k.[ref]
Here hn​=∥pn​∥w2​>0 and δnk​ is the Kronecker delta. Orthogonality does not require hn​=1: an orthonormal set is obtained only after replacing pn​ by pn​/hn​​. Multiplying each member by any nonzero real constant preserves orthogonality, but changes its norm and the corresponding expansion coefficient. The monic orthogonal polynomial convention instead fixes each leading coefficient to one.
The first four Legendre polynomials, named for Adrien-Marie Legendre, use w=1 on [−1,1] and the normalization Pn​(1)=1:
P0​(x)=1,P1​(x)=x,P2​(x)=23x2−1​,P3​(x)=25x3−3x​.
(2)
Their orthogonality and squared norms are
∫−11​Pn​(x)Pk​(x)dx=2n+12​δnk​.
(3)
Thus their Gram matrix has a positive diagonal and zero entries elsewhere, although their function graphs cross and overlap on the page. Orthogonality measures an integral of products, rather than separation of function graphs.[ref]
For these four polynomials, a short verification expands each product and uses ∫−11​x2j+1dx=0 and ∫−11​x2jdx=2/(2j+1). For example, ⟨P0​,P2​⟩=(3(2/3)−2)/2=0 and ⟨P1​,P3​⟩=(5(2/5)−3(2/3))/2=0. The same calculation gives the diagonal entries 2,2/3,2/5,2/7. More generally, apply the Gram-Schmidt process to the monomial basis 1,x,x2,…: at step n, subtract projections onto the earlier polynomials. The result retains its leading term, has degree n, and has positive norm, proving existence without a differential equation. Different degrees also give linear independence, so p0​,…,pm​ form a basis of the polynomials of degree at most m.
This basis turns least-squares solutions for functions into independent scalar calculations, as the decoupled coefficients of orthogonal polynomial projections show. In a spectral method, increasing the degree enlarges the approximation space while keeping earlier projection coefficients fixed. Weighted integrals also connect the same orthogonal polynomials to Gaussian quadrature: its nodes are polynomial roots of a member of the family. The projection problem minimizes an integrated squared error; Gaussian quadrature uses the family to choose evaluation points for an integral.
