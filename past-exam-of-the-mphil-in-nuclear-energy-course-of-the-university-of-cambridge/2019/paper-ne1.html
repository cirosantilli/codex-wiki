a OurBigBook.com
Reduce time, increase distance and use radiation-appropriate shielding. At fixed conditions, accumulated absorbed dose is proportional to exposure time. For an approximately isotropic point source, the inverse-square law reduces the uncollided radiation field as distance increases. Confinement of radioactive material prevents contamination; it addresses a different pathway from attenuation of an external field.
For alpha particles, short stopping ranges make light barriers sufficient against external particles, but confinement remains important. For beta particles, a low-atomic number shield such as plastic limits both penetration and secondary bremsstrahlung; high-atomic number material can then attenuate residual photons. For penetrating gamma rays, dense material such as lead, or a thicker bulk shield such as concrete, provides attenuation. One shield material is not equally appropriate for every radiation type.[ref][ref]

The idealized external photon estimate is approximately 0.557 mSvh−1 at one metre. First apply radioactive decay to the certified radioactive activity:
A=2.4×1092−3/5.27=1.61751×109 Bq.
(4)
Treat the source as isotropic and small compared with the observation distance. Neglect photon self-absorption, capsule attenuation and attenuation in air. The metal and capsule stop the primary beta particles, so the direct external contribution is taken to be the two gamma rays; secondary bremsstrahlung is neglected. Their total energy per transformation is
Eγ​=(1.17+1.33) MeV=4.00544×10−13 J.
(5)
The incident photon energy flow per area at distance r is therefore
ΨE​(r)=4πr2AEγ​​.
(6)
Interpret the given 3 m−1 as the linear energy-absorption coefficient μen​, rather than a total photon-removal coefficient. For an absorbing medium of mass density ρ, a thin volume Sdx absorbs energy per time ΨE​Sμen​dx and has mass ρSdx. Its absorbed dose rate is consequently
D˙=ΨE​ρμen​​.
(7)
For the estimate assume a water-like ρ=103 kgm−3 and local deposition of the secondary charged-particle energy. At r=1 m,
D˙=4π(1.61751×109)(4.00544×10−13)​10003​=1.54671×10−7 Gys−1=0.556815 mGyh−1.​
(8)
For gamma rays, the radiation weighting factor is one, giving H˙=0.556815 mSvh−1 under these assumptions.[ref]
The phrase “equivalent absorbed dose” mixes two quantity names: absorbed dose is measured in gray, while equivalent dose is measured in sievert. Their rates are numerically equal here because the weighting factor is one. The estimate is a physical energy-deposition calculation; the assumed mass density is essential, and the stated inverse-length coefficient must not be used as if it already had area-per-mass units.

H˙0​(1+x)e−x<3 μSvh−1,(1+x)e−x<556.8153​=0.00538778.
(9)
The left side decreases for x>0 because its derivative is −xe−x. Solving the equality gives a unique threshold,
x∗​=7.34532.
(10)

tPb​>45.2x∗​​=0.162507 m,tconcrete​>9.42x∗​​=0.779758 m.​

For example, rounding upward to 0.17 m of lead or 0.80 m of concrete satisfies the stipulated inequality. Equality at the threshold is not sufficient for the strictly smaller target.

The idealized outline is continuous coverage around the source with the required material path length, including the closure; a direct unshielded line through a gap would invalidate this attenuation estimate. The calculation assumes the quoted observation distance, the fixed effective photon coefficients and the prescribed radiation buildup factor. It is not based on the more optimistic uncollided exponential attenuation alone.

a OurBigBook.com
One energy group and a nearly isotropic local neutron distribution are the main approximations. The one-group neutron diffusion equation replaces the full energy-dependent transport problem by one scalar neutron flux and averaged coefficients. A single group cannot resolve spectral changes, resonance absorption or differences between rapidly moving and thermal neutrons; its effective parameters depend on the spectrum used for averaging.
The diffusion approximation also replaces angular transport by a current proportional to the negative neutron flux gradient. It works best where repeated scattering makes the angular distribution nearly isotropic over distances comparable to the field's variation length. It is less reliable close to a sharply localized source, a vacuum boundary, a strong absorber or an abrupt material interface, and in weakly scattering regions. Approximate boundary extrapolation does not restore the lost angular information.
Thus an apparently smooth solution of the one-group neutron diffusion equation need not accurately represent a transport field outside the assumptions that produced its coefficients and boundary conditions.

The one-group particle balance with a prescribed external volume source S can be written
v1​∂t∂ϕ​=−∇⋅J−Σa​ϕ+νΣf​ϕ+S,J=−D∇ϕ.
(12)
Here ϕ is scalar neutron flux, D is the neutron diffusion coefficient, and Σa​,Σf​ are macroscopic cross sections. The terms describe accumulation, current divergence, absorption, nuclear fission production and external input.
For a steady state, set ∂t​ϕ=0; source-free means S=0. In a homogeneous medium D is spatially constant, so substitution gives
D∇2ϕ+(νΣf​−Σa​)ϕ=0.
(13)
The supplied notation defines η as the effective production per absorption, η=νΣf​/Σa​. Therefore
∇2ϕ+D(η−1)Σa​​ϕ=0.​
(14)
This identification uses the absorption accounting for the homogeneous model; a fuel-only reproduction factor with other absorbers excluded would not automatically equal this η. The derivation assumes one prescribed energy group, constant effective coefficients and the diffusion approximation; all neutron production included here is represented by the steady nuclear fission term.

Each equation is stored thermal energy changing at the rate “input minus output”. Define the three heat capacities Cj​=Mj​cj​, and introduce the internal heat-transfer rates
QFM​=R1​TF​−TM​​,QMC​=R2​TM​−TC​​.
(25)
In the fuel balance, CF​T˙F​ is accumulation, P is thermal power generation and QFM​ is transfer to the neutron moderator. In the moderator balance, QFM​ enters, QMC​ leaves and CM​T˙M​ is accumulation. In the coolant balance, QMC​ enters, CC​T˙C​ is accumulation and 2WC​cC​(TC​−Ti​) is net energy carried away by flowing coolant.
The factor two follows the model's arithmetic-mean approximation TC​=(Ti​+To​)/2, since the advective energy removal is WC​cC​(To​−Ti​). The parameters R1​ and R2​ are effective thermal resistances, in KW−1, for fuel-to-moderator and moderator-to-coolant transfer. They summarize the relevant geometry and heat-transfer mechanisms; they are not heat capacities.

At a steady state, all three accumulation terms vanish. The fuel balance gives QFM​=P, the moderator balance then gives QMC​=P, and the coolant balance equates that input to its advective output. Therefore
TC​TM​TF​​=Ti​+2WC​cC​P​,=Ti​+P(R2​+2WC​cC​1​),=Ti​+P(R1​+R2​+2WC​cC​1​).​​
(26)
Each successive region adds the temperature drop needed to carry the same thermal power through its thermal resistance. Summing the original balances cancels both internal transfers, providing the independent total energy balance P=WC​cC​(To​−Ti​).

a OurBigBook.com
Xenon poisoning is neutron removal by xenon-135, which reduces reactor reactivity. Its important precursor is iodine-135. At a long-established constant neutron flux, production through precursor decay is balanced by xenon radioactive decay and neutron capture. A steady model must account for that additional absorber in its neutron balance.
After a rapid removal of the imposed neutron flux, capture removal becomes small immediately, while the existing iodine population continues supplying xenon. The xenon population can therefore rise before eventually falling as the precursor reservoir is exhausted. The resulting extra negative reactor reactivity is a delayed effect, rather than an instantaneous disappearance of the absorber when nuclear fission stops.
An intermediate post-shutdown maximum is possible. This explains why the reactivity associated with a previously operating system can differ substantially from its long-time shutdown value. The population model below quantifies that mechanism; a population maximum is not by itself an operating or restart prescription.

Let G=γI​Σf​ϕ be the constant precursor production rate per volume. Here γI​ is the precursor yield per nuclear fission, and I and X are number densities. At a steady state,
I0​=λI​G​,X0​=λX​+ϕσaX​G​.
(36)
For fixed homogeneous coefficients and spatial shape, the reactivity contribution of an added absorption coefficient ΣaX​=σaX​X0​ is −ΣaX​/(νΣf​). Thus the model gives
ΔρX​=−νγI​​λX​+ϕσaX​ϕσaX​​.
(37)
When capture removal dominates xenon radioactive decay, ϕσaX​≫λX​, and hence
ΔρX​⟶−νγI​​.​
(38)
The limit is independent of the xenon neutron absorption cross section: at high neutron flux, nearly every xenon atom supplied by the precursor ultimately consumes a neutron. The assumptions include constant pre-shutdown neutron flux, sufficient time for precursor and xenon equilibrium, the supplied single-precursor model with no direct xenon fission source, and fixed parameters for translating absorption into reactor reactivity.

The general solution contains both decay of the initial xenon and continued feeding from the initial iodine. Use t=0 for removal of the flux, and retain ϕ as the pre-shutdown value in the initial conditions. Neglect post-shutdown nuclear fission and neutron capture. Then
I(t)=I0​e−λI​t,X˙+λX​X=λI​I0​e−λI​t.
(39)
Multiply this first-order linear differential equation by the integrating factor eλX​t and integrate:
X(t)=X0​e−λX​t+λX​−λI​λI​I0​​(e−λI​t−e−λX​t).​
(40)
With the preceding steady initial conditions, λI​I0​=G=γI​Σf​ϕ and X0​=G/(λX​+ϕσaX​), this is
X(t)=G[λX​+ϕσaX​e−λX​t​+λX​−λI​e−λI​t−e−λX​t​].
(41)
If λX​+ϕσaX​≫∣λX​−λI​∣, the coefficient of the first exponential term is small compared with the coefficients of the feeding term, yielding
X(t)≃λX​−λI​γI​Σf​ϕ​(e−λI​t−e−λX​t).​
(42)
This approximation neglects the finite initial xenon population and is consequently not a small relative error at t=0, where its value is zero while the exact X0​ is positive. The exact expression supplies the initial value and satisfies X˙(0)=G−λX​X0​. For equal nuclear decay constants, the continuous limit of the exact solution is X(t)=(X0​+λI​I0​t)e−λI​t.

The approximate population peaks about 11.3 h after flux removal. Differentiate the preceding difference of exponentials. The stationary condition is
−λI​e−λI​t+λX​e−λX​t=0,
(43)
so for distinct nuclear decay constants,
tmax​=λI​−λX​ln(λI​/λX​)​.​
(44)
Using hours consistently,
λI​=6.7ln2​=0.103455 h−1,λX​=9.2ln2​=0.0753421 h−1,
(45)
and therefore
tmax​=11.2794 h≃11.3 h.​
(46)
The approximate population is positive for t>0, starts at zero, tends back to zero and has only this one stationary point, so it is a maximum.
The delayed maximum corresponds to a delayed maximum in the magnitude of the absorber's negative contribution to reactor reactivity, within the fixed model. It does not determine a permissible restart time, which would require information absent here. The estimate also inherits the high-flux initial-population approximation of part (ii); the exact solution can peak at a different time when that approximation is unsuitable.
