Mitigation should reduce the driving gradients and keep the resulting material response within a demonstrated range. A uniform starting mixture and controlled pore and grain structure reduce pre-existing local compositional and thermal variations. Control of the oxygen-to-metal ratio matters because oxygen chemistry changes both transport and fuel–cladding compatibility.
Reducing peak temperature and unnecessary thermal gradients limits thermally activated redistribution and evaporation–condensation. Possible design responses include improving heat transfer, choosing suitable fuel geometry and thermal properties, and limiting local power peaking. Their benefit has to be checked against neutronic performance, mechanical constraints and changing properties during burnup.
Materials selection and interface design can reduce harmful fuel–cladding chemical interaction. Retaining a suitable gap, limiting cladding attack and accommodating gas and swelling are coupled requirements; simply suppressing one transport pathway can leave other degradation mechanisms.
Finally, use post-irradiation composition profiles and microstructural examinations to test the fuel-performance model. Measurements at different radii and axial positions can reveal whether an assumed homogeneous composition remains adequate. An observed redistribution in one fuel history does not establish a universal correction for all mixed oxide fuel. Mitigation aims at acceptable temperature, integrity and lifetime, rather than promising that every atom remains at its original radial position.
