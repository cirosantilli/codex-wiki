Solution

Words: 195
e OurBigBook.com
Alpha decay releases energy in charged particles with short stopping ranges in matter, so much of their kinetic energy becomes local heat. An appropriate radioisotope thermoelectric generator can use a predictable radioactive decay heat source without requiring sunlight or a sustained nuclear chain reaction. Low penetrating photon and neutron output is desirable, but being an alpha emitter does not by itself establish the complete radiation spectrum.
The isotope's half-life must suit the mission. A short half-life gives high initial specific activity but a rapid decline in useful thermal power; a much longer one gives a steadier output but less activity per atom. Selection also depends on recoverable decay energy, specific power, daughter emissions, chemical and thermal stability, compatibility with the power-conversion system, availability and cost. Practical containment and radiological acceptability remain separate system-level requirements.
A thermoelectric generator converts a temperature difference into electrical output through the Seebeck effect; not all decay heat becomes electricity. NASA's operational account describes this heat-to-electricity distinction and the need to match source lifetime to long space missions.[ref]
For the assumed three-half-life mission, the thermal power falls by a factor of eight. That large decline is the important disadvantage of this particular half-life even though the initial specific thermal power is high.

a OurBigBook.com
Delayed neutrons are emitted after precursor radioactive transformations, rather than promptly at fission. Some neutron-rich fission products undergo beta decay into excited daughter states that can emit a neutron. The waiting time is governed chiefly by the precursor's radioactive lifetime, not by a neutron moving unusually slowly after it has already been emitted.
Although their fraction is small, delayed neutrons introduce a much slower population timescale into the nuclear chain reaction. In the delayed-neutron-controlled regime, changes can therefore develop over times comparable with precursor lifetimes rather than only the prompt neutron generation time. That timescale separation is central to qualitative reactor controllability. It does not imply that every positive-reactivity transient is slow.

The point kinetics model replaces the spatially and spectrally distributed neutron field by a single amplitude n(t), effectively assuming a fixed or sufficiently slowly varying flux shape. The source amplitude is s. A single effective delayed neutron precursor population c(t), delayed neutron fraction β and decay rate λ stand in for many precursor species with different lifetimes. Λ is the prompt neutron generation time and ρ the specified reactor reactivity.
The displayed equations omit explicit thermal-hydraulic, fuel-temperature and isotope-composition feedback. For the stated constant-coefficient transients, ρ,β,Λ,λ are prescribed rather than being solved as evolving fields. The model is useful for an elementary timescale calculation; it does not establish the spatial stability or operating limits of an actual core.

a OurBigBook.com
Cadmium acts as an energy-selective neutron absorber in the stated comparison. Its large thermal capture collision cross-section preferentially reduces the thermal neutron field reaching the source material, while its much lower fast-neutron capture probability allows more of the emitted fast neutrons to escape. This limits thermal neutron-induced changes in the source while retaining its qualitative role as a neutron emitter.
The point is selectivity between incoming thermal and outgoing fast components, rather than a claim that cadmium is transparent to every neutron or that capture produces no secondary radiation. This explains the stated material choice at a conceptual level; no source construction, cladding dimensions or irradiation specification follows from it.

At the stated threshold the idealized system admits a self-sustaining neutron mode. Production and losses balance without the external source. A nonzero continuing source then makes the assumed linear, time-independent response singular rather than selecting a finite equilibrium amplitude.
An apparent infinite steady flux is therefore a failure of that steady linear approximation at the threshold, not a physical prediction that an actual neutron population instantly becomes infinite. Time dependence and feedback must be considered to discuss subsequent behaviour.

a OurBigBook.com
The first balance treats the fertile parent as being removed by neutron absorption. The second has a production contribution associated with capture in the parent and a removal contribution from absorption in the fissile population. Capture and total absorption are not interchangeable: in general absorption includes capture and fission, and a capture channel is the one that feeds the specified transmutation chain.
The lumped description assumes spatially averaged populations and flux, fixed effective reaction coefficients, no transport of material into or out of the system, and a shortened chain in which intermediate decay delays are neglected. It omits explicit precursor accumulation, competing reaction chains and the changing neutron spectrum. It also prescribes flux rather than solving a coupled power-and-composition feedback problem. Neglecting intermediate lifetimes requires them to be short relative to the variation being modelled; that assumption can fail during a rapid change in irradiation.
