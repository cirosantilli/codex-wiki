Both Thomson scattering and Rayleigh scattering can be understood as radiation from a dipole driven by the incident electric field. Their different frequency dependences come from the response of a free charge compared with a bound charge.
For a free particle of charge q and mass m, a harmonic field gives mx¨=qE, hence p=qx=−q2E/(mω2). The radiated field contains two time derivatives of p, cancelling the response's ω−2. The Thomson cross section is consequently independent of frequency in the classical low-recoil regime:

The electron case dominates over equally charged heavier particles because the cross section scales as m−2. recoil corrections become necessary when photon energy is no longer small compared with mc2.

This is Rayleigh scattering. It requires a scatterer small compared with the wavelength and, for the simple expression, an isotropic linear dipole response. Atoms, molecules and small dielectric particles provide examples. The stronger scattering of shorter visible wavelengths explains blue skylight; absorption and the nonuniform solar spectrum prevent treating that simple scaling as a complete color calculation.[ref]
For isotropic electric scatterers the normalized angular and polarization patterns are the same as in Thomson scattering. The frequency scaling distinguishes their limits: a free charge has a displacement proportional to ω−2, whereas a tightly bound charge has an approximately frequency-independent displacement. Near resonance, far outside the small-particle limit, or with anisotropic polarizability, the simple Rayleigh formula must be modified. In particular, the electric-only pattern must not silently be applied to a perfectly conducting sphere whose magnetic dipole response is also retained.
A scattering measurement should subtract stray-light and detector backgrounds, control scatterer density and optical thickness, and calibrate detector response and incident power when comparing wavelengths. A polarization analyzer at 90∘ tests the dipole pattern; multiple scattering or unresolved nonisotropic contributions can reduce its polarization.
