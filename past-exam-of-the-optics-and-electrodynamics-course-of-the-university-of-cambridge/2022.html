The emission mechanism is Cherenkov radiation. An electron moving faster than the phase velocity c/n of light in the material excites electric polarization disturbances whose radiation adds coherently on a cone. This does not require the electron to exceed the speed of light in vacuum. Taking n=1.4 as constant over the detected optical band, the Cherenkov cone angle satisfies
cosθC​=nvc​=nβ1​,β=cv​.
(1)
Emission requires β>1/n. The detector accepts θC​<20∘, so
nβ1​>cos20∘,1.41​<β<1.4cos20∘1​.​
(2)
Numerically,
0.71429c<v<0.76013c,2.1414×108<v<2.2788×108ms−1.​
(3)
The lower endpoint has vanishing Cherenkov radiation yield, and the upper endpoint is excluded by the stated strict angular acceptance. Faster electrons emit at larger cone angles: at v≃c, θC​≃cos−1(1/1.4)=44.4∘, outside this detector's acceptance. The angular condition gives the accepted speed interval conditional on rays reaching the detector; no collection geometry or total signal yield is specified.

Each mutually incoherent patch of an extended star produces a displaced double-slit interference pattern. The detector adds their light intensities. For two apertures separated by a projected interferometric baseline D, a source direction offset by a small angle ϑx​ changes their phase difference by 2πDϑx​/λ. The normalized sum over the source's angular brightness distribution is its first-order optical coherence, whose magnitude is the interferometric visibility for equally bright channels.
For a uniform circular stellar disk of full angular diameter α, the normalized angular Fourier transform gives the uniform-disk stellar visibility
V(D)=​x2J1​(x)​​,x=λπDα​.
(7)
Here J1​ is the order-one Bessel function of the first kind. This follows by integrating ei(2πD/λ)ϑx​ over the disk and dividing by its area. In particular, its power series gives 2J1​(x)/x=1−x2/8+O(x4).
For an ordinary slit separation d=1mm, using the paper's α=22.6×10−8rad and observing wavelength λ=570nm,
λdα​=3.965×10−4,x=1.2456×10−3,1−V≃1.94×10−7.
(8)
Even d=10mm gives a visibility loss of only 1.94×10−5. Thus millimetre separation leaves the interference fringes almost indistinguishable from those of a point source; it gives very little sensitivity to the diameter.
The movable mirrors in the Michelson stellar interferometer collect light at a large adjustable separation D and redirect it into closely spaced output beams. They increase the sampling interferometric baseline independently of the fixed output slit spacing d: D sets sensitivity to source structure, whereas the output optics set the screen's interference fringe spacing. The first Bessel function zero is x1​=3.83170597, hence
Dnull​=πx1​​αλ​=1.2196722.6×10−8570×10−9m​=3.076m≃3.08m.​
(9)
The interference fringes disappear at this baseline even though each beam separately remains bright.
The value 3.08m assumes a uniform disk. The original question supplies a diameter without a surface brightness law; real limb darkening changes the precise visibility zero. A one-dimensional uniformly bright strip of the same full angular width instead has a sinc function visibility and first zero D=λ/α=2.522m. That is a different source model, not the circular-disk result. The robust conclusion without a surface brightness model is that a baseline of a few metres, rather than millimetres, is required.

Birefringence means that two independent polarization eigenmodes have different refractive indices at the same frequency and propagation direction. The rotatory-power formula relevant here requires circular birefringence: the eigenmodes are opposite circular polarizations. General linear birefringence instead introduces phase retardation between linearly polarized components and need not rotate every incident linear polarization through one common angle.
Use the complex amplitude convention Re[Ee−iωt] and transverse unit vectors e1​,e2​. Define

Flint glass is approximately optically isotropic without an applied magnetic field. A longitudinal magnetic field changes the motion of its bound electrons: the magnetic part of the Lorentz force acts with opposite sense on the two circularly rotating components of a driven electronic oscillation. Their optical response, and hence their refractive indices, split. Equivalently, the transverse permittivity tensor acquires a field-odd antisymmetric contribution whose eigenvectors are opposite circular polarizations.
The resulting circular birefringence gives Faraday rotation. For a weak longitudinal bias it is linear in the field:
ρ=ΥB∥​,θ=ΥB∥​L.​
(13)
Here Υ is the Verdet constant and L the path length. The magnetic field breaks time-reversal symmetry, which is why this rotation is nonreciprocal. Reversing the bias reverses the rotation; reversing propagation while keeping the bias fixed does not undo it when physical polarization directions are compared in fixed laboratory axes. A transverse magnetic bias can have different, higher-order effects; the setup here is the longitudinal Faraday effect.

Spin-orbit coupling is a relativistic interaction between a particle's spin and its orbital angular momentum or, more generally, its motion through an electric field. A moving electron experiences a transformed magnetic field, which couples to its magnetic dipole moment. For an electron bound in a central electrostatic potential energy U(r), the leading interaction, including the Thomas precession correction, is
Hso​=2me2​c2r1​drdU​L⋅S.​
(55)
Here L is orbital angular momentum and S is spin. The factor of one-half relative to the simplest rest-field argument comes from Thomas precession of the accelerated electron frame.[ref]
For example, in the hydrogen atom, the 2p states with total angular momentum j=1/2 and j=3/2 have different L⋅S eigenvalues. Their splitting is part of the atomic fine structure. The interaction redistributes and splits spin-dependent energies; it is not the same physical effect as the spin-independent convective magnetic force between charged filaments.

where u is that carrier's laboratory velocity. Exclude its filament's singular self-field and first retain only the prescribed straight convective motion.
In Case 1 there is a common rest frame in which both filaments and the excess charges are stationary and the interfilament magnetic field vanishes. Equivalently, in the laboratory BA​=v×EA​/c2, so B's co-moving charges have BA,rest​=0. Thus uniform common translation alone creates no extra mutual rest-field spin splitting. This does not exclude intrinsic spin-orbit coupling of electrons moving within their atomic binding potentials; that material physics was never specified.
In Case 2 there is relative motion and no frame in which both source distributions are stationary. Put E∗​=η/(2πϵ0​d) as in part (c). At the respective filament axes, the external fields experienced in each carrier distribution's own rest frame are
BA→B(Brest)​=c2γvE∗​​y​,BB→A(Arest)​=c2γvE∗​​y​.​
(57)
For the first expression B is at rest in O; for the second A is at rest in O′ and the field comes from B moving at −vx. For a carrier with magnetic dipole moment μs​, the interaction Hs​=−μs​⋅Brest​ distinguishes opposite transverse spin orientations. A field gradient could exert a spin-dependent force ∇(μs​⋅Brest​) on mobile carriers; spin relaxation could instead produce a spin polarization. These are possible responses, not consequences of charge density alone. In B's frame the displayed interaction is directly a magnetic-moment coupling to the moving source's field; treating the moving carrier's electrostatic orbital term in another frame also requires consistent binding and acceleration dynamics, including any applicable Thomas precession.
For example, if η>0 and mobile electron spins can relax, the lower magnetic-energy orientation has the electron's magnetic moment toward +y​ and its spin toward −y​. Oppositely oriented moments would experience opposite gradient forces. The sign changes if the source charge changes sign. None of these statements fixes a net charge redistribution without a confinement and response model, and the stipulated zero transverse size supplies no resolved “sides” on which charge could accumulate.
Therefore Case 1 has no new mutual spin effect from common bulk motion alone; Case 2 permits motion-induced spin-dependent interactions, but neither case determines an additional charge-density profile from the supplied data. In particular, the factor γ relating proper and laboratory linear charge densities in part (b) follows from length contraction and is not an effect of spin-orbit coupling. If the charges remain pinned in an ideal dielectric, their imposed density remains the prescribed density in either case.
