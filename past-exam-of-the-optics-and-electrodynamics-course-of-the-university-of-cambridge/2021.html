Work in the dilute, single-scattering limit and add the diffuse scattered light intensities of randomly positioned particles. The incident unpolarized light has equal, mutually uncorrelated x and y electric components. Each particle therefore has an induced electric dipole moment along its permitted axis, with the same mean squared magnitude proportional to ∣α∣2.
For an observer in direction n=(sinψ,0,cosψ), choose transverse unit directions e∥​=(cosψ,0,−sinψ) in the scattering plane and e⊥​=y​ perpendicular to it. The electric dipole radiation field is proportional to the transverse projection of the electric dipole. Hence an x electric dipole contributes a wave amplitude factor cosψ along e∥​, while a y electric dipole contributes its full wave amplitude along e⊥​.
Let C>0 collect the common incident light intensity, electric polarizability, distance and frequency factors. Then
I∥​=CN1​cos2ψ,I⊥​=CN2​.
(1)
The two components are uncorrelated, so the coherency matrix is diagonal in this basis. The degree of polarization is
Pxz​(ψ)=I⊥​+I∥​∣I⊥​−I∥​∣​=N2​+N1​cos2ψ∣N2​−N1​cos2ψ∣​.​
(2)
A signed degree of linear polarization, defined positive perpendicular to the scattering plane, has the same expression without the absolute value. The dominant direction is y​ when N2​>N1​cos2ψ, and e∥​ when the inequality reverses. If the denominator vanishes there is no scattered signal and its degree of polarization is undefined. A coherent exactly forward component, including the transmitted incident beam, is not part of this diffuse-intensity calculation; random particle positions do not by themselves destroy forward optical coherence.

The transmitting antenna is matched to its input transmission line, so its real terminal electrical resistance is Zi​. The identical receiving antenna has the same electrical impedance at the operating frequency. Its available received power for a matched load is the incident energy flux times its antenna effective area:
Pav​=4πd2Pi​Ga​​Ae​=Pi​(4πdGa​λ​)2≡Pi​F.
(4)

Distinguish reflection at the input terminal, reflection at the receiving load, and a returned signal that traverses the free-space link twice. The input antenna is initially matched, so its direct terminal reflection is zero.
In the usual weak-coupling, matched-port two-port network model for the antenna pair, let the forward and reverse wave amplitude transfer coefficients be t21​ and t12​. Electromagnetic reciprocity gives equal power transfer factors ∣t21​∣2=∣t12​∣2=F, where F=(Ga​λ/(4πd))2 from part (a). The receiving-load reflection returns wave amplitude t12​ΓL​t21​ to the original input port. The load-mismatch contribution to the returned power is therefore
Prload​=Pi​∣ΓL​∣2F2=Pi​(Zt​+Zi​Zt​−Zi​​)2(4πdGa​λ​)4.​
(7)
For fixed gains and impedances this returned power scales as λ4: it contains two link traversals. By contrast, the reflected power at the receiving load before that return traversal is Pi​F∣ΓL​∣2∝λ2. Calling that local reflected power the power already back in the input line would omit one propagation loss.
The original wording does not specify an antenna scattering model. The boxed result is the load-dependent echo in an ideal matched-port link, with structural scattering and residual input coupling neglected. The actual total input return can include coherent scattering from the receiving antenna even when ΓL​=0; gains and effective areas alone do not specify that background. If “reflected back” means only the direct reflection at the stated matched transmitting antenna, its value is 0. These meanings should not be conflated; the λ4 result requires the two-pass echo interpretation and its stated model.

f OurBigBook.com
Yes: the same physical resonant cavity and its field remain resonant in every inertial description. A Lorentz transformation changes the mirror worldlines and the field together. The boundary phase matching and the constructive-interference condition are statements about the same reflection events, and the contracted wave phase is a Lorentz scalar. A boosted resonant solution therefore still satisfies the moving-mirror boundary conditions.
It would be incorrect merely to insert a Lorentz-contracted length into the formula for two stationary mirrors while leaving both propagation frequencies unchanged. For example, let the mirrors be separated by L in their rest frame and let ω0​L/c=mπ, including the usual equal-mirror reflection-phase convention. For an observer boosted at +v along the cavity axis, the cavity moves at −v and its two travelling components have frequencies
ω+′​=γω0​(1−β),ω−′​=γω0​(1+β).
(59)
They need not have one common laboratory frequency. The simultaneous mirror separation is L′=L/γ, while the forward and return flight times are L′/(c+v) and L′/(c−v). The round-trip interval between events on the same mirror is therefore 2γL/c.
Along that moving mirror the wave phase rate of either wave component is ω′−k′vmirror​=ω0​/γ. Its advance over the round trip is
γω0​​c2γL​=c2ω0​L​=2πm.​
(60)
Thus the resonance condition is unchanged even though the observed travelling frequencies, lengths and flight times all change. For a boost in a general direction the beam directions also undergo relativistic aberration; the event-by-event phase-invariance argument still applies. A separate stationary cavity placed in the new frame, or an external laser whose frequency is not transformed with the original setup, is a different physical system.
