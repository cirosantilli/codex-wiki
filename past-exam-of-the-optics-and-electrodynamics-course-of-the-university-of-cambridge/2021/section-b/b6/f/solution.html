Yes: the same physical resonant cavity and its field remain resonant in every inertial description. A Lorentz transformation changes the mirror worldlines and the field together. The boundary phase matching and the constructive-interference condition are statements about the same reflection events, and the contracted wave phase is a Lorentz scalar. A boosted resonant solution therefore still satisfies the moving-mirror boundary conditions.
It would be incorrect merely to insert a Lorentz-contracted length into the formula for two stationary mirrors while leaving both propagation frequencies unchanged. For example, let the mirrors be separated by L in their rest frame and let ω0​L/c=mπ, including the usual equal-mirror reflection-phase convention. For an observer boosted at +v along the cavity axis, the cavity moves at −v and its two travelling components have frequencies
ω+′​=γω0​(1−β),ω−′​=γω0​(1+β).
(59)
They need not have one common laboratory frequency. The simultaneous mirror separation is L′=L/γ, while the forward and return flight times are L′/(c+v) and L′/(c−v). The round-trip interval between events on the same mirror is therefore 2γL/c.
Along that moving mirror the wave phase rate of either wave component is ω′−k′vmirror​=ω0​/γ. Its advance over the round trip is
γω0​​c2γL​=c2ω0​L​=2πm.​
(60)
Thus the resonance condition is unchanged even though the observed travelling frequencies, lengths and flight times all change. For a boost in a general direction the beam directions also undergo relativistic aberration; the event-by-event phase-invariance argument still applies. A separate stationary cavity placed in the new frame, or an external laser whose frequency is not transformed with the original setup, is a different physical system.
