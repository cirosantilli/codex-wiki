Brownian motion (Bt​, Wt​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Stochastic process
OurBigBook.com Words: 13k Articles: 207
Standard one-dimensional Brownian motion is a real-valued stochastic process (Bt​)t≥0​ on a probability space with three defining properties: B0​=0 almost surely; its sample paths t↦Bt​ are continuous almost surely; and its Brownian increments over any finite collection of disjoint time intervals are independent, with
Bt​−Bs​∼N(0,t−s)(0≤s<t).
(1)
Here N(m,v) denotes the normal distribution with expectation m and variance v. The alternative symbol Wt​ denotes the same kind of stochastic process. Continuity is a simultaneous statement about an entire sample path, rather than a separate assertion at each fixed time. Relative to its natural Brownian filtration, a future Brownian increment is independent of all observations up to its starting time.
The normalization gives Bt​∼N(0,t), so the standard deviation of a Brownian increment over duration h is h​. For h=1, 0.01, and 0.0001, the corresponding standard deviations are 1, 0.1, and 0.01. Time becoming one hundred times shorter makes typical displacement ten times smaller. Brownian scaling expresses this rule for the whole stochastic process, while the Brownian covariance kernel describes how positions at different times remain related despite independent increments.
For a concrete probability calculation, let Φ be the cumulative distribution function of N(0,1). At time four, B4​/2 has that normal distribution, hence
P(∣B4​∣≤1)=Φ(1/2)−Φ(−1/2)=2Φ(1/2)−1≈0.3829249225,P(B4​>2)=1−Φ(1)≈0.1586552539.
(2)
These numbers can be reproduced using the error function: Φ(z)=(1+erf(z/2​))/2. In particular, zero expectation does not mean small displacement: P(∣B1​∣≤1)≈0.6826894921, but the same fixed interval contains only about 38.3% of the probability at time four.
The probability density function of Bt​, for t>0, is
p(t,x)=2πt​1​e−x2/(2t).
(3)
Its partial derivatives satisfy
∂t​p=(−2t1​+2t2x2​)p,∂x2​p=(−t1​+t2x2​)p,∂t​p=21​∂x2​p.
(4)
Thus the normal distribution spreads according to the heat equation. Starting at a fixed position x0​ replaces x by x−x0​. This is a useful bridge between individual random trajectories and the deterministic evolution of their probability density function: the same Brownian motion provides both descriptions.
Physical diffusion uses its own scale. For constant diffusion coefficient D>0, the position model Xt​=x0​+2D​Bt​ has variance 2Dt, and its probability density function satisfies ∂t​pD​=D∂x2​pD​. For example, D=0.5μm2/s gives displacement variance 4μm2 after four seconds, hence standard deviation 2μm. Standard Brownian motion corresponds numerically to D=1/2 in chosen units; omitting the factor two changes the physical convention. Einstein's 1905 paper relates diffusion to the mean squared displacement in precisely this convention. [ref]
Continuity does not supply a smooth physical velocity. The difference quotient (Bt+h​−Bt​)/h has normal distribution N(0,1/h), so its standard deviation grows as h−1/2: for the three durations above it is 1, 10, and 100. This calculation motivates the nowhere differentiability of Brownian motion, but it alone is not a proof of the simultaneous assertion at every time. The theorem says that, with probability one, no finite derivative exists anywhere along the sample path. A drawn polygon only displays a finite grid; its straight segments cannot establish this theorem. See Theorem 7.1.6 in Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett.
For physical particles, Brownian motion is a model of position on suitable observation scales. It does not claim that microscopic velocity is a derivative of this idealized path. Einstein explicitly restricts his assumption of independent increments to intervals that are not too short. [ref]
Another way to measure the roughness is quadratic variation. On [0,T], divide time into n equal intervals and form
Qn​=∑k=1n​(BkT/n​−B(k−1)T/n​)2.
(5)
Writing each Brownian increment as T/n​Zk​ with independent standard normal random variables gives Qn​=(T/n)∑k​Zk2​. Since EZk2​=1 and EZk4​=3, the variance of Zk2​ is 2, and
EQn​=T,Var(Qn​)=n2T2​,E[(Qn​−T)2]=n2T2​⟶0.
(6)
This proves mean-square convergence to T along uniform partitions. For T=2 and n=100, the expectation is 2, the variance is 0.08, and the root mean square of the error is 0.08​≈0.2828427125. For a general deterministic partition of an interval with lengths hk​, the same calculation gives error variance 2∑k​hk2​≤2Tmaxk​hk​, so shrinking the largest interval also suffices.
The resulting quadratic variation is [B]T​=T, whereas the smooth function f(t)=t gives Qn​=T2/n→0. This difference explains why the ordinary rules of calculus need the second-order correction in the Itô formula. In the figure, coarser sums reuse the same finest-grid Brownian increments; their individual values can oscillate with refinement even though the error tends to zero in mean-square convergence. Refining a fixed interpolating polygon forever would instead give zero quadratic variation, so the finite-grid illustration must not be confused with the limiting Brownian motion.
Historically, Brown's 1828 report describes microscopic observations made in 1827, starting with particles released from pollen and extending to inorganic materials; this challenged an explanation based solely on living matter. His observations and the later mathematical model are distinct contributions. [ref] Einstein's 1905 paper derived a quantitative connection between kinetic theory, diffusion, and displacement. [ref] Wiener's 1923 Differential-Space developed integration on spaces of paths, a foundation for Wiener measure; it also acknowledges earlier work and his own previous papers. These dates mark documented contributions rather than an assertion that each subject had a single discoverer. [ref]
