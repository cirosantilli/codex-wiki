Hermitian operator (T=T∗)

 ... Area of mathematics Analysis Functional analysis Hilbert space Riesz representation theorem Adjoint operator
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 16
A bounded operator T:H→H on a real or complex Hilbert space is Hermitian, or self-adjoint, when T=T∗. Equivalently,
⟨Tx,y⟩=⟨x,Ty⟩(x,y∈H).
(28)
Its directional values qT​(x)=⟨Tx,x⟩ are real, since this identity makes qT​(x) equal its complex conjugate. Reality allows either sign; a positive operator additionally requires nonnegative values in every direction.
The diagram compares Hermitian quadratic forms along paths of unit vectors. The Hermitian matrix D=diag(1,−1) takes the values 1 and −1 on the standard basis vectors; P=diag(1,0) takes the values 1 and 0; and
H=(2−i​i2​),e1​=2​1​(1i​),e3​=2​1​(1−i​)
(29)
satisfies He1​=e1​ and He3​=3e3​. These eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis, and every x=ae1​+be3​ has qH​(x)=∣a∣2+3∣b∣2. Thus H is a positive operator, with eigenvalues 1,3 and operator norm 3.
Three conditions describe different geometry. A Hermitian operator satisfies T=T∗; a normal operator satisfies T∗T=TT∗; a unitary operator satisfies T∗T=TT∗=I, where I is the identity operator. Every Hermitian operator and every unitary operator is a normal operator. Concrete matrices separate the conditions:
  • The Hermitian matrix H above is a normal operator and a positive operator, but not a unitary matrix, because ∥He3​∥=3.
  • U=diag(i,1) is a unitary matrix and normal matrix, but not a Hermitian matrix, since U∗=diag(−i,1).
  • N=diag(2,i) is a normal matrix, but neither a Hermitian matrix nor a unitary matrix: its first standard basis vector doubles in norm.
  • J=(00​10​) is not a normal matrix, since J∗J=diag(0,1) and JJ∗=diag(1,0).
The spectrum of a bounded Hermitian operator is real. In finite dimension, the spectral theorem supplies an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, turning its action into independent real scalings. This makes the geometry easy to interpret: each eigenvalue records a signed response along an orthogonal direction. An arbitrary bounded Hermitian operator in infinite dimension need not have any eigenvectors; the general spectral theorem uses a projection-valued measure. MIT's lecture 21 develops the real spectrum and the orthogonality of eigenspaces.[ref]
A historical connection is the study of forms with conjugate variables. In his 1901 account of Charles Hermite's work, Camille Jordan describes these forms and emphasizes their role in reducing forms with real or complex coefficients. In today's notation, x∗Hx is the diagonal value of a Hermitian form; the conjugate variables make a real-valued expression possible even with complex coefficients.[ref]
For a densely defined operator outside this bounded setting, the displayed pairing identity alone defines a symmetric operator. A self-adjoint operator must also have the same operator domain as its adjoint operator.
