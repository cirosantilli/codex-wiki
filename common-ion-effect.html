Common-ion effect

The common-ion effect is the change in a chemical equilibrium caused by adding an ion already participating in it. In the fixed-activity coefficient AB model, adding the common B anion lowers the molar solubility. The shared ion participates regardless of which salt supplied it.[ref]
The preceding solubility product and material balance give a short proof without a qualitative slogan. Taking a derivative of s∗2​+c0​s∗​=Ksp,c​ at fixed Ksp,c​ gives
(2s∗​+c0​)dc0​ds∗​​+s∗​=0,dc0​ds∗​​=−2s∗​+c0​s∗​​<0.
(11)
Thus the molar solubility decreases continuously as c0​ increases. At c0​=0, the derivative is −1/2; for a very large common-ion background, the rationalized expression approaches Ksp,c​/c0​. The solubility product is fixed within this model even while the molar solubility changes.
Neglecting s∗​ beside c0​ replaces s∗​(c0​+s∗​)=Ksp,c​ by c0​sapp​=Ksp,c​. This approximation always overestimates the exact molar solubility, since it neglects the B anions supplied by AB itself. For c0​>0, its relative error has an exact simple form:
sapp​=c0​Ksp,c​​=s∗​+c0​s∗2​​,s∗​sapp​−s∗​​=c0​s∗​​.
(12)
At c0​=0.030M, sapp​=0.003333333M, approximately 10.1% too high. At c0​=0.100M, s∗​=0.000990195M and sapp​=0.001000000M, approximately 0.990% too high. A small solubility product by itself does not justify the approximation: the common-ion background must be large compared with the resulting molar solubility.
For a desired fractional relative error at most ε>0, substituting s∗​/c0​≤ε into the exact balance gives the equivalent criterion
c0​≥ε(1+ε)Ksp,c​​​.
(13)
For this model, a 5% tolerance requires c0​≥0.0436436M. This bound concerns only the algebraic approximation. In a real chemical solution, changes in ionic strength and activity coefficients, or competing chemical reactions, can invalidate a constant Ksp,c​ even when this bound is met.
