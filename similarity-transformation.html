Similarity transformation (A↦S−1AS)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Matrix similarity
OurBigBook.com Words: 535 Articles: 4
A similarity transformation replaces a square matrix by a similar matrix. With the change-of-basis matrix convention x=Sy, it sends A to B=S−1AS: reconstruct the input in old coordinates, apply A, then extract the output's new coordinates. These three operations explain the order of the factors.
The equivalent formula A↦SAS−1 uses S as the conversion from old to new coordinates, reversing the role assigned to S above. For the opening example's numerical S, this opposite conversion gives SAS−1=(20​35​). If the old basis is E, the new basis vectors in that convention are the columns of S−1, namely e1​ and −e1​+e2​. Both formulas are similarity transformations; the direction of the coordinate conversion decides which one describes a specified basis.
An especially useful form is the intertwining operator equation
AS=SB.
(87)
It says that applying B before reconstruction by S gives the same physical output as reconstructing first and applying A. Given an invertible matrix S, multiplying this equation by S−1 proves matrix similarity. Without invertibility, the equation alone does not prove matrix similarity.
