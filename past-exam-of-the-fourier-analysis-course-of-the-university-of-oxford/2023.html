Use the Fourier transform convention f​(ξ)=∫R​f(x)e−ixξdx, with complex-linear distribution pairings throughout. The Riemann-Lebesgue lemma says that, for an integrable function f∈L1(R), its Fourier transform is continuous and tends to zero as ∣ξ∣→∞. It also satisfies ∥f​∥∞​≤∥f∥1​. The product rule, or convolution theorem, is
f∗g​=f​g​(f,g∈L1(R)).​
(1)
Here (f∗g)(x)=∫f(y)g(x−y)dy belongs to L1(R) by Tonelli's theorem, and Fubini's theorem proves the displayed identity. The companion Fourier product formula, for Schwartz functions, is fg​=(2π)−1f​∗g​.
A Schwartz function is a smooth function ϕ:R→C for which every Schwartz seminorm
pm,n​(ϕ):=supx∈R​∣xmϕ(n)(x)∣,m,n∈{0,1,2,…},
(2)
is finite. These seminorms define the topology of the Schwartz space S(R). In particular,
∣ϕ(x)∣≤1+x2p0,0​(ϕ)+p2,0​(ϕ)​,∥ϕ∥1​≤π(p0,0​(ϕ)+p2,0​(ϕ))<∞.​
(3)

ξm∂ξn​ϕ​(ξ)=i−mF[∂xm​((−ix)nϕ)](ξ),

(5)
In particular the Fourier transform maps the Schwartz space continuously into itself.

A tempered distribution is a continuous linear functional u:S(R)→C. Equivalently, for some integer N and constant C,
∣⟨u,ϕ⟩∣≤C∑0≤m,n≤N​pm,n​(ϕ)(ϕ∈S(R)).
(10)
The Fourier transform of a tempered distribution is defined by
⟨u,ϕ⟩:=⟨u,ϕ​⟩.​
(11)
The Fourier bounds for Schwartz functions make the right side a continuous linear functional on the Schwartz space, so u is again a tempered distribution.
For an integrable function f, the regular distribution uf​ acts by ⟨uf​,ϕ⟩=∫fϕ. The estimate ∣⟨uf​,ϕ⟩∣≤∥f∥1​p0,0​(ϕ) proves it is a tempered distribution. Since ∬∣f(x)ϕ(ξ)∣dxdξ<∞, Fubini's theorem gives
⟨uf​​,ϕ⟩=∫f(x)∫ϕ(ξ)e−ixξdξdx=∫f​(ξ)ϕ(ξ)dξ=⟨uf​​,ϕ⟩.
(12)
Thus the distributional Fourier transform agrees with the ordinary Fourier transform.

The 2π periodization of a Schwartz function is
Pϕ(x)=k∈Z∑​ϕ(x+2πk).​
(38)
For every nonnegative integer j, the Schwartz seminorms give ∣ϕ(j)(x+2πk)∣≤Cj​(1+∣k∣)−2 uniformly for x∈[0,2π]. The Weierstrass M-test gives absolute and uniform convergence there of the defining series and all its differentiated series. Termwise differentiation proves that Pϕ is a smooth function, with (Pϕ)(j)=P(ϕ(j)). Reindexing the absolutely convergent series proves Pϕ(x+2π)=Pϕ(x), so it is a periodic function.
For an explicit smooth periodic partition of unity, choose the bump function
η(x)={exp(−1/(1−(x/(2π))2)),0,​∣x∣<2π,∣x∣≥2π.​
(39)
It is a test function, positive on [−π,π]. The smooth function S(x)=Pη(x) is 2π periodic and strictly positive, since every x has a translate in [−π,π]. Set
Ψ(x)=S(x)η(x)​∈Cc∞​(R),PΨ(x)=S(x)∑k​η(x+2πk)​=1.​
(40)
Division preserves smoothness because S>0, and the support remains contained in [−2π,2π].

A distribution u is a 2π periodic distribution when
⟨u,θ(⋅+2π)⟩=⟨u,θ⟩(θ∈Cc∞​(R)).​
(41)
Iteration gives invariance under all integer multiples of 2π. For a tempered distribution the identity also holds for every Schwartz function, since test functions are dense in the Schwartz space: multiplying by cutoff functions χ(x/R) equal to one near zero gives such an approximation in every Schwartz seminorm.
For ϕ∈S(R) define ak​(x)=Ψ(x+2πk)ϕ(x). The smooth periodic partition of unity gives ϕ=∑k​ak​ with convergence in the Schwartz space. Indeed, each ak​ is supported where ∣x+2πk∣≤2π, and the generalized Leibniz rule and arbitrarily rapid decay of the derivatives of ϕ give, for every m,n,M,
pm,n​(ak​)≤Cm,n,M,ϕ​(1+∣k∣)−M.
(42)
Consequently continuity and periodicity of the tempered distribution give
⟨u,ϕ⟩​=k∑​⟨u,Ψ(⋅+2πk)ϕ⟩=k∑​⟨u,Ψ(⋅)ϕ(⋅−2πk)⟩=⟨u,Ψ(⋅)k∑​ϕ(⋅−2πk)⟩=⟨u,ΨPϕ⟩​.​
(43)
For the last interchange, all summands have the fixed compact support of Ψ and their derivatives decrease faster than any power of ∣k∣. Hence the series converges in the space of test functions, as well as in the Schwartz space. Notice that Pϕ itself need not be a Schwartz function; the factor Ψ makes the pairing legitimate.
