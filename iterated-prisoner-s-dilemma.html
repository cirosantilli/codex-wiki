Iterated prisoner's dilemma

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Game theory Prisoner's dilemma
OurBigBook.com Words: 893 Articles: 9
The iterated prisoner's dilemma is a repeated game with a prisoner's dilemma at each round. Earlier choices can change later best responses: a present gain from defection may be outweighed by lost future cooperation. A pure strategy now specifies an action after every possible observed history, including histories that its own prescribed play would never reach. The horizon, observations, initial actions, and payoff convention must be specified before assessing a Nash equilibrium or subgame perfect equilibrium.
For the following worked example, repeat the prisoner's dilemma above infinitely at rounds t=0,1,…. Assume public perfect monitoring: after each round both players observe both actions exactly, without error. They share a discount factor 0≤δ<1 and evaluate a path of stage payoffs ui​(t) using the normalized discounted payoff
Ui​=(1−δ)∑t=0∞​δtui​(t).
(2)
The round-zero weight is one before normalization, including when δ=0. A larger discount factor gives future payoffs more weight; the geometric series makes a constant stage payoff have the same normalized value. These assumptions and normalization follow [ref]. The numerical payoffs here are our 3,5,0,1 example; the lecture uses a different table.
Suppose both players use grim trigger: play C initially and while every past action of both players was C; after any observed D, play D forever. With no unilateral deviation, each gets normalized payoff 3. A first unilateral deviation to D earns 5 now, then at most 1 each round because the opponent switches permanently to always defect. The deviator attains that continuation payoff by choosing D thereafter. At any history of mutual cooperation, the comparison is therefore
Ucooperate​=3,Udeviate​=(1−δ)5+δ⋅1=5−4δ.
(3)
Cooperation is a best response precisely when
3≥5−4δ⟺δ≥21​.
(4)
Equivalently, without normalization the geometric series gives
Vcooperate​=1−δ3​,Vdeviate​=5+1−δδ​.
(5)
Multiplying both unnormalized payoffs by the same positive factor 1−δ leaves the best response comparison unchanged.
For example, with discount factor δ=1/4, the first unilateral deviation gives normalized payoff 4>3, so mutual grim trigger is not a Nash equilibrium. At δ=3/4, it gives 2<3, so cooperation is preferable. At δ=1/2 the two payoffs tie: the incentive to cooperate is weak, which is sufficient for a subgame perfect equilibrium.
The punishment is credible after every history containing a defection: the opponent's pure strategy then prescribes D forever, and D remains the stage best response, earning 1 rather than 0. Together with the cooperative-history comparison, this establishes mutual grim trigger as a subgame perfect equilibrium for 1/2≤δ<1. Delaying the first unilateral deviation merely delays the same gain or loss; further cooperation during punishment only lowers the deviator's payoff. Mutual always defect also remains a subgame perfect equilibrium, so patience permits cooperation without making it inevitable.
This calculation assumes an infinite horizon and δ<1; it does not define a discounted payoff at δ=1. With a finite, commonly known final round and positive weight on each round, backward induction instead gives mutual defection in every round of this stage game. Observational errors change the model too: one mistaken defection permanently activates grim trigger. Historical dependence, initial memories, and the payoff time convention also affect whether a cooperative rule resists or invades other pure strategies; the existing reactive strategy and Tit for tat discussions concern those additional questions.
