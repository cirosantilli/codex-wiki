Matching (graph theory)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 5k Articles: 38
A matching in a graph is a set M of edges of an undirected graph with no shared endpoints: each vertex has capacity for at most one selected edge. It describes simultaneous compatible pairs, such as jobs assigned to distinct available workers, or tasks each occupying two resources that cannot be shared. The empty set is a valid matching in a graph.[ref]
For a finite simple graph G=(V,E), write xe​=1 when e is selected and xe​=0 otherwise. The vertex capacity constraint is exactly
xe​∈{0,1}(e∈E),∑e∈E:v∈e​xe​≤1(v∈V).
(1)
These are constraints on a discrete selection, not a requirement that every vertex be used. A matching in a graph with matching saturation of all vertices is a perfect matching; an unmatched vertex in a matching uses none of its capacity.
The objective must be specified separately. A maximum cardinality matching selects as many edges as possible; a maximum-weight matching selects the greatest total benefit; a minimum-weight perfect matching minimizes cost while requiring every vertex to be paired. A maximal matching only says that no additional edge fits alongside the current choices. The four-vertex path graph below separates these notions: choosing the middle edge blocks both outer edges, yet replacing it by those two edges pairs more vertices.
This model is useful because conflicts are local at vertices, while the quality of a selection can depend on coordinated changes along a whole augmenting path in a matching. A greedy matching gives a quick feasible selection; Berge's theorem explains when searching for such changes has reached a maximum cardinality matching. Repeating the pairing problem over time leads instead to proper edge colouring, where each time slot is a matching in a graph.

Unmatched vertex in a matching

Words: 42
Matching (graph theory) OurBigBook.com
A vertex is an unmatched vertex in a matching M if no edge of M is incident to it. It is also called free or exposed, always relative to the specified matching in a graph: changing M can change which vertices are free. The endpoints of an augmenting path in a matching must be distinct unmatched vertices in a matching before the change.

Matching saturation

Words: 46
Matching (graph theory) OurBigBook.com
A matching in a graph saturates a specified vertex set X when every vertex of X is incident to one selected edge. Exactly one selected edge meets each such vertex, since matching edges have disjoint endpoints. Vertices outside X may remain unmatched. A perfect matching saturates the entire vertex set; Hall's marriage theorem tests matching saturation of a specified side in a finite bipartite graph.

Stable matching in a bipartite graph

Words: 1k Articles: 5
Matching (graph theory) OurBigBook.com
A stable matching in a bipartite graph pairs two groups so that no acceptable unpaired pair would both rather be together. Give a finite bipartite graph G=(L∪R,E) a strict preference ordering at each vertex over its neighbours of a vertex. An edge means that both endpoints regard the pair as acceptable; every acceptable partner ranks above being unmatched. Lists may be incomplete, and L and R may have unequal cardinalities. A matching in a graph M⊆E is stable when it has no blocking pair in stable matching.
Write M(v) for the partner of a vertex v, or ⊥ if v is an unmatched vertex in a matching. An unused edge (ℓ,r) is a blocking pair in stable matching precisely when
r≻ℓ​M(ℓ)andℓ≻r​M(r),
(2)
where ≻v​ denotes the strict preference at v and ⊥ ranks below every neighbour of a vertex. Both inequalities matter: wanting a different partner alone cannot upset a stable matching. A stable matching need not be a perfect matching; the Gale-Shapley algorithm still finds one with incomplete lists and unequal sides.
Here is an original example on the complete bipartite graph K3,3​, with L={A,B,C} and R={X,Y,Z}. Read each row from best to worst:
leftABC​1XXY​2YYZ​3ZZX​rightXYZ​1CAA​2ABB​3BCC​​
(3)
There are two stable matchings,
ML​={(A,X),(B,Y),(C,Z)},MR​={(A,Y),(B,Z),(C,X)}.
(4)
For ML​, the only unpaired partners preferred by left vertices are B wanting X and C wanting Y. But X prefers its partner A to B, and Y prefers its partner B to C. Hence neither is a blocking pair in stable matching. For MR​, the left vertices want A with X, B with X or Y, and C with Y or Z. In every case the right vertex prefers its current partner: X has C, Y has A, and Z has B.
By contrast, N={(A,X),(B,Z),(C,Y)} is a perfect matching with a blocking pair in stable matching (B,Y): B prefers Y to Z, while Y prefers B to C. The abandoned partners need not agree to the change; stability tests the incentives of the two endpoints of the unused edge.
There are 3!=6 permutations of right partners. The following table lists every blocking pair in stable matching for each perfect matching; the middle column gives the partners of A,B,C in that order:
ML​NMR​​(M(A),M(B),M(C))(X,Y,Z)(X,Z,Y)(Y,X,Z)(Y,Z,X)(Z,X,Y)(Z,Y,X)​blocking pairsnone(B,Y)(A,X)none(A,X), (A,Y)(A,Y)​​
(5)
This exhausts the stable matchings: any nonperfect matching in a graph here leaves an unmatched vertex on each side, and those two form a blocking pair in stable matching because every cross-pair is acceptable. Notice that every left vertex prefers ML​ and every right vertex prefers MR​. Stability can accommodate opposing interests without selecting a single universally preferred outcome.
A stable matching addresses the possibility of mutually beneficial departures. Its definition contains no numerical total social benefit or maximum-weight matching objective. For example, assigning benefit 100 to each edge of N and zero to every other edge makes N the unique maximum-weight matching, yet (B,Y) still blocks it under the displayed preference relations. Such external benefits need not represent participants' rankings.

Mutual-worst edge in stable matchings

Words: 98
Stable matching in a bipartite graph OurBigBook.com
If the endpoints of an edge rank one another last and one stable matching in a bipartite graph contains that edge, every stable matching contains it. In the symmetric difference of two stable matchings, preference comparisons propagate along alternating paths: if a vertex prefers its new partner, that partner must prefer its old partner to avoid blocking the old matching. An alternating path cannot end unmatched on the preferred side, so the component is an alternating cycle, on which the two parts prefer opposite matchings. A mutual-worst edge absent from the new matching would make both its endpoints prefer the new matching, contradicting this propagation.

Gale-Shapley algorithm

Words: 848 Articles: 2
Stable matching in a bipartite graph OurBigBook.com
The Gale-Shapley algorithm constructs a stable matching by letting one side propose and the other hold its best offer so far. The word "deferred" is essential: a held proposal is tentative and can be replaced. David Gale and Lloyd Shapley presented this construction in their 1962 paper, College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage, §3, printed pp. 12–13; §4 extends it to college quotas and unacceptable pairs.
For the one-partner bipartite graph model, designate L as proposers and R as receivers:
  • Start with the empty matching in a graph. Each proposer keeps a pointer to its first untried acceptable partner.
  • Choose an unmatched vertex in a matching ℓ∈L whose list is not exhausted. It proposes to its highest-ranked untried neighbour of a vertex r and advances its pointer permanently.
  • If r holds nobody, it holds ℓ. Otherwise r compares ℓ with its currently held proposer, keeps the one it prefers, and rejects the other. A displaced proposer becomes unmatched and may propose again later.
  • Stop when every unmatched proposer has exhausted its list. The held pairs are the output matching in a graph.
Held proposers wait; only unmatched proposers act. A receiver can replace its held partner, whereas a proposer never tries the same edge twice. Isolated vertices simply remain unmatched. These rules also describe incomplete acceptable lists and unequal sides without adding fictitious acceptable partners.
For the preference lists under stable matching, choose available proposers in queue data structure order, initially A,B,C, adding rejected proposers at the end. The proposals are A→X, B→X, C→Y, B→Y, C→Z. At the fourth proposal, Y replaces its held C by B because B≻Y​C. This frees C to try Z, producing ML​.
Reverse the proposing side. Starting in the order X,Y,Z, the proposals are X→C, Y→A, Z→A, Z→B. Now A rejects Z because it already holds Y. The result is MR​. Thus the choice of proposing side can change the output even with strict preferences and complete lists.
Why does the procedure work? Each edge receives at most one proposal, so at most ∣E∣ proposals occur. At all times, the held edges form a matching in a graph, and each receiver holds its favourite proposer among all proposals received. A receiver's held partner can only improve according to its strict preference. If a proposer prefers r to its final partner, or ends unmatched while r is acceptable, it must already have proposed to r. A rejection means r then held someone better, and its final partner is at least as good. Such an edge cannot be a blocking pair in stable matching. This covers every possible blocking pair in stable matching, proving stability at termination.
With a queue data structure of unmatched proposers, a next-choice pointer for each list, and constant-time receiver-rank lookups, each proposal takes constant work. Using Big O notation, the time complexity is O(∣E∣+∣V∣) including initialization, provided list construction and rank preprocessing also take O(∣E∣+∣V∣). Edge-indexed ranks with cross-referenced list entries, or expected constant-time hash lookups, allow sparse storage proportional to ∣E∣+∣V∣. Repeatedly scanning a receiver's preference list to compare offers would lose this bound. For complete lists with n vertices per side, ∣E∣=n2 and the bound is O(n2). Initializing full n×n rank tables itself costs Θ(n2) time and space, even when only a sparse set of pairs is acceptable.
The Gale-Shapley algorithm does more than establish existence: with strict preferences, its output is a proposer-optimal stable matching.[ref] It is also a receiver-pessimal stable matching, as proved below. Both qualifications compare partners across stable matchings.

Proposer-optimal stable matching

Words: 270 Articles: 1
Gale-Shapley algorithm OurBigBook.com
For fixed strict preferences, a proposer-optimal stable matching gives each proposer its best partner obtainable in any stable matching, with unmatched ranked last. The Gale-Shapley algorithm achieves all these individual optima simultaneously. In the worked example, ML​ gives A,B,C partners ranked 1,2,2, respectively; the alternative MR​ gives them ranks 2,3,3.
Suppose a first rejection of a pair belonging to some stable matching S occurs when r rejects ℓ for ℓ′. Every partner ℓ′ prefers to r has already rejected it, so none can be its partner in S. If S(r)=ℓ, then ℓ′ prefers r to S(ℓ′), while r prefers ℓ′ to ℓ: a blocking pair in stable matching, a contradiction. Hence no rejected pair is stable, and each proposer receives its best stable partner. Under strict preferences this also makes the outcome independent of proposal order.
Receiver-pessimal stable matching
Words: 142
Proposer-optimal stable matching OurBigBook.com
For fixed strict preferences, the same output of the Gale-Shapley algorithm gives each receiver its worst partner obtainable in any stable matching, treating unmatched as last. "Worst" is restricted to stable possibilities: a receiver might still receive its favourite acceptable partner if every stable matching assigns it that partner. Reversing the proposing side gives receivers their best stable partners instead.
For a short proof, let M be a proposer-optimal stable matching and S any other stable matching. If a receiver r preferred M(r)=ℓ to S(r), then proposer optimality would make ℓ prefer r=M(ℓ) to S(ℓ), since the partners differ and preferences are strict. The pair (ℓ,r) would block S. Therefore no receiver prefers its partner in M to its partner in S. In the worked example, right-side ranks in ML​ are 2,2,3 for X,Y,Z, whereas MR​ gives them 1,1,2. Neither outcome is universally best for both sides.

Alternating path in a matching

An alternating path in a matching M is a graph path whose successive edges alternate between membership in M and membership outside M. Here graph paths have no repeated vertices. Alternation alone does not make a graph path an augmenting path in a matching: its endpoints must both be unmatched vertices in a matching. For example, relative to M={(2,3),(4,5)} in the six-vertex path graph, 2,3,4,5 alternates but has matched endpoints; toggling just those three edges loses one selected edge.

Augmenting path in a matching

Words: 582 Articles: 2
Matching (graph theory) OurBigBook.com
An augmenting path in a matching M is a nonempty alternating path in a matching with distinct endpoints that are both unmatched vertices in a matching. Its first and last edges lie outside M. Thus it has k+1 unselected edges and k selected edges, for some k≥0. Taking the symmetric difference
M′=M△E(P)=(M∖E(P))∪(E(P)∖M)
(6)
removes the selected edges on P and inserts its unselected edges. Each internal vertex exchanges one selected edge for another; each endpoint gains its first selected edge. All choices away from P stay in place, so M′ is a matching in a graph with ∣M′∣=∣M∣+1.[ref]
For an explicit repair, let the path graph P6​ have vertices 1,2,3,4,5,6 in that order and start with
M={(2,3),(4,5)},P=(1,2,3,4,5,6).
(7)
The endpoints 1 and 6 are initially unmatched vertices in a matching. Along P the edges are outside, inside, outside, inside, outside M. Remove (2,3) and (4,5), then add (1,2),(3,4),(5,6) together:
M′=M△E(P)={(1,2),(3,4),(5,6)}.
(8)
This raises the cardinality from two to three and gives a perfect matching. Six vertices allow at most three disjoint pairs, so it is also a maximum cardinality matching. The initial M is already a maximal matching: every unused edge touches a matched vertex. Augmentation succeeds because it can replace earlier choices instead of only adding to them.
One unused edge joining two unmatched vertices in a matching is the shortest possible augmenting path in a matching. Longer augmenting paths in a matching reassign several partners, explaining why a stalled greedy matching need not be optimal. For edge benefits we​, the change in total benefit is
∑e∈E(P)∖M​we​−∑e∈E(P)∩M​we​,
(9)
which need not be positive even though the cardinality increases. A maximum-weight matching therefore needs a different optimality test. In an assignment equality graph an augmenting path in a matching moves toward a perfect matching while preserving zero reduced costs on matched edges; this is the setting used by the Hungarian algorithm.

Berge's theorem

Words: 211
Augmenting path in a matching OurBigBook.com
For a finite simple graph G and a matching in a graph M⊆E(G), M is a maximum cardinality matching if and only if G has no augmenting path in a matching relative to M. The graph need not be a bipartite graph, and the assertion concerns cardinality, not edge benefits.[ref]
The criterion turns a global comparison with every competing matching in a graph into a search for one repair. Starting from any matching in a graph, repeatedly find and toggle an augmenting path in a matching. Each step adds one selected edge, so finiteness gives termination after at most ⌊∣V(G)∣/2⌋−∣M∣ steps; absence of any further augmenting path in a matching certifies maximum cardinality. This is an optimality criterion, not by itself a bound on the work needed to find each graph path.
For a short proof outline, an augmenting path in a matching immediately contradicts maximum cardinality. Conversely, if another matching in a graph N has ∣N∣>∣M∣, consider the subgraph whose set of edges is M△N. Its vertices have degree of a vertex at most two; its nontrivial connected components are graph paths and even cycles in a graph, alternating between the two matchings in a graph. A cycle in a graph contributes equal numbers from each. Hence some graph path contributes one more edge from N than from M and starts and ends with edges from N. Its endpoints are unmatched vertices in a matching relative to M, yielding the required augmenting path in a matching.

The two sides have n vertices each. Given a nonperfect matching in a graph, choose unmatched vertices u,v on opposite sides. An unmatched neighbour of either supplies a one-edge augmenting path in a matching. Otherwise their neighbours are matched; the matched left vertices corresponding to N(u) and the set N(v) have total size greater than n, so they intersect. This supplies the alternating path in a graph u,b,a,v with ab in the matching in a graph. Repeated augmentation produces a perfect matching.
Choose such an augmentation deterministically. Its reverse is specified by at most four vertices, giving at most 2n4 preimages for a matching in a graph of the next size. Thus the matching generating function coefficients satisfy mk​≤2n4mk+1​.

Perfect matching

Words: 369 Articles: 3
Matching (graph theory) OurBigBook.com
A perfect matching is a matching in a graph that covers every vertex exactly once. Its edges have no shared endpoints, and every vertex is an endpoint of one selected edge. It is useful when a collection must be divided into disjoint pairs with no element left over.
In a bipartite graph with equally many vertices on its two sides, a perfect matching can assign every person one allowed job and every job one person. The example under permanent of a matrix has three people and three jobs, with exactly three valid assignments. The blue edges below show those three perfect matchings; a crossing in the drawing is not an extra vertex.
Every selected edge covers two vertices, so a graph with an odd integer number of vertices has no perfect matching. An even integer number is necessary but does not guarantee existence: an isolated vertex cannot be covered. A perfect matching also need not be unique. For example, the complete bipartite graph K2,2​ has two perfect matchings, corresponding to the two ways of assigning two people to two jobs.
Counting all perfect matchings is a different question from finding one. For a zero-one biadjacency matrix of a balanced bipartite graph, the count is its permanent of a matrix. The nonzero determinant criterion for a perfect matching supplies an existence test, but cancellation means that a zero determinant does not rule one out.

Flexible vertex in a balanced bipartite graph

Words: 91
Perfect matching OurBigBook.com
A vertex on one side of a balanced bipartite graph is flexible if every incident edge lies in a perfect matching. The strict Hall marriage theorem inequalities for nonempty proper subsets imply flexibility of every vertex: after forcing an edge, removal of its ends loses at most one neighbour from each remaining set. If a perfect matching exists without the strict condition, choose a minimal nonempty tight set. Its induced balanced graph has the strict condition internally, and each internal matching extends using the original matching outside that set. Thus at least one flexible vertex always exists.

A minimum-weight perfect matching pairs every vertex exactly once while minimizing total edge weight. General weighted perfect matching has polynomial-time algorithms. Maximal matching

Words: 287 Articles: 1
Matching (graph theory) OurBigBook.com
A maximal matching is a matching in a graph to which no extra edge can be added while retaining all current selected edges. Equivalently, no edge joins two unmatched vertices in a matching; those vertices form an independent set. Every maximum cardinality matching is a maximal matching, but the converse fails.
Take the path graph P4​ with vertices 1,2,3,4 and edges (1,2),(2,3),(3,4). A greedy algorithm that first selects the middle edge produces M={(2,3)}. Both remaining edges touch a matched vertex, so this size-one matching in a graph is maximal. Yet N={(1,2),(3,4)} has two edges. No matching in a graph on four vertices has more than two edges, so N is a maximum cardinality matching. The two remaining free vertices of M, namely 1 and 4, are not adjacent; that does not prevent a longer augmenting path in a matching from joining them.
Maximality is useful as a quick stopping rule and a modest guarantee. For any maximal matching M and maximum cardinality matching N in a finite simple graph, every edge of N touches an endpoint of an edge of M. Assign it to one such edge of M. Since N has disjoint endpoints, each edge of M receives at most two assignments, proving ∣N∣≤2∣M∣. The P₄ example reaches equality. Thus maximality guarantees at least half the largest cardinality, but says nothing comparable about arbitrary edge benefits. Its selected endpoints also form a vertex cover, as explained under vertex cover.

Greedy matching

Words: 78
Maximal matching OurBigBook.com
A greedy matching starts with the empty matching in a graph, scans all edges in some order, and selects an edge exactly when both endpoints are unmatched vertices in a matching. For a finite graph, a complete scan returns a maximal matching: a rejected edge had a matched endpoint, which stays matched thereafter. Stopping before all edges have been considered need not give maximality. Changing the order can change the result; on P₄, middle-first gives one pair, whereas outer-first gives two. A greedy algorithm of this form never revisits its choices, while an augmenting path in a matching does.

Maximum cardinality matching

Words: 49
Matching (graph theory) OurBigBook.com
A maximum cardinality matching is a matching in a graph with as many edges as any matching in a graph of the same graph. It has cardinality ν(G), the matching number, and need not be unique or be a perfect matching. For example, any single edge of a triangle in a graph is a maximum cardinality matching, but leaves one vertex unmatched. Berge's theorem characterizes these optima by the absence of an augmenting path in a matching.

Maximum-weight matching

Words: 118
Matching (graph theory) OurBigBook.com
Assign each edge e a real benefit we​. A maximum-weight matching maximizes ∑e∈M​we​ over all matchings in a graph M; the empty matching in a graph is allowed. Setting every we​=1 recovers a maximum cardinality matching, but otherwise the objectives can disagree.
On the same path graph P4​, give the middle edge benefit 3 and each outer edge benefit 1. The matching in a graph {(2,3)} has total benefit 3, while the two-edge matching in a graph {(1,2),(3,4)} has total benefit 2. Thus the smaller choice is the unique maximum-weight matching. If instead the requirement is to find a maximum cardinality matching and then maximize benefit among those, the outer edges must be chosen. Requiring a perfect matching before optimizing cost is another distinct problem, covered by minimum-weight perfect matching. With negative benefits a maximum-weight matching can even be empty, so it need not be a maximal matching.

Hall's marriage theorem

Words: 1k Articles: 11
Matching (graph theory) OurBigBook.com
For a finite bipartite graph G=(L∪R,E) with disjoint left and right vertex sets, Hall's marriage theorem characterizes when distinct allowed partners can be chosen for every left vertex. A matching in a graph with matching saturation of L exists if and only if every subset S⊆L satisfies
∣NG​(S)∣≥∣S∣,NG​(S)={r∈R:some s∈S has sr∈E}.
(11)
The graph neighbourhood counts distinct right vertices, rather than edges or repeated choices. This is the Hall condition. Its failure supplies a concrete Hall obstruction. Necessity follows because the chosen partners of S are distinct members of NG​(S); sufficiency is the surprising assertion that these collective counts rule out every obstacle.[ref]
The right side may be larger: matching saturation of L leaves ∣R∣−∣L∣ right vertices unmatched. Only when ∣L∣=∣R∣ does a matching in a graph saturating L automatically become a perfect matching. For a specified subset X⊆L, apply the same criterion only to S⊆X, using the induced subgraph on X∪R. The remaining left vertices need not be matched. The empty set gives 0≥0; if X is empty, the empty matching in a graph suffices.
Consider a scheduling model with three unit-duration jobs a,b,c and four available slots 1,2,3,4 on one machine. Each job needs exactly one slot, each slot accepts at most one job, and the allowed slots are
N(a)={1,2},N(b)={1},N(c)={2,3,4}.
(12)

A greedy choice a1 initially blocks b, but the augmenting path in a matching b,1,a,2 replaces a1 by b1,a2; subsequent assignment of c3 succeeds. A bad partial choice is not an impossibility certificate.

Hall condition (∣N(S)∣≥∣S∣(S⊆L))

Hall's marriage theorem OurBigBook.com
The Hall condition requires enough distinct neighbours for every subset of the specified left vertex set in a finite bipartite graph. Checking the whole left set establishes only one inequality. Checking singleton sets establishes only that each left vertex has positive degree of a vertex. Overlapping lists can pass both tests and still fail to admit matching saturation of the left side.

Hall obstruction (∣N(S)∣<∣S∣)

Words: 209
Hall condition OurBigBook.com
A Hall obstruction is a left subset S with fewer distinct neighbours than vertices. At most ∣N(S)∣ members of S can be matched, so at least ∣S∣−∣N(S)∣ must remain unmatched. The pigeonhole principle proves failure without enumerating every proposed assignment.
For a stronger counterexample than an isolated vertex, take L={a,b,c,d} and R={1,2,3,4} with
N(a)=N(b)=N(c)={1,2},N(d)={3,4}.
(13)
Every vertex has positive degree of a vertex, the sides have equal cardinality, and ∣N(L)∣=∣L∣=4. Every two-vertex left subset also passes the Hall condition. Nevertheless S={a,b,c} has only N(S)={1,2}, so 2<3 rules out matching saturation of L.
All sixteen left subsets can be checked by writing k=∣S∩{a,b,c}∣ and ε=1 if d∈S, otherwise 0. Then
∣S∣=k+ε,∣N(S)∣=21{k>0}​+2ε.
(14)
Only k=3,ε=0 violates the Hall condition. Every matching in a graph uses at most two edges incident to {a,b,c} and at most one incident to d, so the matching number is three. There are exactly twelve size-three matchings in a graph: choose two of a,b,c in three ways, assign them to 1,2 in two ways, and choose 3 or 4 for d. Thus the obstruction identifies the bottleneck: adding availability only for d cannot help; allowing c to use slot 3 permits {a1,b2,c3,d4}, a perfect matching.

Hall allocation model

Words: 202
Hall's marriage theorem OurBigBook.com
In a unit-capacity assignment problem, left vertices represent demands and right vertices represent individually usable resources; an edge records compatibility. All constraints must be encoded by those allowed pairs and by using each resource at most once. Hall's marriage theorem decides feasibility. Costs belong to an additional mathematical optimization problem; precedence between jobs or requirements for consecutive slots need additional modelling.
The authored Notes on the design of a conference scheduler by David R. MacIver (Implementation, 7 August 2022) illustrates a Hall obstruction with four talks confined to three slots. With one talk per slot, that subset has size four and a graph neighbourhood of size three. An unused slot outside its graph neighbourhood does not remove the bottleneck. In the three-job example above, the same scheduling model instead has two complete assignments.
The allocation exercise in Graph Theory by Reinhard Diestel (Matching, Covering and Packing, printed p. 20, Exercise 9) asks for disjoint subsets Di​⊆Ai​ of prescribed nonnegative integer sizes di​. Replace demand i by di​ left vertices with allowed resource set Ai​. Hall's marriage theorem gives exactly the condition
​⋃i∈I​Ai​​≥∑i∈I​di​for every index subset I.
(15)
For a numerical instance, let A1​={1,2}, A2​={2,3}, and d1​=2,d2​=1. The separate demands and their combined demand fit, and D1​={1,2},D2​={3} works. Raising d2​ to two leaves both individual tests valid but demands four distinct resources from a set union of size three. This is the same collective obstruction with repeated demand vertices.

System of distinct representatives (xi​∈Ai​,xi​=xj​ (i=j))

A system of distinct representatives for a finite indexed family of finite sets (Ai​)i∈I​ chooses one element xi​∈Ai​ per index, with all choices distinct. Form a bipartite graph with left vertex set I, right vertex set ⋃i∈I​Ai​, and edges ix whenever x∈Ai​. Matching saturation of I is precisely a system of distinct representatives. Hall theorem proof outline

Words: 115
Hall's marriage theorem OurBigBook.com
For sufficiency, take a matching in a graph of maximum size and suppose a left vertex u is unmatched. Follow unused edges from left to right and matching edges back to the left. An unmatched reachable right vertex yields an augmenting path in a matching, contradicting maximal size. Otherwise let S,T be the reachable left and right sets. Every member of T is matched to a distinct member of S∖{u}, and every member of S∖{u} is reached this way. Since N(S)=T, we obtain ∣N(S)∣=∣S∣−1, contradicting the Hall condition. Finiteness ensures that a maximum-size matching in a graph exists.
The alternative Hall induction through a tight set uses mathematical induction: a proper nonempty subset with ∣N(S)∣=∣S∣ splits off a balanced induced subgraph; without such a tight set, deleting one chosen edge's endpoints preserves the Hall condition on the smaller left side.

Random bipartite matching from empty-rectangle exclusion (P(no perfect matching)≤3n2⌈n​⌉(1−p)n/2⟶0)

In the equal-part random bipartite graph with fixed edge probability 0<p<1, rule out empty rectangles of side ⌈n​⌉ and vertices with at least n−⌈n​⌉ nonneighbors. A union bound gives the displayed asymptotic estimate. Small vertex sets satisfy Hall's condition by the degree bound; intermediate sets do so because their omitted-neighbor rectangle would contain a forbidden square; large sets meet every vertex in the other part. Thus Hall's marriage theorem gives a perfect matching asymptotically almost surely. This does not mean probability one at any finite size.

Hall matching of maximal separated sets

Two maximum-cardinality ε-separated subsets A,B of a compact metric space have a bijection that moves each point by less than ε. Join a to b when their distance is below ε. If S⊆A had fewer than ∣S∣ neighbours, replacing these neighbours in B by S would create a larger separated set. The Hall marriage theorem therefore supplies a perfect matching. This allows empirical averages on a compact isometry group to be made approximately invariant without assuming a prior Haar measure construction.

Measurable Hall theorem

Words: 110
Hall's marriage theorem OurBigBook.com
For finitely many Lebesgue measurable sets Ai​⊆[0,1] and nonnegative demands bi​, pairwise disjoint measurable subsets Bi​⊆Ai​ with λ(Bi​)=bi​ exist exactly when λ(⋃i∈I​Ai​)≥∑i∈I​bi​ for every index subset I. Necessity is additivity and monotonicity of Lebesgue measure. For sufficiency, partition the set union into membership cells EJ​, send flow from a source through demand vertices i to cells with i∈J, then to a sink. Source capacities are bi​, cell capacities are λ(EJ​), and intermediate capacities are D=∑i​bi​. Every cut of a flow network has capacity at least D by the assumed inequalities. The max-flow min-cut theorem provides the allocations, and divisibility of Lebesgue measure turns each cell allocation into disjoint pieces. If D=0, all Bi​ can simply be empty.
