Poisson arrivals see time averages

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Queueing theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 386
Poisson arrivals see time averages says that an exogenous, homogeneous Poisson process samples prearrival states with the same long-run frequencies as observation over time, provided the observed system cannot anticipate future arrivals and the relevant time averages exist. The system may depend on all past arrivals: an ordinary queue does. The required independence is between future increments of the Poisson process and the history available to the system, including its service information. Assume measurable state observations with ordinary piecewise continuous functions as sample paths; write X(t−) for the state immediately before time t.
For a state set B, let A(T) count offered arrivals by time T and let tk​ be their times. With a positive finite constant arrival rate, the PASTA equality is
limT→∞​A(T)1​∑k:tk​≤T​1{X(tk​−)∈B}​=limT→∞​T1​∫0T​1{X(t)∈B}​dt,

almost surely when the time limit exists. The indicator functions count visits on the left and time spent on the right. For a stationary process with ergodicity, the right side is the stationary probability of B. It matters that the arriving customer has not yet changed the observed state.
In the M/M/1 utilization and delay example, server utilization is 0.8. Thus PASTA says that 80 percent of offered arrivals find the server busy and 20 percent find the whole queue empty. If an offered arrival seeing population n is admitted with conditional probability p(n), using fresh randomness independent of that state, the accepted fraction is ∑n​πn​p(n), where π is the stationary distribution. Multiplying this fraction by the offered rate gives the admitted queue throughput when every admitted customer eventually completes service. Accepted arrivals are selected by state and need not themselves form a Poisson process or see the unconditioned time frequencies.
Arbitrary arrivals need not see time averages. Suppose a customer arrives every two minutes and its service time is exactly one minute, with one server and no other customers. The server is busy half the time, but every arrival finds it idle. A uniformly random initial phase can even make the occupancy a stationary process, without repairing the disagreement. Nevertheless Little's law still gives L=(1/2)⋅1=1/2, with rate measured in customers per minute. This separates Little's law from PASTA.
Ronald W. Wolff's 1982 paper gave a general PASTA theorem under nonanticipation, rather than requiring the observed system to be independent of its driving Poisson process. Its Theorem 1 compares the two long-run fractions; stationarity is a way to establish convergence, not the central sampling condition. [ref]. [ref].
