Poisson process (Nt​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 28
A Homogeneous Poisson process models arrivals at a constant rate λ>0. Its counting process Nt​=N(t) counts arrivals in (0,t], with N0​=0. Its paths are right-continuous nondecreasing functions, take nonnegative integer values, and have unit jumps almost surely. The defining Poisson distribution of each increment is
P(Ns+t​−Ns​=k)=e−λtk!(λt)k​,s≥0,t>0,k=0,1,2,….
(1)
It has stationary increments: the probability distribution above depends on the duration t, not the starting time s. It also has independent increments: counts in any finite collection of disjoint time intervals are independent random variables. Together these conditions specify the joint count laws, not merely the Poisson distribution at a single time.[ref]
The rate has units of arrivals per unit time; λt is dimensionless. The Poisson distribution gives
E[Ns+t​−Ns​]=Var(Ns+t​−Ns​)=λt.
(2)
For a toy rate λ=2 per minute and a duration of 1.5 minutes, the expected value and variance of the count are both 3. The probability of no arrival is e−3≈0.049787, and the probability of exactly three is 33e−3/3!=29​e−3≈0.224042. In two consecutive one-minute intervals, the probability of exactly two arrivals in the first and exactly one in the second is (2e−2)(2e−2)=4e−4≈0.073263, using independent increments. These are exact model calculations with rounded decimals, not measured frequencies.
The diagram's nine hand-chosen arrivals illustrate a path; the line λt is an expected value, not a prescribed trajectory. Constant rate does not imply evenly spaced arrivals. Poisson interarrival times connect the count description to waiting, while Poisson thinning explains how independently routing arrivals produces separate streams. This makes the Poisson process useful as a simple arrival model for a queue, provided constant rate and independent increments are plausible. For a deterministic changing rate, use an Inhomogeneous Poisson process; bunching or history-dependent arrivals need additional assumptions. Conditional on Nt​=n, the arrival times are the order statistics of n independent uniform distributions on [0,t], as developed in Poisson process conditional arrival times.

Two-sided interarrival test for overlap (P(overlap)=1−e−2λd)

Words: 119
Poisson process OurBigBook.com
Consider a homogeneous Poisson process of arrival rate λ, with each arrival staying for a fixed duration d>0. For any fixed arrival index k≥2, its visit overlaps another visit if the preceding interarrival time is below d or the following interarrival time is below d. If the nearest preceding arrival has already left then all earlier ones have left; if the next arrival is too late then all later ones are too late. These two gaps are independent exponential random variables, so the probability of no overlap is e−λde−λd. Taking its complement gives the formula. This is an unconditional statement for a fixed indexed arrival; conditioning on a fixed calendar closing time would change the problem.

Poisson count-arrival joint central limit theorem (ρ=−min{λ​,1/λ​})

Words: 294
Poisson process OurBigBook.com
For a rate-one Poisson process with Poisson arrival times Tn​, suppose tn​→∞ and tn​/n→λ∈[0,∞]. Then
(tn​​Ntn​​−tn​​,n​Tn​−n​)d​BN(ρ),ρ={−λ​,−1/λ​,​0≤λ≤1,1≤λ≤∞,​
(3)
where 1/∞​=0 and BN(ρ) denotes the bivariate normal distribution with standard normal marginal distributions, including the singular cases. In particular, equal scales give ρ=−1 and separated scales give ρ=0.
To prove this, fix real x,y and put sn​=n+yn​>0 for large n. The event (Tn​−n)/n​≤y is exactly Nsn​​≥n. Counts at the two deterministic times tn​,sn​ are sums of a shared count on [0,min(tn​,sn​)] and an independent count on the remaining interval. The central limit theorem for a centered Poisson random variable of diverging parameter follows by writing a count at a real time u as its count at ⌊u⌋, a sum of independent unit increments, plus an independent remainder with expected value at most one. Markov's inequality makes that remainder negligible after division by u​.
Apply this central limit theorem to the two independent count blocks whenever their lengths diverge. A block with bounded length, or length negligible compared with the scale of its coordinate, contributes a term tending to zero in probability. The standardized pair of counts therefore has a joint normal limit whose correlation coefficient is
limn→∞​tn​sn​​min(tn​,sn​)​={λ​,1/λ​,​0≤λ≤1,1≤λ≤∞.​
(4)
This includes λ=1: the difference block then has negligible variance after normalization. In the arrival event the standardized count at sn​ must be at least (n−sn​)/sn​​→−y. Reversing this inequality changes the sign of the second limiting normal random variable, giving the asserted negative correlation coefficient. Continuity of the marginal distributions permits the moving thresholds and the weak inequalities, including at the singular limits.
Every ρ∈[−1,0) is realized by tn​=λn with λ=ρ−2≥1, and zero by tn​=n4. Conversely, even when tn​/n has no limit, it has a subsequence converging in the extended interval [0,∞]. If the original pair has a joint bivariate normal distribution limit, that subsequence has the same limit, forcing its correlation coefficient into [−1,0].

Negative quadrant dependence of a Poisson count and an arrival time (P(Ns​≤k,Tn​≤t)≤P(Ns​≤k)P(Tn​≤t))

Words: 172
Poisson process OurBigBook.com
For a Poisson process, the count and Poisson arrival time have the displayed inequality at every deterministic s,t≥0. A large count and a late arrival reflect opposite directions of the same fluctuation.
Here is a proof from independent increments. If s≤t, write Nt​=Ns​+V with V independent of Ns​. Conditional on Ns​=u, the event Tn​≤t has probability g(u)=P(V≥n−u), a nondecreasing function of u. The event Ns​≤k has a nonincreasing indicator function f(u). For any random variable W and an independent copy W′, oppositely monotone bounded functions satisfy
2Cov(f(W),g(W))=E[(f(W)−f(W′))(g(W)−g(W′))]≤0.
(5)
Taking W=Ns​ proves the claim. If s>t, condition instead on Nt​=u; then P(Ns​≤k∣Nt​=u) is nonincreasing in u and 1{u≥n}​ is nondecreasing, giving the same inequality.
The inequality is preserved under joint convergence in distribution when the marginal distributions of the limit are continuous. Consequently, a limiting bivariate normal distribution with standard normal marginal distributions cannot have positive correlation coefficient. For 0<ρ<1, conditioning on Z1​=z<0 makes P(Z2​≤0∣Z1​=z)>1/2, so P(Z1​≤0,Z2​≤0)>1/4, contradicting the inequality. For ρ=1, that quadrant has probability 1/2, which also contradicts it.

Poisson interarrival times (Wn​=Tn​−Tn−1​∼Exp(λ))

Words: 202
Poisson process OurBigBook.com
Let Tn​=inf{t≥0:Nt​≥n} be the nth arrival time, and set T0​=0. In a Homogeneous Poisson process of rate λ>0, the gaps Wn​=Tn​−Tn−1​ are independent and identically distributed random variables with an exponential distribution:
P(Wn​>w)=e−λw,fWn​​(w)=λe−λw,w≥0;E[Wn​]=λ1​,Var(Wn​)=λ21​.
(6)
Here Exp(λ) uses the rate convention. Under the scale convention θ=1/λ, the same probability density function is θ−1e−w/θ; a simulation function accepting a scale must receive 1/λ. Conversely, summing independent rate-λ exponential random variables to form Tn​ and counting those sums constructs a Homogeneous Poisson process.[ref]
The first-gap formula follows immediately from {W1​>w}={Nw​=0} and the Poisson distribution. Independence of all successive gaps is the additional interval-theorem assertion; deterministic-time independent increments alone should not simply be substituted at random arrival times without justification. The memorylessness of the exponential distribution gives
P(Wn​>a+w∣Wn​>a)=e−λw,a,w≥0.
(7)
At the toy rate of two per minute, the mean gap is half a minute, the median is (log2)/2≈0.346574 minutes, and the probability of waiting longer than half a minute is e−1≈0.367879. Here log is the natural logarithm. Already having waited a minute without an arrival leaves this same conditional probability of waiting another half minute. The exponential distribution describes a model with no aging of the remaining wait; a scheduled arrival with a fixed gap has no such property.

Poisson observation scheme and rate likelihood (λ=NH​/Horn/Tn​)

Words: 92
Poisson process OurBigBook.com
A Poisson process observed for a fixed duration H has likelihood proportional to λNH​e−λH. Complete independent exponential distribution gaps observed until a fixed event count n have likelihood λne−λTn​. Both give count divided by exposure, but the exposure is random in the second design. Under fixed exposure the total count is sufficient: conditional on it, the event locations are independent of the rate, so exact timestamps add no rate information to interval counts. Gap estimates and fixed-horizon estimates should not be compared without distinguishing their stopping rules and any final censored gap.

Strong law for Poisson arrival times (Yn​/n→1/λ)

Words: 47
Poisson process OurBigBook.com
For a homogeneous Poisson process of rate λ>0, N(t)/t→λ almost surely: unit-time increments are independent Poisson variables and the strong law of large numbers applies, then monotonicity fills the intervals between integers. At its nth point Yn​, simplicity gives N(Yn​)=n. Consequently Yn​/n→1/λ. Rate and mean interarrival time are reciprocals.

Every second Poisson arrival has underdispersed counts (M(t)=⌊N(t)/2⌋)

Words: 72
Poisson process OurBigBook.com
For a rate-λ Poisson process, keeping every second arrival gives a renewal process with independent interarrival times equal to sums of two exponential waiting times. Put x=λt. Its count mean and variance are (2x+e−2x−1)/4 and (4x−8xe−2x−e−4x+1)/16. The variance is strictly smaller than the mean for x>0: sixteen times their difference has derivative 4[−1+4xe−2x+e−4x]<0 and is zero at zero, because sinh(2x)>2x. Therefore this process is not a Cox process.

Random directing measure of a Cox process (N∣Λ∼PRM(Λ))

Words: 66
Cox process OurBigBook.com
A Cox process can be directed by a random locally finite measure, which need not have a density. Conditional counts in disjoint sets are independent Poisson variables with that measure's masses. Uniform circular offspring displacements give a directing measure supported on random circles; its unconditional mean can still be a multiple of planar Lebesgue measure. A random intensity function is the special case in which the directing measure is absolutely continuous.

Poisson thinning

Words: 895 Articles: 6
Poisson process OurBigBook.com
Poisson thinning splits a Poisson process by independently marking each arrival as retained or rejected. Let the original Homogeneous Poisson process have rate λ>0, and let p(s)∈[0,1] be a deterministic measurable function of time. Attach independent uniform distributions U1​,U2​,… on (0,1), independent of the entire original Poisson process. At arrival time Ti​, retain the arrival if Ui​≤p(Ti​). Given all arrival times, these decisions are independent and have the specified retention probabilities. Define the two counting processes using an indicator function:
Rt​=∑i:Ti​≤t​1{Ui​≤p(Ti​)}​,Dt​=Nt​−Rt​.
(10)
The Poisson thinning theorem gives independent retained and rejected Inhomogeneous Poisson processes. Their rates are λp(s) and λ(1−p(s)), respectively, in the sense of the intensity function of a point process. In particular, for 0≤a<b,
Rb​−Ra​∼Poisson(λ∫ab​p(s)ds),Db​−Da​∼Poisson(λ∫ab​(1−p(s))ds).
(11)
The independence is of the two whole processes: every finite collection of retained counts is independent of every finite collection of rejected counts. It is not merely zero covariance for one interval. For constant p, the streams are Homogeneous Poisson processes of rates λp and λ(1−p); the endpoints p=0,1 include the identically zero stream.[ref]
For an exact toy calculation, take λ=2 per minute and retain with p(s)=1/4 on (0,2] and p(s)=3/4 on (2,4]. In the first two minutes, the retained and rejected expected values are 2⋅2⋅1/4=1 and 2⋅2⋅3/4=3. In the next two minutes they are 3 and 1. Over all four minutes, R4​,D4​ are independent Poisson distributions with mean 4 each, and their sum N4​ has mean 8. Thus
P(R4​=0,D4​=0)=e−4e−4=e−8,P(R4​=2,D4​=3)=e−82!42​3!43​=3256​e−8.
(12)
These probabilities and expected values describe the model, not empirical measurements. In the diagram, the original nine synthetic arrivals split into five retained and four rejected arrivals; those realized counts need not equal their expected values.
The mechanism explains why mutually exclusive labels can yield independent counts: randomness of the original total offsets the dependence from allocating a fixed total. For constant p and an interval with original mean m, the conditional retained count has a binomial distribution given N=n. The joint probability generating function is
E[zRwD]=E[(pz+(1−p)w)N]=exp{m(pz+(1−p)w−1)}=emp(z−1)em(1−p)(w−1),0≤z,w≤1.
(13)
This factorization proves the two independent Poisson distributions; disjoint original intervals give independent pairs. For varying p(s), the Independent marking theorem for Poisson point processes gives the same conclusion with the two integrated intensities. The joint red and green Poisson count formula gives the corresponding direct calculation with probability mass functions.
Independence here is unconditional. After conditioning on N=n, the identity D=n−R gives covariance Cov(R,D∣N=n)=−np(1−p) for constant p. Thus the retained and rejected counts are dependent for n>0 and 0<p<1, even though their unconditional covariance is zero. Poisson thinning can model independent routing of arrivals, or independent detection of arrivals with a known efficiency schedule; using only a retention fraction without checking the marking mechanism can give a wrong count law.

Dependence in Poisson thinning

Words: 252
Poisson thinning OurBigBook.com
Independent marking is the hypothesis that guarantees the Poisson thinning theorem. Equal marginal retention probabilities do not guarantee it. Consider one Bernoulli random variable B with success probability p∈(0,1), independent of a Poisson process, and retain every arrival when B=1 and none when B=0. Each arrival is retained with probability p, but the marks share the same coin. In an interval whose original count has Poisson distribution of mean m>0, the retained count is R=BN and the rejected count is D=(1−B)N, so
E[R]=pm,Var(R)=pm+p(1−p)m2,Cov(R,D)=−p(1−p)m2.
(14)
The extra variance shows that R is not Poisson distributed; the negative covariance proves dependence. Its no-arrival probability is 1−p+pe−m, rather than e−pm. For the toy values p=1/2,m=2, both rules have retained mean 1, but the common-coin rule has variance 2 and no-arrival probability (1+e−2)/2≈0.567668, compared with variance 1 and no-arrival probability e−1≈0.367879 for independent marking. These formulas follow by conditioning on B and using the law of total variance.
An arrival-dependent or adversarial retention rule can change the law just as strongly. Keeping every second arrival gives Rt​=⌊Nt​/2⌋. Its successive retained gaps are sums of two independent exponential random variables, hence have the Erlang distribution of shape two and rate λ, not an exponential distribution. The resulting renewal process and its count variance are analyzed in Every second Poisson arrival has underdispersed counts, and the two dependent streams in alternating split of a Poisson process. As an even more restrictive rule, keeping only the first arrival ever gives Rt​=1{Nt​≥1}​, a Bernoulli distribution at each t>0, so it cannot have a positive-mean Poisson distribution. A thinning rule must specify how decisions depend on arrivals and on one another; the independent-marking conclusion cannot be applied automatically to arbitrary deletion.

Poisson characterization by independent binomial splitting

Poisson thinning OurBigBook.com
Let N be a nonnegative integer-valued random variable with finite expected value μ, and independently label each of its items with either of two equally likely labels. If the two resulting counts are independent random variables, then N has a Poisson distribution. Indeed, its probability generating function obeys G(s)=G((1+s)/2)2. With H(t)=G(1−t), iteration gives H(t)=H(t/2k)2k. Finite expected value gives H(h)=1−μh+o(h), hence H(t)=e−μt and G(s)=eμ(s−1). Conversely, Poisson thinning produces independent Poisson distributions for the two counts.
