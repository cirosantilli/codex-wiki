Lagrange's four-square theorem (n=a2+b2+c2+d2)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 661
Every nonnegative integer is a sum of squares with four terms, with zero terms allowed. Precisely, for every integer n≥0, there exist nonnegative integers a,b,c,d such that
n=a2+b2+c2+d2.
(32)
Allowing arbitrary integer roots gives the same statement: changing a root to its absolute value leaves the resulting square number unchanged. Four is a uniform upper bound on the number of nonzero terms, rather than a demand for four positive terms. Repeated square numbers are allowed. Conrad states the positive case and explicitly explains the role of zero terms; n=0 follows by taking all four roots zero. [ref]
The small cases make the convention visible:
01237​=02+02+02+02,=12+02+02+02,=12+12+02+02,=12+12+12+02,=22+12+12+12.​
(33)
In particular, 1 could not be a sum of squares with four strictly positive roots, since each term would be at least one. Geometrically, a square of integer side length a has area a2: the theorem says that any nonnegative whole-number total area can be split into at most four such square areas. It does not assert that those squares tile one larger square or a prescribed rectangle.
Why does the fourth term matter? Two square numbers do not suffice for 3: in modular arithmetic, a square number has remainder zero or one modulo four, so their sum cannot be three modulo four. The sum of two squares theorem gives a complete criterion for the positive integers representable with two terms. Even three terms leave gaps. A square number has remainder 0,1, or 4 modulo eight; adding three of these, with repetition allowed, never gives seven modulo eight. Thus 7,15,23,31,… cannot be sums of three square numbers, although Lagrange's four-square theorem represents all of them with four. This also proves that the bound four cannot be reduced to three.
The modulo-eight obstruction is not the only way that three terms can fail. For example, 28 needs four: if three square numbers added to 28, their remainders modulo four would force all three roots even. Dividing by four would then express 7 as a sum of three square numbers, contradicting the preceding calculation. These short checks explain the examples without requiring a full classification of sums of three square numbers.
The practical benefit of Lagrange's four-square theorem is an existence guarantee for a family of Diophantine equations: for every nonnegative integer n, the displayed equation has an integer solution. It does not specify a unique solution. For instance, 65=82+12+02+02=72+42+02+02=62+42+32+22. To find a representation by a finite search, enumerate 0≤d≤c≤b≤a≤⌊n​⌋ and test the sum. The floor function supplies a finite bound because no single term can exceed n, and sorting roots removes duplicate orderings without discarding any possible sum. The theorem guarantees that the search succeeds.
A greedy algorithm that repeatedly subtracts the largest available square number need not find four terms: for 23 it chooses 16+4+1+1+1, while 23=9+9+4+1 uses four. The appeal of the theorem is this gap between local choices and a global promise: individual square numbers become farther apart, and two or three terms leave arithmetic obstructions, yet four terms cover every nonnegative integer.
For historical orientation, Conrad dates Lagrange's four-square theorem to 1770 and identifies the memoir as Démonstration d'un théorème d'arithmétique: it appeared in the Berlin Academy's volume for the year 1770, published in 1772, pages 123–133. Keeping the volume year and publication year distinct avoids an apparent conflict between dates. [ref]
