Incompressible flow (∇⋅u=0)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 458 Articles: 4
An incompressible flow preserves the volume of each moving fluid element. Its local condition is zero velocity divergence, ∇⋅u=0. Combined with mass conservation, it says that each parcel's mass density stays unchanged as it moves. This does not require every parcel to have the same mass density: an initially nonuniform distribution can be transported without compression. Nor does incompressibility require a constant velocity or constant pressure.
For positive mass density, the continuity equation makes the equivalence precise:
∂t∂ρ​+∇⋅(ρu)=0,DtDρ​+ρ∇⋅u=0,DtD​=∂t∂​+u⋅∇.
(138)
The material derivative follows a fluid element. Thus Dρ/Dt=0 if and only if the velocity field is a divergence-free vector field. The distinction between following a parcel and observing a fixed position prevents confusing incompressibility with an unchanging density field. [ref]
For a rigid pipe with impermeable walls, no leaks and steady flow, mass conservation at constant mass density implies a constant volumetric flow rate:
Q=∫A​u⋅ndA=AU,A1​U1​=A2​U2​.
(139)
Here n points downstream, A is the section's area and U is the mean normal velocity, not necessarily the centreline speed. No assumption of inviscid flow or a uniform velocity profile is needed. If unequal amounts entered and left a fixed segment, its mass would accumulate, contradicting steady flow.
For a worked example, take horizontal water flow with stipulated mass density 1000kgm−3, inlet radius R1​=0.010m, outlet radius R2​=0.005m and inlet mean velocity U1​=0.20ms−1. The circular areas give
Q=πR12​U1​=2π×10−5m3s−1,U2​=πR22​Q​=0.80ms−1.
(140)
The radius halves, the area falls to one quarter, and the mean velocity quadruples. The mass flow rate is ρQ=0.02πkgs−1. The same amount of water passes both sections each second; narrowing changes its speed rather than compressing it. The Reynolds number and Bernoulli's principle discussions use this same example to examine viscous effects and the additional assumptions needed to calculate pressure.
