Density matrix

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 6k Articles: 110
A finite-dimensional density operator ρ represents a quantum state on a Hilbert space. It is Hermitian, positive semidefinite, and has trace one:
ρ=ρ†,⟨v∣ρ∣v⟩≥0 for every ∣v⟩,Trρ=1.
(1)
The density matrix is its matrix in a chosen orthonormal basis. In dimension N it has N2−1 real parameters: a Hermitian matrix has N2, and fixing its trace removes one; positivity restricts the allowed values.
A quantum state ensemble of normalized pure states ∣ψi​⟩ with positive probabilities pi​ gives
ρ=∑i​pi​∣ψi​⟩⟨ψi​∣,pi​>0,∑i​pi​=1.
(2)
Zero-weight terms can be omitted. Conversely, the spectral theorem gives such an ensemble for every density operator, using its orthonormal eigenvectors and nonnegative eigenvalues, with zero eigenvalues omitted. A pure state has ρ=∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣; a mixed state needs at least two nonzero eigenvalues. [ref]
For a projective measurement with orthogonal projections Πa​, the Born rule is
P(a)=Tr(ρΠa​)=∑i​pi​⟨ψi​∣Πa​∣ψi​⟩.
(3)
This is the average of the component pure state probabilities. Positivity makes each outcome probability nonnegative, and ∑a​Πa​=I makes them sum to one. The same density operator therefore predicts every measurement on the system without requiring a unique preparation history. [ref]
For a closed system, Schrödinger picture evolution under a unitary operator is
ρS​(t)=U(t)ρH​U(t)†
(4)
when ρH​=ρS​(0) denotes the time-independent Heisenberg picture state and U(0)=I. In the Schrödinger picture the density operator evolves; in the Heisenberg picture the observables evolve instead, with identical measurement predictions. This unitary operator preserves the eigenvalues and hence the purity of a density operator. [ref]
A historical source for the probabilistic formulation of quantum mechanics is von Neumann's 1927 paper Wahrscheinlichkeitstheoretischer Aufbau der Quantenmechanik. [ref]

The reduced density matrix obtained by taking the partial trace of a larger entangled state. Leading high-temperature density kernel (ρ(x,x′)≃Z−1m/(2πβℏ2)​e−m(x−x′)2/(2βℏ2)−βV(x))

Density matrix OurBigBook.com
Neglecting commutators between kinetic and potential energy permits a leading product approximation to the imaginary-time evolution operator. The kinetic factor is a Gaussian Fourier integral; the potential factor multiplies it in position space. The one-body density matrix contains first-order spatial coherence and mode occupations of a many-particle state. Its trace is the particle number in the convention displayed. Quantum state population (pk​=⟨k∣ρ∣k⟩)

For a chosen normalized level state ∣k⟩, its population is the probability ⟨k∣ρ∣k⟩=Tr(∣k⟩⟨k∣ρ) of finding the system in that state. Quantum coherence in a specified basis (ρjk​=0, j=k)

Words: 111 Articles: 1
Density matrix OurBigBook.com
For a chosen orthonormal basis, the off-diagonal entries of a density operator encode quantum coherence between distinct basis states. A diagonal density operator is an incoherent mixture in that basis. This property is basis-dependent: diagonalizing the operator does not imply that it lacked coherence in the measurement or energy basis of interest. Relative phases affect these entries, while a global wavefunction phase cancels from the density operator. Dephasing damps the entries and can spoil stimulated Raman adiabatic passage by removing the ground-state interference that maintains the dark state.

Two-qubit Werner state (ρp​=pPs​+(1−p)(I−Ps​)/3)

Words: 349 Articles: 4
Density matrix OurBigBook.com
This collective-unitary-invariant two-qubit state has singlet weight p and equal weights on the three triplet Bell states. With Ps​ the spin singlet state projector, its partial transpose has eigenvalues (1−2p)/2 once and (1+2p)/6 three times. It is entangled exactly when p>1/2. At p=1/2, it is the equally weighted mixture of six antiparallel product states along the three Pauli axes. In the interval 1/4≤p≤1/2, convex mixing of this boundary state with I/4 explicitly proves separability. The weight p here is the singlet probability, not the alternative coefficient multiplying a pure singlet in a white-noise mixture.

Single-copy Werner filtering cannot increase concurrence (C(ρretained​)≤C(WF​))

Words: 69
Single-copy Werner filtering normalization bound OurBigBook.com
Combine the single-copy Werner filtering normalization bound with concurrence scaling under invertible local filters. Every complete classical record of local operations and classical communication has a product filter A⊗B; singular branches are separable. Thus every retained branch has concurrence at most that of the input. Coarse-graining successful records preserves the bound by convexity. Since concurrence of a two-qubit Werner state increases strictly with F, no successful single-copy protocol can output a Werner state with larger F. Several input copies permit collective distillation and are outside this statement.

Bell twirling followed by triplet symmetrization

Words: 89
Two-qubit Werner state OurBigBook.com
Average the four correlated local Pauli operations to remove coherences in the Bell states basis. A one-party Pauli operation can permute the largest of the four resulting weights into the singlet sector; that weight is at least 1/4. Correlated common local unitaries then permute the three triplet projectors, while collective-unitary covariance of the two-qubit singlet keeps the singlet projector unchanged. Averaging all six permutations makes the triplet weights equal, producing a two-qubit Werner state. Shared randomness or local operations and classical communication coordinates these local operations. Common unitaries alone cannot increase an initially small singlet weight, because that weight is invariant under them.

Extreme points of the density-operator state space (ρ extreme⟺rankρ=1)

Words: 114
Density matrix OurBigBook.com
The density operators on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space form a convex set. Its extreme points are exactly the rank-one projectors, hence exactly the pure states. To prove this, suppose ∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣=∑i​ai​ρi​ with ai​>0 and ρi​ positive semidefinite. For w⊥ψ, 0=∑i​ai​⟨w∣ρi​∣w⟩ is a sum of nonnegative numbers, so every term vanishes. Diagonalizing ρi​ shows that ⟨w∣ρi​∣w⟩=0 implies ρi​w=0. Thus each ρi​ is supported on the line spanned by ψ, and its unit trace forces ρi​=∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣.
Conversely, a density operator of rank at least two has a spectral decomposition with at least two positive eigenvalues. If λ is one of them, 0<λ<1 and ρ=λ∣e⟩⟨e∣+(1−λ)σ, where σ is the normalized remainder. These are distinct density operators, proving that ρ is not an extreme point.

Quantum state ensemble ({pi​,∣ψi​⟩},ρ=∑i​pi​∣ψi​⟩⟨ψi​∣)

Words: 241 Articles: 2
Density matrix OurBigBook.com
A quantum state ensemble specifies a random preparation: normalized pure states ∣ψi​⟩ are selected with probabilities pi​≥0, ∑i​pi​=1. Without an accessible preparation label, its measurement statistics are those of the density operator ρ=∑i​pi​∣ψi​⟩⟨ψi​∣. Indeed every POVM effect E has probability ∑i​pi​⟨ψi​∣E∣ψi​⟩=Tr(Eρ). Different ensembles can therefore have identical operational statistics: equal mixtures of computational-basis qubits or Hadamard-basis qubits both have density operator I/2. The ensemble decomposition contains information about the preparation, not additional information accessible from the system alone.

Convex roof extension (F(ρ)=infρ=∑j​pj​πj​​∑j​pj​μ(πj​))

Words: 161 Articles: 1
Quantum state ensemble OurBigBook.com
A convex roof extension extends a function μ on pure states to arbitrary density operators by minimizing its ensemble average over all pure state decompositions. It agrees with μ on pure states: every positive-weight state in a decomposition of a rank-one density operator has the same one-dimensional support. Concatenating nearly minimizing ensembles proves that F is a convex function. Moreover, every convex function that is no larger than μ on pure states is no larger than F, by applying convexity to each decomposition. Infima need not be attained; an arbitrarily small additive error suffices when F is finite.

Monotonicity of a convex roof under a quantum instrument (F(ρ)≥∑k​Pk​F(ρk​))

Words: 76
Convex roof extension OurBigBook.com
If a convex roof extension is non-increasing on average for every pure input to a quantum instrument, then it is non-increasing on average for mixed inputs too. For an input ensemble {pj​,πj​}, let qk∣j​ and σjk​ be its conditional outcome probabilities and states. Linearity of each unnormalized outcome map gives Pk​ρk​=∑j​pj​qk∣j​σjk​. Convexity gives ∑k​Pk​F(ρk​)≤∑j,k​pj​qk∣j​F(σjk​)≤∑j​pj​μ(πj​). Taking the infimum over input ensembles proves the result, even when conditional outputs of pure inputs are mixed, provided the averages are well defined.

Maximally mixed state (ρ∗​=IN​/N)

Words: 147 Articles: 1
Density matrix OurBigBook.com
The maximally mixed density operator on an N-dimensional Hilbert space assigns equal weights to every vector of any orthonormal basis. It is invariant under every unitary operator. Its purity is Tr(ρ∗2​)=1/N, the minimum possible because the nonnegative eigenvalues sum to one and obey the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. For N>1 it is a mixed state; for N=1 it is the sole pure state. A rank-k projector has probability k/N in this state.

Obstruction to uniform probability lowering by a unitary (Tr(PUIN​U†/N)=k/N)

Words: 83
Maximally mixed state OurBigBook.com
Let P be a rank-k projector with 0<k<N. No unitary operator can map every pure state whose P probability equals k/N to a state with the same fixed smaller probability. Average k/N​∣g⟩±1−k/N​∣b⟩ over good and bad basis vectors and both signs. The average density operator is the maximally mixed state, so every unitary preserves its average P probability. This contradicts a strictly smaller probability for all states in the ensemble. A preparation tailored to one known starting state is a different resource and is used in exact amplitude amplification.

Mixed state

Words: 210
Density matrix OurBigBook.com
A mixed state is a density operator that is not a rank-one orthogonal projection. In finite dimension it is characterized by Tr(ρ2)<1, so its purity of a density operator distinguishes it from a pure state.
Compare a pure state quantum superposition with an equal classical choice between two computational-basis states of a qubit:
∣+⟩=2​∣0⟩+∣1⟩​,ρ+​=∣+⟩⟨+∣=21​(11​11​),ρmix​=21​∣0⟩⟨0∣+21​∣1⟩⟨1∣=2I​.
(5)
A Z projective measurement in the computational basis gives P(0)=P(1)=1/2 for both density operators. An X projective measurement in the Hadamard basis ∣±⟩=(∣0⟩±∣1⟩)/2​ distinguishes them: the Born rule gives P(+)=1 for ρ+​ and P(+)=1/2 for ρmix​. These comparisons use separate copies of each preparation; measuring Z first changes the state tested by a subsequent X measurement.
The off-diagonal entries of ρ+​ encode quantum coherence in the computational basis. Such quantum coherence depends on the basis: ρ+​ is diagonal in the Hadamard basis but remains a pure state. Mixedness is independent of the basis, because a unitary change of basis preserves the eigenvalues and the purity of a density operator.
Even the quantum state ensemble is not unique:
2I​=21​∣0⟩⟨0∣+21​∣1⟩⟨1∣=21​∣+⟩⟨+∣+21​∣−⟩⟨−∣.
(6)
The off-diagonal entries of the two Hadamard basis projectors cancel. Thus the density operator retains precisely what measurements on the system can reveal, while an accessible preparation label carries additional information. A mixed state can also be the reduced density matrix of an entangled state, even when the whole system is in a pure state; classical ignorance about a selected component is not its only physical origin. Trace distance (D(ρ,σ))

Words: 1k Articles: 8
Density matrix OurBigBook.com
How different can two preparations look to someone who can perform any measurement in quantum mechanics? The trace distance answers this question for density operators ρ,σ on the same finite-dimensional vector space:
D(ρ,σ)=21​∥ρ−σ∥1​=21​Tr∣ρ−σ∣,∣A∣=A†A​.
(8)
Here ∣A∣ is the absolute value of an operator, and ∥A∥1​ is its trace norm. Because Δ=ρ−σ is Hermitian, its trace norm is the sum of the absolute values of its eigenvalues; it is not the sum of the absolute values of its matrix entries. The factor 1/2 gives
0≤D(ρ,σ)≤1.
(9)
The trace distance is a metric: it is symmetric, vanishes exactly when ρ=σ, and obeys the triangle inequality. Identical preparations have trace distance zero; preparations with orthogonal occupied eigenspaces have trace distance one and can be identified perfectly in a single measurement in quantum mechanics.
Operationally, the trace distance is the largest total variation distance between the outcome probability distributions obtainable by a POVM. Equivalently, the variational characterization of trace distance says it is the largest possible probability gap for one event in a binary measurement in quantum mechanics. For one copy of either known preparation, each chosen with prior probability 1/2, the Holevo–Helstrom theorem gives
Psuccessopt​=21+D(ρ,σ)​.
(10)
Thus trace distance zero leaves only guessing, while trace distance one permits perfect quantum state discrimination. The optimum ranges over measurements; different measurement bases can expose different amounts of distinguishability. [ref]
For a worked example with commuting density operators, take a qubit with
ρ=(0.80​00.2​),σ=(0.30​00.7​).
(11)
Their difference has eigenvalues 0.5,−0.5, so D=(∣0.5∣+∣−0.5∣)/2=0.5. Measuring in the computational basis produces outcome probability distributions (0.8,0.2) and (0.3,0.7), whose total variation distance is also 0.5. Guessing ρ on outcome zero and σ on outcome one succeeds with probability (0.8+0.7)/2=0.75. A projective measurement in the Hadamard basis instead produces (0.5,0.5) for both preparations and gives no discrimination advantage.
The trace-distance contraction under quantum channels ensures that a common quantum channel, including the partial trace, cannot increase the trace distance. This makes trace distance a useful error measure: if D≤ϵ, every event in every measurement in quantum mechanics has a probability gap at most ϵ. Losing access to a subsystem can hide distinguishability, but cannot create more of it.

For qubit density operators, the trace distance has a particularly simple geometry. Write their Bloch vectors as r,s in the Bloch ball:
ρ=2I+r⋅σ​,σ=2I+s⋅σ​,Δ=ρ−σ=2(r−s)⋅σ​,
(12)
where I is the identity operator and σ=(σx​,σy​,σz​) denotes the Pauli matrices. This Bloch vector representation includes both pure states on the Bloch sphere and mixed states in the interior of the Bloch ball. [ref]
The Pauli matrix multiplication law gives [(r−s)⋅σ]2=∣r−s∣2I. Hence the two eigenvalues of Δ are ±∣r−s∣/2, and their absolute sum gives
D(ρ,σ)=2∣r−s∣​.
(13)
The trace distance is half the straight-line Euclidean distance between the Bloch vectors, not the angular or surface distance along the Bloch sphere. Opposite Bloch vectors have trace distance one; the centre I/2 is at trace distance 1/2 from every pure state.
For the pure states ∣0⟩ and ∣+⟩=(∣0⟩+∣1⟩)/2​, the Bloch vectors are r=(0,0,1) and s=(1,0,0). Their straight-line separation is 2​, so
D(∣0⟩⟨0∣,∣+⟩⟨+∣)=2​1​.
(14)
These Bloch vectors are perpendicular, but the pure states are not orthogonal: ∣⟨0∣+⟩∣=1/2​. The Helstrom measurement for the zero and plus states measures along the difference direction n=(r−s)/2​=(−1,0,1)/2​, rather than along either input Bloch vector.

Variational characterization of trace distance

Words: 372 Articles: 1
Trace distance OurBigBook.com
The variational characterization of trace distance turns a matrix distance into the best possible binary experiment. For density operators ρ,σ on the same finite-dimensional vector space,
D(ρ,σ)=max0≤P≤I​Tr[P(ρ−σ)]=max0≤P≤I​∣Tr[P(ρ−σ)]∣.
(15)
An operator P with 0≤P≤I is an effect of a POVM: both P and I−P are positive operators. The Born rule assigns the first outcome probabilities Tr(Pρ) and Tr(Pσ). Their signed gap is Tr(PΔ), where Δ=ρ−σ. Since TrΔ=0, replacing P by I−P reverses this gap, explaining the equality with the absolute-value maximum.
One optimal P is the spectral projector P+​ onto the positive eigenspaces of Δ. In the spectral decomposition of Δ, this choice includes all positive eigenvalues and excludes all negative eigenvalues. Their positive sum equals the trace distance by the spectral-parts formula for trace distance. Any zero eigenspace can be assigned to either outcome without changing the gap. This is the effect selected by the equal-prior Holevo–Helstrom theorem. [ref]
The same optimum has a classical description. For any finite-outcome POVM {Ei​}, let pi​=Tr(Ei​ρ) and qi​=Tr(Ei​σ). Then
D(ρ,σ)=max{Ei​}​21​∑i​∣pi​−qi​∣=max{Ei​}​maxA​​∑i∈A​(pi​−qi​)​.
(16)
The expression 21​∑i​∣pi​−qi​∣ is the total variation distance of the entire outcome probability distributions; the expression for A is the gap for one event. Grouping outcomes in A gives the single effect PA​=∑i∈A​Ei​. Conversely, an effect P gives the binary POVM {P,I−P}, whose two probability gaps are opposites, so their total variation distance is ∣Tr(PΔ)∣. Thus the optimum over all POVMs is already attained by one binary projective measurement. [ref]
For quantum state discrimination with equal prior probabilities, declare ρ on outcome P and σ on outcome I−P. Directly averaging the two conditional success probabilities gives
Psuccess​(P)=21​Tr(Pρ)+21​Tr((I−P)σ)=21​(1+Tr(PΔ)).
(17)
Optimizing this expression gives (1+D)/2. For the diagonal example in trace distance, P=∣0⟩⟨0∣ gives a gap 0.8−0.3=0.5. For zero versus plus, that same effect gives only 1−1/2=1/2, while the effect in Helstrom measurement for the zero and plus states gives the larger gap 1/2​. The trace-distance-preserving binary measurement depends on the pair of preparations; a fixed measurement basis does not generally attain the trace distance for every pair.

The optimizing measurement in quantum mechanics depends on the pair; a single fixed measurement need not preserve every pair's distance.

Purity of a density operator

Words: 283
Density matrix OurBigBook.com
The purity of a density operator is γ=Tr(ρ2). It equals one exactly for a pure state; in finite dimension d it lies between 1/d and 1. The minimum occurs exactly at the maximally mixed state I/d.
A worked qubit family interpolates between the mixed state I/2 and the pure state ρ+​=∣+⟩⟨+∣, where ∣+⟩=(∣0⟩+∣1⟩)/2​:
ρp​=pρ+​+(1−p)2I​=21​(1p​p1​),0≤p≤1.
(18)
The eigenvectors are ∣±⟩=(∣0⟩±∣1⟩)/2​, with eigenvalues λ±​=(1±p)/2. Squaring and summing these eigenvalues gives
γ(p)=4(1+p)2+(1−p)2​=21+p2​,PX​(+)=Tr(ρp​∣+⟩⟨+∣)=21+p​.
(19)
Here PX​(+) is the Born rule probability of the positive outcome in an X projective measurement. The Bloch vector is (p,0,0), since ρp​=(I+pσx​)/2 with σx​ one of the Pauli matrices. At p=0 the purity is 1/2; at p=1/2 it is 5/8, with PX​(+)=3/4; at p=1 it is one. Within this family, p=1 is a pure state and every p<1 is a mixed state. Extending the formula to p>1 produces a negative eigenvalue (1−p)/2, so it ceases to be a density operator despite remaining Hermitian with trace one.
The Bloch vector length measures polarization: for any qubit density operator, γ=(1+∣r∣2)/2. This connects the interior of the Bloch ball with mixed states and its boundary, the Bloch sphere, with pure states. [ref]
For a short proof of the general bound, use the spectral theorem: the nonnegative eigenvalues λj​ sum to one, so γ=∑j​λj2​. Then
γ−d1​=∑j=1d​(λj​−d1​)2≥0,1−γ=2∑j<k​λj​λk​≥0.
(20)
The lower bound is attained only when all eigenvalues equal 1/d; the upper bound is attained only when exactly one eigenvalue is nonzero, and hence equals one. Purity is therefore a simple quantitative test of mixedness, invariant under a unitary operator. It does not specify the entire density operator: even distinct pure states all have purity one. [ref]
