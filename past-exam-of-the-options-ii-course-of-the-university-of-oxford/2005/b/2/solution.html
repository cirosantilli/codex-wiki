Map the integers into a field by n↦n1. Its kernel is a prime ideal of Z, since the image has no zero divisors. Thus the kernel is either zero or (p) for a prime p. In the zero case, the map extends injectively to the rational numbers by taking quotients of nonzero integers. In the other case, its image is the field Fp​. This proves the prime-field assertion.
A finite field cannot contain the infinite rational field, so it has characteristic p. It is a finite-dimensional vector space over its prime field Fp​, say of dimension n≥1, and choosing a basis counts its elements as
∣F∣=pn.​
(3)
For existence, put q=pn and take a splitting field of xq−x over Fp​. Its derivative is −1, so it has exactly q distinct polynomial roots. The polynomial roots are closed under addition and multiplication because the Frobenius identity gives (u+v)q=uq+vq and (uv)q=uqvq. They include 0,1, are closed under additive inverses, and for a nonzero polynomial root u, (u−1)q=(uq)−1=u−1. They therefore form a field of exactly q elements. Indeed this field is the splitting field because it contains all polynomial roots and is generated by them.
