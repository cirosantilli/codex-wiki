The Milne-Thomson circle theorem says that if a complex potential f(z) has no singularities on or inside ∣z∣=a, then a circular impermeable boundary can be introduced by taking
W(z)=f(z)+f∗(a2/z),f∗(w)=f(w)​.
(59)
The new singularities are the reflected ones inside the circle. On ∣z∣=a, a2/z=z, so W(z)=f(z)+f(z)​ is real; the stream function is constant and there is no normal velocity. At infinity the added term tends to f∗(0), a harmless potential constant, so it preserves the far-field flow. This proves the theorem with zero added net circulation about the cylinder. A logarithmic term may then specify a different cylinder circulation.
For f(z)=−iΓlog(z−b)/(2π), put c=a2/b. The reflected term is
f∗(a2/z)=2πiΓ​log(a2/z−b)=2πiΓ​log(z−c)−2πiΓ​logz+constant,
(60)
with locally consistent logarithm branches. The circle-theorem solution has zero cylinder circulation: its image vortex at c and its central vortex cancel. To impose circulation −Γ, add iΓlogz/(2π). The result is
w(z)=−2πiΓ​log(z−b)+2πiΓ​log(z−c)+constant.​
(61)
It consists of the physical vortex and an opposite image vortex inside the obstacle. On the circle, ∣z−b∣=(b/a)∣z−c∣, so its stream function is indeed constant.
The complex velocity is
q(z)=w′(z)=−2πiΓ​(z−b1​−z−c1​).
(62)
Inside the positively oriented obstacle contour, q2 has only the double pole at c. Its residue is
Resz=c​q(z)2=dzd​[−4π2(z−b)2Γ2(b−c)2​]​z=c​=−2π2(b−c)Γ2​.
(63)
The residue theorem and the supplied Blasius force theorem therefore give
Fx​−iFy​=2iρ​(2πi)[−2π2(b−c)Γ2​]=2π(b−c)ρΓ2​.
(64)
Consequently
Fx​=2π(b2−a2)ρΓ2b​,Fy​=0.​
(65)
The force points toward the held vortex, independent of the sign of its circulation.
The residue calculation is the force on a circular cylinder from a held exterior vortex.
