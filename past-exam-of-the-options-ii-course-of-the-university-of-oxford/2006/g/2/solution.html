Write K[y]=∫ab​G(x,y,y′)dx. At a regular constrained extremal, the first variation of K is not identically zero. Choose a variation ζ for which δK[ζ]=0; the implicit function theorem supplies a correction in the ζ direction to each other variation so that K remains one. Stationarity of J on these corrected variations implies δJ=λδK for one scalar λ.
Put L=F−λG. Integration by parts gives, for an arbitrary twice continuously differentiable variation η,
0=δ(J−λK)[η]=∫ab​(Ly​−dxd​Ly′​)ηdx+[Ly′​η]ab​.
(32)
Taking variations with zero endpoint values and using the stated fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations gives the Euler-Lagrange equation. The remaining endpoint values are free and independent, giving the natural boundary conditions:
Fy​−λGy​−dxd​(Fy′​−λGy′​)=0,(Fy′​−λGy′​)(a)=(Fy′​−λGy′​)(b)=0.​

For that example L=py′2−qy2−λwy2, so the equation and endpoint conditions reduce to
(py′)′+qy+λwy=0,py′(a)=py′(b)=0.

Multiply the equation by y and integrate. The boundary term [py′y]ab​ vanishes, giving
−∫ab​py′2dx+∫ab​qy2dx+λ∫ab​wy2dx=0.

The last integral equals one, so λ=J[y], the functional value at the constrained extremal.
Finally multiply the equation for y1​ by y2​ and the equation for y2​ by y1​, then subtract and integrate. The derivative terms give only the vanishing boundary term [p(y2​y1′​−y1​y2′​)]ab​, while the q terms cancel. Therefore (λ1​−λ2​)∫ab​wy1​y2​dx=0. For distinct multipliers this proves
