Right multiplication gives a right action (Hx)⋅g=Hxg. To obtain a homomorphism into permutations with the usual function-composition convention, use the associated left action
ρ(g)(Hx)=Hxg−1.
(1)
This is well-defined: replacing x by hx, h∈H, does not change the image coset. Moreover ρ(g)ρ(k)(Hx)=Hxk−1g−1=ρ(gk)(Hx), and each map is bijective with inverse ρ(g−1).
An element g is in the kernel precisely when Hxg−1=Hx for every x∈G. This says xg−1x−1∈H, equivalently g∈x−1Hx. Hence
kerρ=x∈G⋂​x−1Hx.​
(2)
This is the largest normal subgroup of G contained in H.
Now the index of H is q, so G/kerρ embeds into Sq​. Its order therefore divides q!. Since p>q is prime, p does not divide q!. From ∣G∣=pq it follows that p divides ∣kerρ∣. But kerρ⊆H and ∣H∣=p, forcing kerρ=H. Therefore H is normal.
For the last case let H=⟨b⟩. Normality makes conjugation by a an automorphism, so a−1ba=br with r∈{1,2,3,4}. Applying it twice and using a2=1 gives b=br2, so r2≡1(mod5). Thus (r−1)(r+1)≡0(mod5), and
a−1ba=b or b−1.​
(3)
Both possibilities are allowed; the order assumptions alone do not force the inverse case.

An irreducible polynomial has positive degree, so its ideal is proper: otherwise 1=fh would contradict degrees. Every nonzero class [g] has a representative not divisible by f. Irreducibility gives gcd(g,f)=1, and the Euclidean algorithm supplies polynomials u,v with ug+vf=1. Passing to the quotient gives [u][g]=1. Thus the quotient is a nonzero commutative ring in which every nonzero element has an inverse, hence a field. Constants embed injectively because no nonzero constant lies in the positive-degree ideal (f).

First address the general splitting-field request. A splitting field is an extension in which the polynomial factors into linear factors and which is generated over the base field by its roots. For a degree-m polynomial, adjoin a root of an irreducible factor. The quotient construction in the preceding parts gives an extension of degree at most m. [L:F]≤m(m−1)⋯1=m!.

A primitive root modulo p is an integer whose residue generates the multiplicative group Fp×​, equivalently has order p−1.
The Chinese remainder theorem states that, for pairwise coprime positive integers m1​,…,mr​, the system x≡ai​(modmi​) has a unique solution modulo M=∏i​mi​. For a constructive proof put Mi​=M/mi​. Bézout's identity gives an integer ui​ with Mi​ui​≡1(modmi​). Then x=∑i​ai​Mi​ui​ has all the required residues. If two solutions exist, their difference is divisible by every mi​; pairwise coprimality makes it divisible by their product, proving uniqueness modulo M.
Here 3,5,7 divide p−1, and none is zero modulo p. Choose a primitive root g and exponents a,b,c with
3≡ga,5≡gb,7≡gc(modp).
(12)
Use the theorem with moduli 3,5,7 to choose r such that r≡a(mod3), r≡b(mod5), r≡c(mod7). Define nonzero residues
n=gr,x=g(r−a)/3,y=g(r−b)/5,z=g(r−c)/7.
(13)
The exponents are integers; negative ones are interpreted using inverses in Fp×​. Taking integer representatives now gives
p∤n,n≡3x3≡5y5≡7z7(modp).​
(14)
The theorem aligns the exponent modular congruences, which is why a primitive root converts the three power conditions into a linear modular congruence problem.

A subset is compact if every cover of it by open sets of the ambient space has a finite subcover; equivalently use open sets of its subspace topology. A cover of A∪B separately covers A and B. Compactness of each supplies two finite collections, whose union is finite and covers A∪B. If A is closed in a compact space, add the open complement of A to any open cover of A. Compactness supplies a finite subcover of the whole space, and discarding the complement leaves a finite cover of A.
For the enlarged space, every old open set is open in C and has the same trace on X. The trace on X of a new open set V∪{∞} is V, which is open in X because its complement is closed. Thus the induced topology on X is exactly the original one.
Given an open cover of C, choose a member containing infinity. It has the form V∪{∞} with K=X∖V compact. The remaining cover restricted to K has a finite subcover. Together with the chosen infinity-neighborhood, these finitely many sets cover C. Hence C is compact, with no Hausdorff assumption needed.
Finally put a=f(∞). Continuity makes U=f−1((a−ϵ,a+ϵ)) open and containing infinity. Therefore U=V∪{∞} with W=X∖V compact and closed in X. For every x∈X∖W=V we have
∣f(x)−a∣<ϵ.​
(23)
This expresses convergence to the value at infinity outside compact closed subsets.

A quotient map π:X→Y is a surjection such that U⊆Y is open if and only if π−1(U) is open in X. Thus the topology on Y is exactly the final topology induced by π.
For an open continuous surjection, continuity gives one implication. For the other, if π−1(U) is open then U=π(π−1(U)) is open by openness and surjectivity. Hence it is a quotient map.
If π:X→Y and σ:Y→Z are quotient maps, they are surjective and
(σ∘π)−1(U)=π−1(σ−1(U)).
(24)
This set is open exactly when σ−1(U) is open, exactly when U is open. Thus the composition is a quotient map.
For the factorization, continuity of g implies continuity of f=g∘π. Conversely, if f is continuous and U is open in Z, then π−1(g−1(U))=f−1(U) is open. The quotient property makes g−1(U) open. This holds for every open U, so g is continuous.

The two singly marked sides of the first triangle are glued with their endpoints at A matched. A cross-section parallel to the remaining side has its two endpoints identified, becoming a circle; the apex becomes its cone point. Thus this quotient triangle is a disc, whose boundary is the doubly marked edge.
In the second triangle, the two triply marked sides run consecutively along the boundary from E through D to F. Pairing these sides in the indicated directions introduces one reversal; its boundary word is aab−1, where b is the remaining doubly marked edge. This is a Möbius band with boundary b. One can obtain the usual rectangle model by cutting from the identified triple-edge seam to the midpoint of b and opening the triangle.
The double markings glue the disc boundary to the boundary of that Möbius band. Capping a Möbius band by a disc gives
RP2, the real projective plane.​
(25)

A cubic spline is a twice continuously differentiable function whose restriction to each knot interval is a polynomial of degree at most three. Its second derivative is continuous and piecewise linear. Natural endpoint conditions are C′′(x0​)=C′′(xn​)=0.
Linearity of both the piecewise-polynomial and smoothness conditions, and of the endpoint equations, makes the natural splines a vector space V. A continuous piecewise linear second derivative with zero endpoint values is determined freely by its n−1 interior nodal values. Integrating twice adds two arbitrary affine constants. Hence
dimV=(n−1)+2=n+1.​
(74)
This count includes the case of one interval.
An interpolating spline has S(xi​)=fi​ at every node. Set e=T−S, so e vanishes at all nodes. On each open knot interval S(3) is constant. Two integrations by parts give
∫xi−1​xi​​e′′S′′dx=[e′S′′−eS(3)]xi−1​xi​​.
(75)
The terms containing e vanish at nodes. When summed, the interior e′S′′ terms cancel because e′ and S′′ are continuous. The two outer terms vanish by the natural endpoint conditions. Thus
∫x0​xn​​(T′′−S′′)S′′dx=0.​
(76)
Integrating the given square identity now yields the sharper energy decomposition
∫x0​xn​​(T′′)2dx=∫x0​xn​​(S′′)2dx+∫x0​xn​​(T′′−S′′)2dx.
(77)
In particular the natural interpolating spline minimizes the squared-curvature integral.
If T is another natural interpolant, the same minimizing argument in both directions makes the two energies equal. The final nonnegative integral then vanishes, so T′′=S′′ everywhere by continuity. Their difference is affine and zero at all knots, including the two distinct endpoints, so T=S. This proves uniqueness.
It also supplies existence if needed: nodal evaluation is a linear map from the (n+1)-dimensional space V to Rn+1. Its kernel is zero by the same argument with zero data and the zero competitor. It is therefore surjective, so every data vector has a natural spline interpolant.
