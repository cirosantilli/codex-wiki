Count the pairs (g,ω) for which g fixes ω. Counting first by group elements gives ∑g​Fix(g), while counting first by points gives ∑ω​∣Stab(ω)∣. On a group orbit of size r, orbit-stabilizer theorem makes each stabilizer have size ∣G∣/r, so that group orbit contributes ∣G∣ to the second sum. Dividing by ∣G∣ proves Burnside's lemma:
number of orbits=∣G∣1​g∈G∑​Fix(g).​
(1)
A tetrahedron has four vertices. A rotational symmetry is determined by the image of one vertex, with four possibilities, and the cyclic orientation of the three vertices around it, with three possibilities. All these choices are realizable, so its rotational symmetry group of a tetrahedron has order 12. More explicitly, its elements are the identity, the two nontrivial rotational symmetries around each of four vertex-to-opposite-face axes, and the half-turn about each of three axes through opposite edge midpoints. Thus there is one element of order 1, eight of order 3, and three of order 2. The induced permutations of vertices are the even permutations, identifying the group with A4​.
Apply the group action to the set of edge colourings. A colouring is fixed by a rotational symmetry precisely when its colour is constant on each cycle of the induced edge permutation. The identity has six edge cycles and fixes 46 colourings. A vertex-axis rotational symmetry has two cycles of length three, fixing 42. An opposite-edge half-turn fixes the two edges on its axis as sets and exchanges the other four in two pairs, giving four cycles and 44 fixed colourings. Therefore
1246+8⋅42+3⋅44​=416.​
(2)
Reflections are excluded because the equivalence in the source uses rotational symmetries.

Map the integers into a field by n↦n1. Its kernel is a prime ideal of Z, since the image has no zero divisors. Thus the kernel is either zero or (p) for a prime p. In the zero case, the map extends injectively to the rational numbers by taking quotients of nonzero integers. In the other case, its image is the field Fp​. This proves the prime-field assertion.
A finite field cannot contain the infinite rational field, so it has characteristic p. It is a finite-dimensional vector space over its prime field Fp​, say of dimension n≥1, and choosing a basis counts its elements as
∣F∣=pn.​
(3)
For existence, put q=pn and take a splitting field of xq−x over Fp​. Its derivative is −1, so it has exactly q distinct polynomial roots. The polynomial roots are closed under addition and multiplication because the Frobenius identity gives (u+v)q=uq+vq and (uv)q=uqvq. They include 0,1, are closed under additive inverses, and for a nonzero polynomial root u, (u−1)q=(uq)−1=u−1. They therefore form a field of exactly q elements. Indeed this field is the splitting field because it contains all polynomial roots and is generated by them.
In order 4, characteristic is 2. For ω=0,1, the multiplicative group has order 3, so ω3=1 and ω2=1,ω. Factorization of x3−1 gives ω2+ω+1=0. The quadratic x2+x+1 has no polynomial root in F2​ and is the minimal polynomial. Write s=ω2=ω+1. The complete tables are

Fubini's theorem for the product of two sigma-finite measure spaces states that an integrable measurable function on the product has integrable sections almost everywhere, the section integrals define Lebesgue integrable functions, and both iterated integrals equal the product integral. The integrability hypothesis is absolute integrability. For nonnegative measurable functions, Tonelli's theorem gives equality of the possibly infinite product and iterated integrals without first assuming finiteness.
The displayed function is not Lebesgue integrable. Its value at the origin can be assigned arbitrarily, since a point is a null set. On 0<x<1 and 0<y<x/2,
​(x+y)3x−y​​≥(3x/2)3x/2​=27x24​.
(24)
Consequently Tonelli's theorem gives the divergent lower bound
∫01​∫01​∣f(x,y)∣dydx≥272​∫01​xdx​=∞.
(25)
The signed iterated integrals illustrate why the hypothesis matters. For x>0, f(x,y)=∂y​[y/(x+y)2], so integration first in y and then in x gives 1/2. Integrating first in x gives −1/2, using f(x,y)=−∂x​[x/(x+y)2]. Exceptional boundary sections have measure zero. Thus finite iterated values do not establish product integrability.

The product topology on X×Y has the open rectangles U×V, with U open in X and V open in Y, as a topological basis. The projection preimages are pX−1​(U)=U×Y and pY−1​(V)=X×V, so both projections are continuous.
If f:Z→X×Y is continuous, both coordinate compositions are continuous. Conversely, if both are continuous, then
f−1(U×V)=(pX​∘f)−1(U)∩(pY​∘f)−1(V)
(30)
is open for every basic rectangle, proving continuity of f. Given paths between two chosen endpoints in each factor, their coordinatewise pair is therefore a continuous path in the product, proving that a product of path-connected spaces is path-connected.
For the compact-neighborhood assertion, fix x∈K. For each y∈L, choose an open rectangle Uy​×Vy​ containing (x,y) inside W. Compactness of L chooses finitely many Vyj​​ covering L. Put
Ux​=⋂j​Uyj​​,Vx​=⋃j​Vyj​​.
(31)
Then x∈Ux​, L⊆Vx​, and Ux​×Vx​⊆W: any point of Vx​ belongs to one of those rectangles' second factors, while Ux​ lies in every first factor.
Compactness of K now selects finitely many Uxi​​ covering K. Set
U=⋃i​Uxi​​,V=⋂i​Vxi​​.
(32)
These are open, contain K and L, and if (u,v)∈U×V, choose an i with u∈Uxi​​ and use v∈Vxi​​. Hence
K×L⊆U×V⊆W.​
(33)
If K or L is empty, take the corresponding open factor empty and the other factor the whole space; the assertions then hold directly. No Hausdorff assumption on the factors is needed.

A Hausdorff space separates any two distinct points by disjoint open neighborhoods. For a subspace A⊆X, intersect such neighborhoods in X with A to obtain disjoint neighborhoods in the subspace topology. Thus subspaces inherit the Hausdorff property.
If (z1​,z2​) lies off the diagonal of a Hausdorff space's square, choose disjoint open neighborhoods U of z1​ and V of z2​. The open rectangle U×V contains the pair and avoids the diagonal. Therefore its complement is open and the diagonal is closed.
The map F:X×Y→Z×Z given by F(x,y)=(f(x),g(y)) is continuous by the coordinate criterion from E3. The equality locus is F−1(ΔZ​) and is closed as an inverse image of a closed set.
Let q:X→X/∼ be the quotient map. If the quotient is Hausdorff, apply the same argument to q in both coordinates:
R=(q×q)−1(ΔX/∼​)
(34)
is closed in X×X.
Conversely suppose R is closed and q is open. Take two distinct quotient points, with representatives x,y. Since (x,y)∈/R, an open rectangle U×V around it avoids R. The images q(U) and q(V) are open quotient neighborhoods. They are disjoint, because a quotient point in their intersection would have equivalent representatives u∈U, v∈V, giving (u,v)∈R. Hence the quotient is Hausdorff. Thus closedness of the relation and openness of the quotient map suffice for a Hausdorff quotient.
