Words: 249 Articles: 1
A OurBigBook.com

Solution

Words: 249
A2 OurBigBook.com
Every nonzero vector can be extended to a basis, and the invertible map between two such bases carries the first vector to the second. The action is therefore transitive. Counting ordered bases gives
∣G∣=(8−1)(8−2)(8−4)=168.
(1)
The stabilizer H of one nonzero vector consequently has order 168/7=24. There are three planes containing that vector, corresponding to the three lines of the two-dimensional quotient space. Adapted bases show H acts transitively on these planes, so the stabilizer of one such plane has order 24/3=8.
Let Q be any subgroup of order eight. Its orbit lengths on the seven nonzero vectors are powers of two. Since their sum is odd, at least one orbit has length one. Fix such a vector v. The action on the three planes through v likewise has a fixed plane P. Thus Q is contained in the stabilizer of the flag ⟨v⟩⊂P, and equality follows because that stabilizer already has order eight. The full group acts transitively on these flags, so all the subgroups are conjugate.
There are 7⋅3=21 flags. To see they yield distinct subgroups, use the standard upper unitriangular matrices relative to an adapted basis. Their only common nonzero fixed vector is the first basis vector: varying entries in positions (1,2) and (1,3) forces the other two coordinates to vanish. On the quotient by that line, varying position (2,3) leaves only the line of the second basis vector fixed. Hence this subgroup fixes exactly one full flag. Conjugation transfers uniqueness to each flag stabilizer, giving
21 subgroups of order eight​.
(2)

An element is algebraic over F if it is a zero of some nonzero polynomial in F[x]. An extension is algebraic if every element is algebraic. If [K:F]=d<∞, the d+1 elements 1,α,…,αd are linearly dependent over F, proving every α∈K is algebraic.
The converse fails for the algebraic closure Q​ inside C. All its elements are algebraic by definition, but it contains 21/n of degree n for every positive integer n, since xn−2 is irreducible by Eisenstein's criterion at two. A finite extension could not contain elements of unbounded degree, so this algebraic extension is infinite.

C2 OurBigBook.com
Wilson's theorem states that for a prime p, (p−1)!≡−1(modp). For odd p, pair each nonzero residue with its multiplicative inverse. Only the self-inverse residues remain unpaired; x2=1 in a field gives x=±1. Their product is −1, proving the result. For p=2 the assertion is immediate.
If composite n≥6 is not the square of a prime, choose a prime divisor p such that p and n/p are distinct. Both occur among the factors of (n−1)!, so their product n divides it. In the remaining case n=p2, p≥3, and the two distinct factors p,2p both occur in the factorial and have product divisible by p2. Thus the factorial is zero modulo every such composite n.
For prime n, Wilson's congruence makes the numerator sine squared equal sin2(π/n), so its ratio is one. For composite n≥6, the numerator vanishes. The only exceptional composite in the sum is four; its ratio is sin2(3π/2)/sin2(π/4)=2. Therefore for x≥4 the sum counts the primes plus two, and subtracting two gives exactly the required prime count. All statements concern the integers 2≤n≤⌊x⌋.

b OurBigBook.com
The surface is a Klein bottle. Here is a cell-count justification that keeps all the arrow directions. Name the corners of the first square a,b,c,d, beginning at its upper left and proceeding clockwise; name those of the second e,f,g,h in the same way. The single-arrow gluing identifies a with e and b with h. The triple-arrow gluing identifies d with h and c with g. The first square's quadruple-arrow gluing identifies b with d and c with a. The second square's double-arrow gluing identifies e,f,g with one another. There are exactly two resulting vertex classes:
{a,c,e,f,g},{b,d,h}.
(12)
There are four edge classes and two faces, so the Euler characteristic is 2−4+2=0. Every edge is paired and the corner links close into circles, giving a compact surface without boundary; the cross-square identifications make it connected. It is nonorientable: the quadruple-arrow identification glues two boundary edges of the same square in the same boundary-traversal direction, so no orientation of that face can extend across the gluing. The classification of compact connected surfaces now identifies the nonorientable surface of Euler characteristic zero as the Klein bottle.
Geometrically, the internal identification in each square produces a Möbius band. The remaining single- and triple-arrow edges comprise their two boundary circles, which are glued together. The double of a Möbius band is another description of the same Klein bottle.

A Poisson process of rate λ is a counting process starting at zero with independent stationary increments, with N(t)−N(s) Poisson of mean λ(t−s) for t≥s, and right-continuous paths with unit jumps. Equivalently the small-interval probabilities are λh+o(h) for one event and o(h) for two or more.
Independence of the two processes makes their joint increments over disjoint intervals independent. On an interval of length h, the sum of their independent Poisson increments has probability generating function
eαh(z−1)eβh(z−1)=e(α+β)h(z−1),
(42)
so it is Poisson with mean (α+β)h. Simultaneous arrivals have probability zero, so the sum retains unit jumps. It is therefore a Poisson process of rate α+β.
The first arrival times are independent exponential variables of rates α and β. For positive rates,
P(first arrival red)=∫0∞​αe−αte−βtdt=α+βα​​.
