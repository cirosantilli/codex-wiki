The permutation inverse is ρg−1​: applying the two maps successively sends ω to (ωg)g−1=ω. With the paper's right-action convention, permutation products also act on the right, and
ω(ρg​ρh​)=(ωg)h=ω(gh)=ωρgh​.
(1)
Thus ρ(gh)=ρ(g)ρ(h)​. If function composition is written on the left instead, the same right action is represented by g↦ρg−1​; keeping these conventions consistent avoids an artificial reversal. The kernel consists precisely of elements fixing every point, so
kerρ=ω∈Ω⋂​Gω​.​
(2)
The rotational tetrahedral group acts transitively on its four vertices. A rotation fixing a vertex must preserve its opposite face and rotate its three vertices cyclically; its stabilizer has order three. The orbit-stabilizer theorem gives ∣G∣=4⋅3=12. The rotations are the identity, two nontrivial rotations about each of the four vertex-to-opposite-face axes, and a half-turn about each of the three opposite-edge-midpoint axes. Therefore the counts by element order are
n#​11​23​38​40​60​120​.​
(3)
A rotation fixing all vertices fixes three linearly independent vertex vectors and is the identity. The vertex permutation action is therefore faithful. The listed rotations induce the identity, 3-cycles and double transpositions, all even permutations. Its injective image has order 12, the full order of A4​. Hence G≅A4​​.

Take K=F[x]/(f)​ and let α be the residue class of x. Division with remainder represents every class by a polynomial of degree less than n, so every element is a polynomial in α and f(α)=0. A nonzero class represented by u has gcd(u,f)=1 because f is irreducible. Bézout's identity gives au+bf=1, making the class of a its inverse. Thus K is a field, containing F through its constant classes.
A splitting field of a nonconstant polynomial is an extension in which it factors into linear factors and which is generated over the original field by its roots. To construct one, choose an irreducible factor and use the preceding quotient construction to adjoin a root. Divide off a linear factor in that extension and repeat on the remaining polynomial. Each stage lowers the remaining degree, so finitely many stages suffice. The field generated by all roots in the resulting field is the required splitting field. This induction works with repeated roots and does not assume separability. The two roman clauses in the source specify properties of this one construction; they are not separate questions.

Lebesgue measurability of g means that g−1(U) is a Lebesgue measurable subset of R for every open subset U⊆R. Equivalently one can check all sets {x:g(x)>t} with real t.
There are two conventions for a step function on the whole real line. Under the bounded-support integration convention it is a finite linear combination of indicators of bounded intervals; under the unrestricted piecewise-constant convention nonzero constant tails are allowed. The conclusions below state both conventions explicitly, since only the first assertion depends on this choice.

a OurBigBook.com
Under the bounded-support convention, false in general, true if f(0)=0. For the first claim, take g=0 and f≡1: the composition is one on all of R, and cannot have bounded support. For the second, refine the finitely many defining intervals into disjoint intervals on which g is constant. The composition is constant on each of them and vanishes outside their bounded union because f(0)=0. Hence it is another step function.
Under the unrestricted piecewise-constant convention, true in both cases: composition with any function preserves constancy on the same partition, including its unbounded cells. This also applies to the broader measurable-partition convention. Continuity of f is not needed for this particular assertion.

The nested closed sets have the finite intersection property: the intersection of finitely many is the one with the largest index and is nonempty. Compactness gives Y∞​=∅​.
For an open neighbourhood W of the intersection, suppose no Yn​ were contained in W. Then Zn​=Yn​∩(X∖W) would be nested, nonempty and closed. The same argument gives a point in all Zn​, contradicting Y∞​⊆W. Hence
Yn​⊆W for some n.​
(16)
For connectedness, suppose Y∞​=A∪B were a separation into two nonempty relatively closed sets. Since Y∞​ is compact, A,B are disjoint compact subsets of X. The supplied separation fact gives disjoint open neighbourhoods UA​,UB​. Their union contains Y∞​, so some Yn​ lies in that union. But Yn​ meets both neighbourhoods, since it contains A and B. Its intersections with the two neighbourhoods are then a separation, contradicting its connectedness. Thus the nested intersection is connected.

For the quotient map π, a subset U of the quotient is open exactly when π−1(U) is open in X. This definition immediately makes π continuous. If g is continuous then g∘π is continuous. Conversely, continuity of the composition gives, for every open V⊆Z, that π−1(g−1(V)) is open. By the defining criterion g−1(V) is open, so g is continuous.
A map h constant on each equivalence class defines a unique map hˉ([x])=h(x). It is well-defined by the assumed class constancy, and its composition with π is h. The preceding criterion proves that hˉ is continuous.
For the integer lattice quotient, define
F(x,y)=(e2πix,e2πiy).
(17)
This continuous map is onto the product of the two unit circles, and its fibres are exactly the lattice equivalence classes. It therefore induces a continuous bijection Fˉ:R2/Z2→T2. Every class has a representative in [0,1]2, so the quotient is the continuous image of that compact square and is compact. The target is Hausdorff as a subspace of C2. A continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism, proving
R2/Z2≅T2.​
(18)
Finally, if U⊆R2 is open, then π−1(π(U))=⋃m∈Z2​(U+m) is open, being a union of translates. The quotient criterion makes π(U) open. Thus the quotient map is an open map.
