Uniform convergence (∥fn​−f∥∞​→0)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Sequence and series
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 13
A sequence of functions fn​:X→Y converges uniformly to f:X→Y, where Y is a metric space with metric d, precisely when
∀ε>0∃N∈N>0​∀x∈X∀n≥N:d(fn​(x),f(x))<ε.
(116)
For real numbers, use d(u,v)=∣u−v∣, the absolute value of the difference. The universal quantification over inputs and existential quantification of the index change order: pointwise convergence allows ∀x∀ε>0∃N(x,ε), whereas uniform convergence requires ∀ε>0∃N(ε)∀x. Every later approximation must meet the same prescribed accuracy everywhere. This is valuable when the input is unknown beforehand or changes with the approximation index. Uniform convergence implies pointwise convergence; the converse fails. [ref].
For functions taking values in real numbers on a nonempty domain of a function, the supremum error is En​=supx∈X​∣fn​(x)−f(x)∣, allowing +∞. Then uniform convergence is equivalent to En​→0, using the supremum norm when the difference is a bounded function. A supremum need not be attained, so bounds ∣fn​(x)−f(x)∣<ε at every input give En​≤ε; applying them with ε/2 gives the strict bound needed for the limit of a sequence.
For an easy uniform convergence example, let hn​(x)=x/(n+1) on the interval [0,1], for integers n≥1. Its uniform limit is zero and its supremum error is exactly 1/(n+1), attained at x=1. For every ε>0, the explicit choice N=max{1,⌈1/ε⌉} works, where the ceiling function rounds upward: if n≥N then 1/(n+1)<ε. For ε=0.01, choose N=100, giving En​≤1/101≈0.00990099. This provides a finite stopping rule before the input is known.
For fn​(x)=xn and its pointwise limit on [0,1], the error is zero at one but approaches one as x→1 from below. Consequently
supx∈[0,1]​∣fn​(x)−f(x)∣=1
(117)
for every positive integer n. This supremum is not attained: at x<1 the error is xn<1, and at x=1 it is zero. Even deleting the endpoint leaves supremum error one on [0,1). The supremum error persists although every fixed input eventually becomes accurate. Indeed, xn​=2−1/n<1 gives error exactly 1/2 at every index. On the smaller interval [0,a], for any fixed 0≤a<1,
supx∈[0,a]​∣fn​(x)∣=an⟶0.
(118)
For a=0.8 and n=20, the exact bound is 0.820=0.01152921504606846976. A tolerance of 0.01 needs n≥21, since 0.821=0.009223372036854775808. Taking the logarithm gives n>log(0.01)/log(0.8)≈20.6377; the ceiling function gives 21 here because the ratio is not an integer. If the ratio were an integer, the strict tolerance would require the next integer. The domain of a function determines whether this approximation gives a common accuracy guarantee. [ref].
The uniform limit theorem explains why uniform convergence of continuous functions preserves continuity. Its proof is short: fix x and ε>0, choose one fN​ with supremum error below ε/3, and use its continuity to make ∣fN​(y)−fN​(x)∣<ε/3 for y close enough to x. The triangle inequality then gives ∣f(y)−f(x)∣<ε. The same fN​ must control both inputs. No compactness assumption is needed. [ref].
On a fixed finite interval [a,b], uniform convergence preserves integrals on a compact interval: if each fn​ is Riemann integrable, so is f, and ∣∫ab​fn​−∫ab​f∣≤(b−a)En​→0. For hn​(x)=x/(n+1) on the interval [0,1], the integral is 1/[2(n+1)]→0, agreeing with the integral of its zero uniform limit. [ref].
However, uniform convergence of functions does not imply convergence of their derivatives. On the interval [0,π], take qn​(x)=sin(nx)/n, using the sine function. Its supremum error from zero is 1/n→0, attained at x=π/(2n). But its derivative is qn′​(x)=cos(nx), using the cosine function, and qn′​(π)=(−1)n alternates between one and minus one. Thus the derivatives fail even pointwise convergence, despite uniform convergence of the original functions. Here the derivatives mean those of the same formulas defined on the real line and then restricted to the interval [0,π]. To exchange differentiation and a limit of a sequence, additional control such as uniform derivative convergence with an anchored value is needed. [ref].
