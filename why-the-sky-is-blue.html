Why the sky is blue

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Electromagnetic radiation Rayleigh scattering
OurBigBook.com Words: 168
In a clear terrestrial atmosphere, Rayleigh scattering by air molecules redirects some sunlight toward an observer looking away from the Sun. Shorter wavelengths are preferentially scattered, enriching the scattered light in blue compared with the incoming sunlight. Along the longer atmospheric path to the low Sun, more short-wavelength light is removed from the direct beam, helping produce red sunsets. [ref].
Violet light has an even shorter wavelength than blue light, so the Rayleigh scattering wavelength dependence alone cannot predict the perceived sky color. The incoming solar optical spectrum and the eye's differing response to wavelengths both matter; human vision is less sensitive to violet than to blue. [ref]. Multiple scattering and other atmospheric particles also affect the observed sky. Cloud droplets are generally too large for this simple Rayleigh scattering model: Mie scattering describes spherical droplets, with much weaker visible wavelength selectivity, while repeated scattering helps make clouds appear white. [ref].
