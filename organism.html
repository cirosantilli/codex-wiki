Organism

 Home Biology
OurBigBook.com Words: 12k Articles: 428
An organism is an individual living system, made of one biological cell or an integrated assembly of biological cells. An Escherichia coli cell is an entire organism; a neuron is normally a specialized part of an animal. Metabolism, regulated exchange through cell membranes, and inherited information connect these very different scales. See OpenStax Biology 2e, 1.2: living systems and levels of organization.
Keep two questions separate: how many biological cells make the organism, and how those biological cells are organized. A yeast and an Escherichia coli are both unicellular organisms, but the yeast is a eukaryote and the Escherichia coli a prokaryote. A plant and an animal are multicellular organisms with eukaryotic cellular organization. Neither distinction ranks organisms as better or worse: natural selection acts on reproduction in particular environments.

Unicellular organism

Words: 289
Organism OurBigBook.com
A unicellular organism is an organism whose living body consists of one biological cell. That biological cell maintains itself, grows and participates in reproduction; it is not merely one specialized component of a larger body. Escherichia coli and ordinary free-living Saccharomyces cerevisiae illustrate prokaryotic and eukaryotic examples respectively. Cellular organization and the number of biological cells are separate questions. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 4.1: cells as units of organisms.
How can one biological cell do enough to sustain an organism? Its cell membrane retains useful material while membrane transport allows exchange; metabolism transforms nutrients and supplies usable energy; gene expression makes proteins needed to maintain and rebuild the machinery. These functions operate together inside the same boundary. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, for example, glucose can support alcoholic fermentation and production of ATP, while the cell cycle and budding produce descendants. A single biological cell therefore combines chemical processing, regulated exchange and inherited organization. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell (2002): universal cellular machinery and the experimental accounts under Saccharomyces cerevisiae and yeast.
Being a unicellular organism does not mean isolated or independent of all other life. Unicellular organisms can interact and cooperate in groups. A biological colony describes association; clonal development describes descent; developmental integration describes coordination of the developing whole. Neither shared ancestry nor a visible clump alone establishes the latter. This distinction makes single-cell experiments easier to interpret: measuring the growth of one biological cell, the expansion of a group and the reproductive success of a reproducing cluster can answer different questions. See Queller and Strassmann, Beyond society: the evolution of organismality (2009): cooperation and the limits of simple organism criteria.

Multicellular organism

Words: 2k Articles: 25
Organism OurBigBook.com
A multicellular organism has a body of multiple biological cells whose growth and activities are integrated into the life of the whole organism. Extensive cell differentiation is common in complex examples, but specialized biological cells are not a prerequisite for every simple form. Ask how the body develops, how its parts cooperate and how it produces descendants, rather than counting attached biological cells alone. The boundary with a biological colony depends on the degree of integration and the criteria being investigated; there is no universal cell-count threshold. See the Queller and Strassmann account cited under unicellular organism.
Specialization makes the whole capable of combinations that one general-purpose biological cell cannot achieve in the same way. In an animal, a neuron transmits signals while a red blood cell carries oxygen; each still requires a working cell membrane and metabolism. A mature mammalian red blood cell lacks a cell nucleus and mitochondria, illustrating how a useful part need not retain the equipment of a self-reproducing organism. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 40.2: red blood cell specialization. Cell differentiation generally involves different gene expression, rather than a different inherited genome for each function. Biological development must coordinate growth, position, interactions and specialization: increasing the number of biological cells is only one component. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell, chapter 21 (2002): construction of multicellular bodies.
A motivating experiment: select for a property of groups. Ratcliff, Denison, Borrello and Travisano's 2012 study began with Saccharomyces cerevisiae growing as unicellular organisms. Daily transfer favored rapidly settling material. Their initial protocol allowed a 10mL culture to settle for 45min, discarded the upper 9.9mL, and transferred the bottom 100μL into fresh medium. After seven days, they instead sampled 1.5mL and centrifuged it at 100 times gravitational acceleration for 10s, again transferring the bottom 100μL. Faster settling made it more likely that a lineage would seed tomorrow's culture; the procedure selected a physical outcome without specifying how it must arise. These protocol details are explicitly recounted in Gulli, Herron and Ratcliff, Evolution of altruistic cooperation among nascent multicellular organisms (2019): original settling protocol.
The primary 2012 paper reports snowflake yeast dominating all ten initial replicate populations by 60 transfers. To distinguish the joining of previously separate biological cells from clonal development, the researchers enzymatically separated clusters and followed individual biological cells for 16h: attached descendants rebuilt clusters, with no such joining observed. Staining localized attachments at bud scars. Time-lapse observations also showed clusters releasing smaller multicellular offspring, called propagules; small clusters grew before they reproduced. See Ratcliff et al., Experimental evolution of multicellularity (2012), figures 1–3: emergence, development and reproduction.
The experimental comparisons ask different questions. Competition with the unicellular organism ancestor showed a reported 34% advantage under settling selection. Without settling selection, the estimated 10% disadvantage had a one-sided P=0.06, so it was suggestive rather than decisive evidence of a cost. Separately, three populations derived from one evolved isolate retained the cluster form through 35 transfers without settling selection. That persistence tests stability, not whether clusters always outperform single biological cells. See Ratcliff et al. (2012), figure 2 and the cluster-formation results cited above.
A 2015 follow-up added a causal test: deleting the gene ACE2 in the unicellular organism ancestor produced snowflake yeast, while supplying functional ACE2 to one evolved isolate restored separation into single biological cells. Sequence changes altering the encoded protein were found in ACE2 in five of ten examined lineages; the study did not identify that same sequence-change mechanism in every lineage. The deletion and restoration comparisons support a mechanism beyond the observation that clusters settle quickly. See Ratcliff, Fankhauser, Rogers, Greig and Travisano, Origins of multicellular evolvability in snowflake yeast (2015), figure 1.
What follows, and what does not. The evidence supports inherited cluster formation, clonal development and a reproducing collective whose properties can respond to natural selection. Calling these simple multicellular organisms recognizes those demonstrated properties; it does not turn every clump into an animal or establish animal-like cell differentiation. This laboratory route also does not reconstruct the actual ancestry of animals or show that complex developmental integration must follow. The interesting achievement is narrower and testable: a change in how descendants remain connected creates a new scale at which growth and reproductive success can be measured.

Cellular differentiation

Words: 1k Articles: 20
Developmental integration OurBigBook.com
Cell differentiation is the acquisition of a specialized cell state, involving changes in structure, function and gene expression. A neuron and a red blood cell illustrate different outcomes: signaling and oxygen transport. Different functions usually do not require a different inherited DNA sequence for each function. The shared-genome principle concerns nucleated precursors and most nucleated descendants: a mature mammalian red blood cell has lost its cell nucleus and mitochondria, so it is not an example of two mature biological cells both retaining the same nuclear DNA. [ref]
The puzzle is how a shared set of genes supports different jobs. Gene expression changes which RNA and proteins a biological cell produces, and in what amounts. A transcription factor can regulate several genes; different combinations of regulators can interpret the same signal differently. Stable regulatory patterns can persist through the cell cycle, providing memory of developmental history. Specialization therefore usually changes use of inherited information, rather than replacing the genome wholesale. [ref]
A small counting example separates sequence from activity. Suppose the same three toy genes are present in every nucleated biological cell, and independently assign each gene an on/off state. There are 23=8 possible expression profiles with no DNA replacement. The figure shows only two: H and A on versus H and B on. Real gene expression is graded and coordinated, so this count illustrates possible descriptions, not a prediction of eight actual cell types. The interesting consequence is that a stable cell state can depend on how a shared record is used.
Stem cells combine self-renewal with production of differentiated descendants; progenitor cells generally have more restricted developmental options and renewal capacity. Neither term means that a biological cell must give rise to every cell type. Terminal differentiation denotes advanced specialization within a lineage, often accompanied by exit from the cell cycle. Cell fate concerns the developmental outcome of a biological cell or its descendants, whereas cell state describes its current properties. A temporary change in gene expression after signal transduction is not, by itself, proof of a new cell fate. [ref]

Can a specialized donor still supply information for other cell types? The 1962 The Developmental Capacity of Nuclei taken from Intestinal Epithelium Cells of Feeding Tadpoles by J. B. Gurdon used mid-intestinal cells of feeding Xenopus laevis tadpoles. Donors were recognized by columnar shape, basal cell nuclei and a striated absorptive border. Dissociation used Versene solution; this made individual donors accessible. The readout was subsequent development, including normal feeding tadpoles, rather than just division or survival. [ref]
Apparatus and intervention. A microscope with ultraviolet illumination directed light at the surface region containing the unfertilized ovum's chromosomes, inactivating the recipient nuclear contribution. Irradiation also softened the surrounding jelly so a glass micropipette could enter. The pipette delivered donor nuclear material into recipient cytoplasm. In the technique's validation, fertilizing irradiated eggs produced haploid embryos, supporting destruction of the maternal nuclear contribution. This is inactivation, not a diagram of surgically removing an intact cell nucleus. [ref]
Controls ask different questions. Donor cell nuclei carried a marker absent from recipients; post-blastula embryos carried that marker. The marker distinguished diploid donors with one nucleolus per cell nucleus from the usual two-nucleolus recipient background, rather than mistaking one nucleolus for proof of a haploid state. [ref] Interspersed transfers of blastula or later embryonic cell nuclei checked variable recipient quality. A positive embryonic-donor control thus tests the preparation, while the marker tests donor origin. Abnormal development remained common. Neither control establishes universal transfer competence. [ref]
Inference and limits. A successful marked donor demonstrates that specialization had not irreversibly removed all information required to build other cell types. Failure is less decisive: transfer damage, recipient condition and incomplete cellular reprogramming can also prevent development. The marker identifies nuclear origin; it is not a complete DNA sequence comparison. The experiment tests donor nuclear potential in an altered cytoplasm, rather than normal donor cell fate, a universal capacity of every donor, or development from a mature mammalian red blood cell. A prokaryote is an organism whose biological cells lack a membrane-bounded cell nucleus. The DNA of its chromosomes occupies a nucleoid within the cytoplasm. Bacteria and archaea have this cellular organization. Both contain ribosomes that perform biological translation and a selective cell membrane; neither is a bag of unorganized chemicals. The word prokaryotic describes organization, not a single exclusive clade. Yeast

Words: 1k Articles: 4
Fungus OurBigBook.com
Yeasts are fungi whose growth is predominantly unicellular, commonly through budding or division into two cells. The word describes a growth form across different fungal lineages, rather than one species or an exclusive clade; some fungi can switch between unicellular and filamentous growth. Thus Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a yeast, but yeast is not another name for that one species. See Kurtzman, Fell and Boekhout, The Yeasts, chapter 1 (2011): definition and classification.
A yeast cell has eukaryotic organization, including a cell nucleus, mitochondria and a cell wall. Small size and a unicellular lifestyle do not make it a member of bacteria. These compartments let an experimentally accessible biological cell retain much of the organization shared with other eukaryotes. See Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 24.1: fungal cells and yeast growth.
A motivating experiment: can fermentation continue without intact yeast cells? In 1897, Eduard Buchner ground brewers' yeast with quartz sand and diatomite, added water, and pressed out a liquid extract. Adding concentrated cane sugar produced carbon dioxide after 15–60 minutes. A cell-retaining filter still yielded an active filtrate: with sterilized cane sugar in an icebox, fermentation began after about a day. Saturated lactose gave no observed fermentation. These are reported observations, not invented control results. See Buchner, Alcoholic fermentation without yeast cells (1897), translated excerpt hosted by Carmen Giunta.
The filtration comparison addresses the objection that surviving intact yeast cells caused the activity. The lactose comparison tests substrate dependence; it is not a no-sugar blank, nor a comparison at matched sugar concentrations. A proposed modern follow-up would also compare the same extract with and without added sugar, accounting for material already in the extract. That is an experimental suggestion, not a result attributed to Buchner. His report specifies brewers' yeast; it does not identify the preparation here as a pure Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain.
Heat treatment asks a different question: does destroying activity in the extract stop fermentation? Buchner's preliminary report described weak or absent activity after heating and filtration, rather than one clean universal threshold. Harden's 1914 account of the subsequent work summarized loss of activity on heating the juice to 50∘C. Harden also described requirements for both enzymes and coenzymes. Cell-free therefore means neither chemical-free nor a preparation containing one purified enzyme. See Arthur Harden, Alcoholic Fermentation (1914), chapters II and VI: cell-free activity and the fermenting complex.
The inference is that extracted cellular machinery can sustain these chemical reactions without an intact living biological cell. This does not show that the extract reproduces, grows or maintains itself like a yeast. Moreover, gas evolution alone does not identify ethanol: cellular respiration can also release carbon dioxide. Product identification is a separate measurement; Harden's account describes both ethanol and carbon dioxide as products of cell-free alcoholic fermentation. The beautiful separation is between the organism that produces and maintains the machinery and the machinery that catalyzes a particular transformation.

A haploid strain has one set of chromosomes, so a changed allele is not accompanied by a second copy at that genetic locus. But a mutation need not produce an observable phenotype under every condition. Germination assay (G(t)=g(t)/N)
Words: 277
Germination OurBigBook.com
A specified test for germination, recording the endpoint, conditions and observation window. For a proposed red/far-red light test, use randomization to allocate one seed lot to darkness, red, red then far-red, and red then far-red then red. Match hydration, temperature, handling and elapsed time before counting; use sham waiting periods to equalize schedules. Use dish replication, retain the dark scientific control, and record radicle emergence using an observation procedure that does not itself provide an uncontrolled light treatment. This is a proposed design, not a claim about the 1952 paper's randomization or replication.
As an original hypothetical counting example, begin with N=40 seeds in one dish. Suppose 12 first meet the radicle endpoint by 24 hours, eight additional seeds by 48 hours, and four additional seeds by 72 hours. These invented event counts give
g(24)=12,g(48)=12+8=20,g(72)=12+8+4=24,
(3)
and cumulative sample proportions G(24)=0.30, G(48)=0.50 and G(72)=0.60. The 48-hour increment is 8/40=0.20, not 0.50; the latter includes earlier events. Counting the same emerged radicle again at each inspection would inflate the total. These numbers illustrate bookkeeping, not biological rates or treatment effects.
With a fixed denominator and irreversible classification of the first endpoint event, the set counted at an earlier inspection is a subset of the set counted later. Hence 0≤G(t)≤1 and G(t) cannot decrease. This short proof applies to cumulative germination counts even if a later seedling dies. A count of currently surviving seedlings has a different definition and can decrease.
For treatment comparisons, the dish may be the experimental unit: seeds in the same dish share conditions. Forty seeds in one dish do not supply forty independent dish replicates. Report the sample size, replication, endpoint time and denominator; a sample proportion by a deadline measures this assay's outcome, not an unconditional fraction with seed viability.
