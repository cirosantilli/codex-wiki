∮C​E⋅dl=−dtd​∫Σ​B⋅dS,∇×E=−∂t∂B​.

The second equation is the differential Maxwell equation, related to the first by Stokes theorem. For a moving wire, the relevant motional electromotive force is E=∮(E+vwire​×B)⋅dl=−dΦB​/dt. Thus a static magnetic field can induce current in a moving circuit even when ∇×E=0.
For translational magnetic damping of a square wire loop, while the upper horizontal leg remains in the field, a downward speed v reduces the flux at rate Bav. Hence ∣E∣=Bav, ∣I∣=Bav/R, and the upward magnetic braking force is B2a2v/R. With negligible electrical self-inductance,
mv˙=mg−RB2a2​v,vt​=B2a2mgR​,v(t)=vt​(1−e−t/τt​),τt​=B2a2mR​.​

τt​=B216ρm​ρe​​=2.403072ms,t95​=τt​log20=7.20ms.​

These use the printed copper values and B=1T. The wire dimensions cancel. The terminal-speed approximation is restricted to the interval before the upper leg leaves the field, as assumed in the question.
For rotational magnetic damping of a square wire loop, consider rotation about the central x axis in a uniform field, the flux is Ba2cosθ. The induced current produces torque −B2a4θ˙sin2θ/R. A thin square loop has moment of inertia Ix​=ma2/6: the two legs parallel to x contribute ma2/8, and the other two ma2/24. Averaging sin2θ over a rotation gives
Ix​ω˙=−2RB2a4​ω,τrot,ω​=B2a42Ix​R​=3τt​​=0.801024ms.​

This distinction, and the assumed separation between rotation period and slow decay, are essential. The electrical and mechanical time scales in magnetic braking must be separated: electrical self-inductance is neglected in both calculations.
