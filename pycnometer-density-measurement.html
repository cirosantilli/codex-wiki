Pycnometer density measurement

 ... Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Intensive and extensive thermodynamic quantities Intensive quantity Normalizing extensive quantities
OurBigBook.com Words: 266
A pycnometer makes the distinction between mass and mass density experimentally tangible. NIST's 1994 report describes a glass vessel with filling capillaries, wire plugs and a covered overflow bowl in a controlled water bath. The operators soaked a pure-water filling to establish the bath temperature, dried the outside, then left it overnight in the balance enclosure to reach thermal equilibrium before weighing. Empty and full force-restoration balance readings, together with known water mass density, calibrated its capacity at the bath temperature. A tin-oxide coating suppressed stray electric charge; an electrometer detected none during use. The authors distinguish bath and weighing temperatures and correct for buoyancy and changes of displacement volume. Capacity and water-reference uncertainties carry into later liquid mass density determinations. Their pycnometer data were not yet analyzed, so this account provides no measured density or achieved precision.[ref]
The ideal accounting behind this measurement calibration can be derived without numerical observations. Let m0​,mw​,mx​ denote corrected masses of the empty vessel, water-filled vessel and sample-filled vessel. At the same filling temperature, with known reference water mass density ρw​ and reproducibly filled volume Vc​,
Vc​=ρw​mw​−m0​​,ρx​=Vc​mx​−m0​​=ρw​mw​−m0​mx​−m0​​.
(85)
The empty-vessel subtraction removes vessel mass; the water filling sets volume. Doubling Vc​ for the same uniform sample doubles the sample mass increment but preserves ρx​. Uncorrected balance indications, trapped bubbles, incomplete filling or unequal filling temperatures break the simple identification with corrected mass and a common Vc​. This explains why replication is a statement about comparable states rather than arbitrary instrument readings.
