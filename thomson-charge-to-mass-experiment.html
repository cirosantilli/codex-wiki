Thomson charge-to-mass experiment (e/m=v/(Ba​r))

 ... Branches of physics Electromagnetism Relativistic electromagnetism Electromagnetic four-potential Electromagnetic field tensor Lorentz force
OurBigBook.com Words: 692
The Thomson charge-to-mass experiment infers the magnitude of an electron's charge-to-mass ratio by combining electric and magnetic deflection of cathode rays. The central idea is to determine speed separately from magnetic curvature: a known speed turns the observed gyroradius into a measurement of e/m.
In 1897, J. J. Thomson used two grounded slits in an evacuated glass tube, then aluminium plates 5cm long, 2cm wide and 1.5cm apart. A battery supplied their electric potential difference. Deflected cathode rays produced a phosphorescence patch on the glass; an external scale and movable luminous pointer recorded its position in darkness. Greater evacuation reduced gas electrical conductivity that screened the electric field; reversing plate polarity reversed deflection. External coils supplied the magnetic field, calibrated by induction against larger coils. He adjusted equal electric and magnetic deflections. [ref]
His comparison included air, hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and aluminium versus platinum cathodes, supporting a common carrier. He explicitly noted uncorrected magnetic bending outside the plates. His tables give m/e, the reciprocal of the magnitude used here. The inferred ratio alone could not separate electric charge from mass. [ref]
To connect this readout with the Lorentz force, derive a simple nonrelativistic model in modern SI notation. Let the uniform deflecting region have length ℓ, followed by a field-free drift of length L. Assume a narrow speed distribution, small deflection angles, negligible collisions, and matching electric and magnetic field profiles. For an electron with speed v, the electric acceleration has magnitude eE/m, the transit time is approximately ℓ/v, and the subsequent drift adds exit transverse velocity times L/v. Thus the displacement magnitudes measured separately with only electric field or only magnetic field are

This is an ideal derivation of the comparison principle, not a numerical reanalysis of the historical tube. Different exterior magnetic field profiles invalidate cancellation of the common geometrical factor. The comparison determines e/m through deflection and the known apparatus dimensions; it does not require a downstream semicircle.
For a second, deliberately different teaching geometry, combine a velocity selector with a separate region containing only a uniform magnetic field Ba​ perpendicular to the selected velocity. Here Bs​ belongs to the velocity selector, while Ba​ belongs to the analyzer: they need not be equal. The magnetic Lorentz force supplies the centripetal acceleration, so
rmv2​=evBa​,me​=Ba​rv​=Bs​Ba​rE​.
(26)
If the analyzer turns the beam through a semicircle before it hits a screen in the entry plane, the separation D between entrance and impact is the diameter: r=D/2. For a shorter observed arc, the screen displacement is not generally a radius; fit the appropriate circle geometry. This two-stage velocity selector and curvature apparatus is an original ideal teaching model, not a claim that Thomson's Fig. 2 used a separate selector and semicircular analyzer.
For a worked example, stipulate E=2.00×103Vm−1, Bs​=1.00×10−3T, Ba​=2.00×10−4T, and D=0.1136m. The selected speed and inferred charge-to-mass ratio magnitude are
v=1.00×10−32.00×103​=2.00×106ms−1,r=0.0568m,

The SI units provide an independent check: (V/m)/T=m/s and (m/s)/(Tm)=C/kg, because T=kg/(Cs). The charge-to-mass ratio of the electron is negative; this calculation gives its magnitude. The bending direction gives the sign of the electric charge once the magnetic field direction is known. Scaling both e and m by the same positive factor preserves every displayed trajectory, so an independent electric charge measurement is required to determine mass separately.
The curvature formula assumes nonrelativistic momentum p=mv. At higher speeds, the relativistic cyclotron orbit gives p=γmv=eBa​r, hence e/m=γv/(Ba​r), where γ is the Lorentz factor; the ideal straight-through selection v=E/Bs​ still follows from vanishing Lorentz force. The stipulated speed is about 0.0067 times the speed of light, making γ−1 about 2.2×10−5. For this example the nonrelativistic approximation is adequate, but no massive particle can be selected at E/Bs​≥c. Finite slit widths, collisions with residual gas, uncertain magnetic field calibration, and fields outside the ideal boundaries limit a real inference; repeat at several selected speeds and analyzer strengths to test consistency of e/m rather than trusting one spot position.
