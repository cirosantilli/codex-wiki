The Milky Way has a thin stellar galactic disk, radius roughly 15–20kpc and scale height about 0.3kpc, containing gas, dust, young and old stars, spiral arms and most current star formation. Its stellar mass is a few 1010M⊙​. A thicker, older galactic disk has scale height around 1kpc and stellar mass of order 1010M⊙​, with little cold gas.
The central galactic bulge and bar extend a few thousand parsecs and contain about 1010M⊙​, largely older stars. The central supermassive black hole has mass about 4×106M⊙​ and is negligible in the total galactic budget. A diffuse old metal-poor stellar halo, extending tens to over 100kpc, contains globular clusters and roughly 109M⊙​ in stars. An extended dark matter halo reaches roughly 200kpc and dominates the total mass, of order 1012M⊙​. These are approximate component scales; the gaseous galactic halo also extends beyond the stellar galactic disk.

a OurBigBook.com
A blackbody is an ideal body that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation, at every frequency and direction. Its absorptivity is one, so Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation makes its equilibrium emissivity one as well. At a specified temperature its thermal spectrum is universal: it depends on temperature, not material or shape. This does not mean it looks black when hot; it can glow brightly while remaining a perfect absorber.
A small hole in a nearly isothermal opaque cavity approximates a blackbody, because radiation entering the hole undergoes many interactions before it could escape. In thermal equilibrium the cavity contains an approximately isotropic Planck spectrum. The emitted radiance follows Planck's law, its integrated outward radiant exitance follows the Stefan–Boltzmann law, and its wavelength peak follows Wien's displacement law. A real surface can have wavelength- and direction-dependent emissivity below this ideal limit.

Compton scattering transfers energy and momentum from a photon to an electron. For an initially stationary, approximately free electron, a photon scattered through a nonzero angle generally has lower frequency and longer wavelength; the recoiling electron carries the difference. The effect demonstrates the photon momentum pγ​=hν/c=h/λ, beyond a purely elastic classical scattering description.
The middle panel of the figure in solution shows the incident photon along +x, the outgoing photon at angle θ above that direction, and the recoiling electron at deflection angle ϕ below it. Each arrow labels its particle and momentum; the initial electron is at rest at the interaction point. The scattered photon and electron have opposite transverse momentum components, while their forward components sum to the incident photon momentum.

Inside the infinite square well, V=0, and the time-independent Schrödinger equation is

The linear ramp state in an infinite square well has a boundary subtlety: the limiting value at L is 3/L​, rather than zero. The function is still a normalized element of the well's Hilbert space L2(0,L), so position measurement and energy-projection probabilities are meaningful. The two probabilities concern different quantum measurements, so there is no requirement that they match or that one bound the other. The spatial interval [0,L/3] contains little of the state: its probability density is smallest near zero and increases towards L. By contrast, the positive ground state extends across the whole well. It has substantial same-sign overlap with the positive linear wavefunction, with no cancellation between lobes in the overlap integral. The right panel of the figure in solution illustrates these two amplitudes and marks the small position interval.
Consequently a large global ground-state probability can coexist with a small probability of finding the particle in the left third. This is not because the ground state is spatially confined to that third; a ground-state particle can be found throughout the well. In operator language the two events are projections onto a spatial subspace and onto one energy eigenstate, respectively.
