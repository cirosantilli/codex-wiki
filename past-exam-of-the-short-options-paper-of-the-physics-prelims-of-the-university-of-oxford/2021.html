c OurBigBook.com
Transit photometry. A planet crossing the stellar disc produces a periodic reduction in flux, approximately δ=(Rp​/R∗​)2 for a small, dark planet away from the limb. The orbital period and stellar radius allow an inferred planetary radius, but not its mass by this method alone. The geometric geometric transit probability is roughly (R∗​+Rp​)/a for a circular orbit, so the method strongly favours close orbits and large planets. Small signals compete with instrumental noise and stellar activity; eclipsing stellar binaries and blended sources can mimic a planetary exoplanet transit, and long orbital periods require long observing baselines.
Doppler spectroscopy. Periodic shifts of stellar spectral lines reveal the stellar radial velocity caused by motion around the centre of mass. For a light companion, K∝Mp​siniP−1/3M∗−2/3​/1−e2​, favouring massive, short-period companions and low-mass host stars. Unknown orbital inclination gives a minimum mass; stellar activity, line broadening, and instrumental calibration limit precision. Long orbital periods require years of measurements.
Direct imaging. Spatially resolved reflected light or thermal emission separates a planet from its host. The large star–planet brightness contrast and small angular separation demand starlight suppression and precise correction of optical aberrations. The method favours wide orbits around nearby stars, and young, hot, massive planets with stronger thermal emission. Translating brightness to mass depends on planetary cooling models and age; it is difficult to image an Earth analogue close to a bright star.

A blackbody absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation, at every frequency, direction and polarization: its absorptivity is one. In thermal equilibrium, Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation makes its emissivity one as well. Its emitted spectrum depends only on its absolute temperature, through Planck's law, rather than on the material of its surface.
A small aperture in a large isothermal cavity approximates a blackbody because radiation entering it undergoes many reflections and is very unlikely to escape before absorption. The equilibrium radiation field is isotropic and unpolarized. The total emitted radiant exitance is σT4 by the Stefan–Boltzmann law; the peak of the wavelength spectrum shifts to shorter wavelengths as temperature increases according to Wien's displacement law. An ideal absorber can therefore glow brightly when hot: “black” describes absorption, not the absence of thermal emission.

In the photoelectric effect, incident light ejects electrons from a material when each absorbed photon supplies sufficient energy to overcome its work function W. Conservation of energy gives the maximum emitted kinetic energy,
Kmax​=hν−W,ν≥ν0​=W/h.​
(57)
At fixed frequency above threshold, increasing light intensity primarily increases the electron emission rate; it does not increase Kmax​ in the single-photon regime. Emission occurs without the long accumulation delay predicted for weak classical illumination.
Classical electromagnetism distributes incident energy continuously. By itself, that picture suggests that sufficiently intense or sufficiently long illumination could eject electrons at any frequency, and that emitted electron energies should increase with light intensity. It does not account for the observed threshold frequency and the linear dependence of Kmax​ on frequency with slope h. Quantized photon absorption accounts for all three features.
