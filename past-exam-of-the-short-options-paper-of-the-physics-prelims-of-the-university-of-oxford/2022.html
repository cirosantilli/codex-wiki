Assume spherical stars, central eclipses, uniform disc surface brightness, and negligible third light. Let s denote the smaller star and ℓ the larger one, with r=Rs​/Rℓ​≤1 and ℓs​=Ls​/Lℓ​. Normalize the out-of-eclipse flux to one, and denote the deep and shallow fractional losses by D and d. The graph gives approximately Fout​≃0.98, Fdeep​≃0.57, Fshallow​≃0.90, so D≃0.42, d≃0.08; the last digits are graphical estimates.
At total occultation of the smaller star, the lost flux is Ls​/(Ls​+Lℓ​). At its central transit across the larger star, the lost flux is r2Lℓ​/(Ls​+Lℓ​). There are two identifications:
  • Smaller star hidden at the deep eclipse. Then D=ℓs​/(1+ℓs​) and d=r2/(1+ℓs​), giving ℓs​=D/(1−D) and r=d/(1−D)​. Thus Rs​/Rℓ​≃0.37,Ls​/Lℓ​≃0.72​. The smaller star has the higher surface brightness; the deep eclipse hides it and the shallow eclipse is its transit.
  • Smaller star hidden at the shallow eclipse. Then d=ℓs​/(1+ℓs​) and D=r2/(1+ℓs​), giving ℓs​=d/(1−d) and r=D/(1−d)​. Thus Rs​/Rℓ​≃0.67,Ls​/Lℓ​≃0.089​. The smaller star has the lower surface brightness; the deep eclipse is its transit across the hotter, larger star.
These are ratios of luminosities in the observed band. Treating them as bolometric luminosities requires similar band corrections or an explicitly bolometric light curve. Limb darkening and finite exposure times limit the precision of these elementary estimates.

  • A main-sequence star is supported by hydrostatic equilibrium and powered predominantly by hydrogen nuclear fusion in its core. More massive main-sequence stars are generally hotter and more luminous and consume their fuel faster.
  • On the red giant branch, core hydrogen is exhausted. Hydrogen-shell nuclear fusion surrounds an inert helium core; the envelope expands and cools while the luminosity rises. In low-mass stars the contracting helium core becomes supported by electron degeneracy pressure before helium ignition.
  • A horizontal-branch star has core helium nuclear fusion and usually hydrogen-shell nuclear fusion. Its luminosity varies comparatively little across the branch, while envelope mass produces a wide range of effective temperatures. It follows helium ignition in a low-mass red giant.
  • A white dwarf is a compact exposed remnant, typically with a carbon-oxygen interior after ordinary low- or intermediate-mass stellar evolution. Electron degeneracy pressure supports it; sustained core nuclear fusion has ended. It can be hot yet faint because its radius is small, and it moves down its cooling sequence as it loses stored thermal energy.

Let r be radial position, F=L/(4πr2) the outward radiative energy flux, and Pr​=aT4/3 the radiation pressure. A thin layer absorbs a fraction κρdr of an incident directed beam. Since a photon carries momentum E/c, the outward force per unit volume deposited by the radiation is κρF/c. Balance it against the radiation pressure gradient:
drdPr​​=−cκρ​F=−4πr2cκρL​.
(20)
Substituting dPr​/dr=(4a/3)T3dT/dr yields the radiative diffusion equation
L=−4πr23κρ4ac​T3drdT​.​
(21)
The sign expresses outward energy flux down the temperature gradient. The approximation assumes optically thick material and near local thermodynamic equilibrium, with an appropriate mean opacity.
The printed attenuation law describes the loss from an incident beam; it is not generally the radial derivative of the net luminosity, since absorption is accompanied by emission. For a concrete counterexample, in a steady envelope without local energy generation the net luminosity is constant even though κρ>0 and dT/dr<0. Applying dL/dr=−κρL to that net luminosity would contradict energy conservation. The local momentum deposition argument supplies the physically correct derivation.

The angle estimates the largest transverse region at decoupling that could have established causal contact after the big bang in the assumed decelerating model. The cosmic microwave background has nearly the same temperature in directions separated by much larger angles. This is the horizon problem: those regions have no shared causal history in that simple model, yet their thermodynamic conditions are strikingly alike. The particle horizon should be distinguished from the smaller sound horizon, which controls acoustic propagation in the primordial fluid.
An early epoch dominated by a sufficiently large positive effective cosmological constant gives R¨>0 and approximately R∝eHt. During this cosmic inflation, the comoving Hubble length c/(RH) shrinks. A patch that was initially in causal contact can expand to contain the whole presently observable cosmic microwave background surface, while expansion suppresses spatial curvature. A finite period of cosmic inflation followed by reheating and the usual hot expansion therefore explains the uniformity without superluminal local signals. An eternally large unchanged cosmological constant would prevent the later matter-dominated history; an exit from cosmic inflation and reheating are required. Sufficient duration and a suitable pre-inflationary causal patch are part of this explanation.

In the double-slit experiment, each photon is detected as a localized event carrying energy hν, while repeated events build the interference pattern of coherent probability amplitudes from the two slits. Acquiring information that distinguishes the paths suppresses the interference; splitting the classical intensity into two incoherent alternatives would not reproduce the fringes.
Put one plate before each slit at normal incidence, with the same known refractive index n and illumination of vacuum wavelength λ. Relative to the plate-free reference, their thickness difference Δd=d1​−d2​ produces an optical path difference (n−1)Δd and a phase shift
Δϕ=λ2π(n−1)Δd​.
(36)
Observe the displacement of the full interference fringe pattern, or scan a controllable reference phase and estimate Δϕ. This compares the plates directly; a detectable phase displacement establishes unequal thickness under the common-material assumption. For fringe spacing λD/b, the displacement is −D(n−1)Δd/b, where b is slit separation and D the screen distance, with sign set by the slit labeling.
A differential phase measurement can resolve a small difference sensitively and avoid determining two large absolute thicknesses. It does not guarantee a universally faster or more photon-efficient test: noise, coherence, alignment, and required accuracy matter. Moreover a null phase shift at one wavelength only implies
Δd=mλ/(n−1),m∈Z,​
(37)
so equality requires a prior bound, a broadband or multiple-wavelength measurement, or continuously counting interference fringes as the plates are introduced. If the refractive indices differ, this apparatus measures optical thickness difference rather than geometrical thickness difference.

Explicitly, p1​−p2​=cosϕ and p1(π/2)​−p2(π/2)​=cos(ϕ+π/2)=−sinϕ. Hence the ordered pair determines ϕ modulo 2π, removing the sign ambiguity of a cosine alone.
It still leaves the integer fringe order undetermined:
d=2π(n−1)λ​(ϕmod2π​+2πm),m∈Z,d≥0.​
(44)
Thus the thickness remains periodic with period λ/(n−1). A known thickness interval shorter than this period, a suitable multiple-wavelength measurement, or continuous phase tracking supplies the missing information. If n is unknown, a single wavelength only determines the excess optical thickness (n−1)d modulo λ.

A Stern-Gerlach experiment uses a nonuniform magnetic field to exert force Fz​≃μz​∂z​B on a magnetic dipole. The two resolved beams reveal discrete projections of spin, rather than a continuum of classical dipole orientations. For the ground-state silver chemical atom, filled shells contribute no net angular momentum and the outer 5s electron has orbital angular momentum zero, leaving an effective spin-1/2 magnetic moment. This provides evidence for the electron's two-valued intrinsic spin.
Neutral silver chemical atoms can pass through the apparatus without the large charge-dependent Lorentz force that would deflect and broaden a free-electron beam. Neutrality isolates the small magnetic-gradient force far more cleanly. The later parts use an ideal spin filter model; a literal free-electron apparatus requires additional control of charge forces.

A pure bipartite quantum state is entangled if it cannot be written as a tensor product ∣a⟩⊗∣b⟩. More generally a mixed quantum state is entangled if its density matrix cannot be expressed as a convex mixture of product density matrices. Two applications are quantum teleportation, which transfers an unknown qubit using a shared entangled pair plus classical communication, and superdense coding, which uses a shared entangled pair and transmission of one qubit to communicate two classical bits. Neither permits faster-than-light communication.

∣α∣2(∣γ∣2+∣δ∣2)=∣γ∣2(∣α∣2+∣β∣2),∣αδ∣=∣βγ∣.​

Expand a normalized product state as
(a∣0⟩+b∣1⟩)⊗(c∣0⟩+d∣1⟩)=ac∣00⟩+ad∣01⟩+bc∣10⟩+bd∣11⟩.

Conversely the nonzero coefficient matrix C=(αγ​βδ​) has determinant zero precisely when it has rank one, so the condition also suffices for a pure two-qubit state to factor.

p(i,j)=⟨a∣Ei​∣a⟩⟨b∣Fj​∣b⟩=pA​(i)pB​(j).​

Alice's outcome is random. Conditional on knowing it, Bob may assign a different quantum state, but without that classical record he sees the same density matrix. For the displayed pure two-qubit state, his reduced density matrix before Alice's measurement is
ρB​=(∣α∣2+∣γ∣2α∗β+γ∗δ​αβ∗+γδ∗∣β∣2+∣δ∣2​).
(62)
After Alice's computational-basis measurement with the outcome unannounced, the weighted mixture p0​ρB∣0​+p1​ρB∣1​ is exactly this same matrix. More generally, for any local trace-preserving operation with measurement operators Ka​,
ρB′​=TrA​∑a​(Ka​⊗I)ρAB​(Ka†​⊗I)=TrA​ρAB​=ρB​,∑a​Ka†​Ka​=I.
(63)
The equality follows from the cyclic property of the partial trace for operators acting on Alice's subsystem. Bob's every local outcome probability therefore remains unchanged. This is the no-communication theorem: entanglement alone cannot send information faster than light​. Conditional state changes become usable correlations only when the classical outcomes are compared.
