Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)

 Home Chemistry Molecule Nucleotide
OurBigBook.com Words: 521
ATP is a nucleotide consisting of adenosine, itself adenine joined to ribose, carrying three phosphate groups. From the ribose outward, these groups are labelled α, β, and γ. ATP connects energy-supplying processes to energy coupling for synthesis and active transport; the same carrier can be regenerated and reused. [ref].
The net hydrolysis commonly used in energy coupling is
ATP+H2​O⟶ADP+Pi​.
(40)
Here Pi​ denotes inorganic phosphate. At buffered pH 7, this biochemical shorthand groups the appropriate protonation states into pools and leaves proton exchange with the buffer solution implicit. An explicit, charge-balanced choice is ATP4−+H2​O⟶ADP3−+HPO4​2−+H+, without implying these are the only chemical species present. See the IUBMB recommendations for biochemical thermodynamics.
The quoted textbook biochemical standard state value is approximately
ΔG∘′=−30.5kJmol−1.
(41)
This convention uses fixed pH 7, temperature 298.15K (25∘C), reference pressure 100kPa (1bar), unit reference thermodynamic activities for dissolved reactants and reaction products, and water thermodynamic activity approximated by one. The prime indicates the fixed-pH biochemical convention. See OpenStax Biology 2e, 6.4: standard ATP hydrolysis value and Biology 2e, 6.2: biochemical standard conditions. This is a reference value, rather than a universal value inside a biological cell; precise tables must also specify chemical solution conditions and any magnesium ion binding convention. Different reference conventions and data treatments give different tabulated values, as discussed in Li et al., 2010: a database of thermodynamic quantities for biochemical reactions.
An exergonic reaction has a negative change in Gibbs free energy, so the overall ATP hydrolysis is favourable under those reference conditions. Breaking a chemical bond by itself costs energy; forming new chemical bonds can release energy. The net change compares all reactants with all reaction products, including their interactions with water and the entropy contribution. Calling the phosphate connection a "high-energy bond" describes a large favourable change in Gibbs free energy on hydrolysis, rather than energy released by bond breaking alone. See 7.5 Strengths of Ionic and Covalent Bonds and Cooper, The Cell: ATP and metabolic energy.
Composition changes the driving force. At the same fixed pH, temperature, pressure, and binding convention, write the molar reaction change as
ΔG=ΔG∘′+RTlnQ,Q=aATP​aADP​aPi​​​.
(42)
Here R is the molar gas constant, T the absolute temperature, and a a dimensionless thermodynamic activity for a biochemical pool. Constant water thermodynamic activity is absorbed into the reference term; fixed pH accounts for buffered protons. The shorthand ΔG means transformed molar reaction Gibbs free energy change, Δr​G′, rather than a finite sample's total change. The natural logarithm acts on a dimensionless reaction quotient, never on concentrations with units. See the IUBMB biochemical thermodynamics recommendations and the general equilibrium constant relation.
For a stipulated ideal dilute chemical solution, let ai​=[i]/c∘, with c∘=1molL−1. Choose [ATP]=5mmolL−1, [ADP]=0.5mmolL−1, and [Pi​]=1mmolL−1, ignoring activity coefficient corrections and magnesium binding. These are illustrative inputs, not a claim about a particular biological cell. Then
Q=0.005(0.0005)(0.001)​=10−4,RT=(0.008314462618)(298.15)=2.47895703kJmol−1,
(43)
and
ΔG=−30.5+2.47895703ln(10−4)≈−53.332kJmol−1.
(44)
Increasing Q tenfold raises ΔG by RTln10≈5.708kJmol−1 at this temperature. Thus the ATP to ADP ratio and available inorganic phosphate matter, even with the same standard state value. These are local molar predictions at the stipulated composition, rather than a constant total yield as an isolated sample reacts.
