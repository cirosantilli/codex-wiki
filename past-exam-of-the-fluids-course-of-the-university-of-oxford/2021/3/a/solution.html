Let H be the typical thickness, ℓr​ the radial variation length, U the horizontal speed, and W the vertical speed. For a thin smooth profile, ε=H/ℓr​≪1. The axisymmetric continuity equation is
r1​∂r​(rvr​)+∂z​vz​=0.
(30)
Away from singular boundary regions, balancing its terms gives U/ℓr​∼W/H, hence W/U∼ε≪1​. Near r=0, regularity gives vr​=O(r) and the same conclusion follows without a divergent vr​/r term.
Vertical viscous derivatives dominate horizontal ones by ε−2. The ratio of convective acceleration to vertical viscous acceleration is
νU/H2U2/ℓr​​=νℓr​UH2​.
(31)
For thickness-evolution time T, the local inertial ratio is H2/(νT). When both ratios are small, lubrication theory reduces the Navier-Stokes equation to
0≈μ∂z2​v−∇p−ρgz.​
(32)
Thinness must therefore accompany a slow enough motion; it is not a justification for dropping arbitrarily rapid imposed acceleration. The small vertical velocity also makes the leading vertical balance hydrostatic balance, pz​=−ρg. The neglected normal viscous stress at the interface is smaller than the characteristic lubrication pressure by order ε2.
Use the source's curvature sign: pair​−pliquid​=−γκ. The Young–Laplace equation and atmospheric pressure p0​ thus give p(r,h,t)=p0​+γκ. Integrating the hydrostatic balance yields
p(r,z,t)=p0​+γκ(r,t)+ρg[h(r,t)−z].​
(33)
Consequently pr​=γκr​+ρghr​, independent of z at this order. The radial Stokes equation and boundary conditions are
μ∂z2​vr​=pr​,vr​(r,0,t)=0,∂z​vr​(r,h,t)=0.
(34)
The first is the no-slip boundary condition; the second is the leading stress-free boundary condition, since the atmosphere has negligible dynamic viscosity and the surface tension is constant. Integrating twice gives
vh​≈(ρghr​+γκr​)2μz(z−2h)​r.​
(35)
This is the horizontal velocity only. A smaller vertical velocity is generally required by the continuity equation.
