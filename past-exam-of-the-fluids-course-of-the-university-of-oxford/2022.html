Take x along a horizontal gap and s perpendicular to its plates, with 0<s<b. Assume a Newtonian fluid, stationary plates, a steady flow, a laminar flow and a fully developed flow away from the entrances and side edges. The no-slip boundary condition holds on both plates. Any transverse gravity is absorbed in the hydrostatic pressure; there is no imposed transverse through-flow. Translational symmetry and the no-penetration boundary condition then give u=(u(s),0,0). The Navier-Stokes equation reduces to
0=−dxdp​+ηds2d2u​,dxdp​=−LΔp​,u(0)=u(b)=0.
(1)
Twice integrating gives the plane Poiseuille flow:
ux​(s)=2ηLΔp​s(b−s),us​=uspan​=0.​
(2)
The volumetric flow rate through one gap is
Qgap​=w∫0b​ux​(s)ds=12ηLwb3Δp​.
(3)
There are n−1 internal gaps. Their total cross-sectional area is (n−1)wb, because the plate thickness is negligible, so the Darcy velocity is
q=(n−1)wb(n−1)Qgap​​=12ηb2​LΔp​,Π0​=12b2​.​
(4)
Thus the permeability of a porous medium has the dimensions of squared length. If finite plate thickness were included, the Darcy velocity averaged over the whole manifold would also contain its porosity.
Both the local acceleration and the convective acceleration vanish: ∂t​u=0 and (u⋅∇)u=ux​∂x​u=0. The dominant balance is therefore between the pressure gradient and viscous stress, with no inertia contribution in this fully developed solution. This exact cancellation does not guarantee that arbitrary large-Reynolds number realizations remain laminar flows; entrances, disturbances and turbulence can invalidate the assumptions.

Use a fixed representative volume V containing many pores. Let φ be the porosity and let q be the Darcy velocity, defined per unit total area. The conservation of mass is
dtd​∫V​φρdV=−∫∂V​ρq⋅ndS.
(5)
The solid matrix is rigid and fixed, and the liquid is fully saturating and has constant mass density, so the stored mass on the left is constant. Microscopic internal liquid-solid boundaries contribute no through-flux by the no-penetration boundary condition. Applying the divergence theorem to the external flux gives
∫V​∇⋅(ρq)dV=0.
(6)
Since the representative volume is arbitrary, the macroscopic continuity equation is
∇⋅(ρq)=0,∇⋅q=0for constant ρ.​
(7)
There is no extra factor of φ in the flux: that factor is already incorporated in the definition of the Darcy velocity. A compressible liquid or deformable matrix would require retaining the storage term.

At a fixed point beside a river, the Eulerian coordinate description measures u(x,t). Its local acceleration ∂t​u measures change at that fixed point. Following a floating parcel gives a Lagrangian trajectory X(t) satisfying X˙=u(X,t). The chain rule gives its physical material acceleration:
X¨=DtDu​=∂t​u+(u⋅∇)u.​
(17)
The extra term is the convective acceleration. For a river that narrows steadily, an observer at one fixed position measures a constant velocity, yet a parcel speeds up as it reaches the narrowing. A parcel following a steady bend also experiences a centripetal acceleration even when its speed is constant. An Eulerian calculation of the physical acceleration must include the same convective acceleration; the two descriptions do not predict different forces.

A stationary viscous wall imposes the no-slip boundary condition, u=v=0, whereas the outer inviscid flow has u=Ax. At large Reynolds number, the transition is confined to a thin viscous boundary layer, where normal velocity gradients amplify the otherwise small viscous term.
Take a horizontal scale L, an outer speed U=AL, and a boundary layer thickness δ≪L. The continuity equation gives v=O(Uδ/L). Write ϵ=δ/L and ReL​=UL/ν. The tangential Navier-Stokes equation has scales
uux​+vuy​=O(U2/L),νuyy​=O(νU/δ2),νuyy​νuxx​​=O(ϵ2).
(21)
Balancing convective acceleration with normal viscous diffusion gives ϵ=O(ReL−1/2​). The leading tangential equation and incompressible flow constraint are
uux​+vuy​=−ρ1​∂x∂p∞​​+νuyy​,ux​+vy​=0.​
(22)
Here and below ν is the kinematic viscosity, while u is the horizontal velocity.
For the normal equation, first remove the hydrostatic pressure by writing P=p+ρgy. Normal inertia and normal viscous terms are O(ρU2δ/L2), so the change of P across the layer is only O(ρU2ϵ2). Matching to the outer pressure therefore makes the leading O(ρU2) excess pressure independent of y:
P(x,y)=Pe​(x)+O(ρU2ϵ2),py​=−ρg+O(ρU2δ/L2).
(23)
The outer expression similarly has (p∞​)y​=−ρg−ρA2y. Thus the printed condition
py​=(p∞​)y​​
(24)
is a leading-order boundary layer matching statement, with the smaller normal dynamic terms discarded on both sides. It is not an exact identity at the next order. In particular, retaining the outer −ρA2y while dropping normal inertia and normal viscous terms inconsistently mixes orders. The tangential driving term is unambiguous: −(p∞​)x​/ρ=A2x.

The Hiemenz flow profile changes on η=O(1), so its boundary layer thickness is
δBL​∼Aν​​.​
(29)
A specified edge convention, such as u/(Ax)=0.99, introduces an order-one numerical multiplier requiring the profile to be solved. Scaling alone does not determine that multiplier. The result has no separate dependence on x, y, ρ or p0​ at fixed kinematic viscosity. At fixed dynamic viscosity, it is η/(ρA)​.
The viscous diffusion time across a thickness δ is δ2/ν; the imposed strain has time scale A−1. Their balance sets the thickness. Increasing kinematic viscosity spreads the layer, while increasing the imposed velocity gradient presses the layer toward the wall. The outward tangential velocity increases as Ax, so the decreasing travel time per unit distance offsets the usual downstream broadening.
For a conventional estimate of transition to turbulence, form the local horizontal Reynolds number:
Rex​=ν(Ax)x​=νAx2​.
(30)
If transition is assumed to occur at an empirical value Recrit​, then
l∼ARecrit​ν​​.​
(31)
A rough borrowed threshold of order 105–106 would give l of order 102–103ν/A​. This is a heuristic estimate, not a universal stability prediction: the accelerating outer flow and favorable pressure gradient can delay transition, and disturbance amplitudes and wall roughness matter. Simply setting Rex​∼1 locates failure of the thin-layer approximation near the stagnation point, not onset of turbulence.

Each layer has constant mass density, initially negligible velocity and hence zero vorticity. Its Euler equations for an inviscid fluid have a conservative gravitational body force, so Kelvin's circulation theorem preserves zero bulk vorticity. In a simply connected layer, the irrotational flow therefore has vi​=∇ϕi​. The incompressible flow condition gives
∇2ϕi​=0.​
(32)
This applies while the ideal layerwise solution is smooth. It allows an interfacial vortex sheet; it does not assert that the whole two-fluid flow has zero vorticity or that a smooth interface must exist for arbitrarily long times.
The rigid wall no-penetration boundary conditions are
∂z​ϕ1​(r,−h,t)=0,∂z​ϕ2​(r,h,t)=0,∂r​ϕi​(a,z,t)=0,

with boundedness and ∂r​ϕi​=0 at r=0 for regular axisymmetric fields. For a small interface displacement and small slope, linearization of the kinematic boundary condition about z=0 gives

For an inviscid flow, the force per unit volume is F=−∇p−ρgz^. The gravitational acceleration has zero curl. Using the curl product rule,
∇×(−ρ1​∇p)=−∇(ρ−1)×∇p=ρ2∇ρ×∇p​.
(44)
The supplied two-dimensional vorticity equation therefore becomes
∂t​ω+v⋅∇ω=ρ2∇ρ×∇p​.​
(45)
The out-of-plane vorticity has no stretching term in this two-dimensional setting. The source is baroclinic vorticity generation: differently directed mass density and pressure gradients generate rotation even from zero initial vorticity.
For an explicit sign check, set ωy​=∂z​u−∂x​w, with z upward. A thin smoothed interface has ρ≃ρˉ​(z−η(x,t)), with ρˉ​′>0. If P(x,t) denotes the total pressure evaluated on the interface, then within the thin layer px​+ηx​pz​≃Px​ and
ρ2(∇ρ×∇p)y​​≃ρ2ρˉ​′​Px​.
(46)
Integrating through that layer and taking its thickness to zero gives the vortex sheet strength γ=u2​−u1​ and its linear evolution:
∂t​γ=(ρ1​1​−ρ2​1​)Px​.
(47)
For a growing Fourier mode, the potentials from the preceding calculation with α replaced by k give
P−p∗​=−ρ1​+ρ2​2ρ1​ρ2​​gη,∂t​γ=−2Agηx​.​
(48)
Thus the right-hand descending slope of a light-fluid crest produces positive ωy​, while the left-hand ascending slope produces negative ωy​. Their induced velocity raises the light-fluid crest and lowers the heavy-fluid trough, increasing the slope and generating more vorticity. This is the positive feedback underlying Rayleigh-Taylor instability.
One can close this mechanism quantitatively. For η=H(t)cos(kx), the kinematic boundary conditions give γ=2H˙coth(kh)sin(kx). Combining with the sheet-generation equation gives
H¨=Agktanh(kh)H.
(49)
For kh≪1, the lids confine motion throughout both layers and horizontal redistribution is slow, yielding σ∼Agh​k. For kh≫1, motion is localized within depth k−1 on each side. Less fluid must accelerate at shorter wavelength, giving σ∼Agk​. Since kn​=nπ/b, the ideal growth rate increases with n with no short-wave cutoff.
The sharp-interface result requires kδ≪1 for the smoothed layer. When kδ becomes order one, it no longer applies. A smooth adverse mass density gradient has a local overturning time of order [gρˉ​′/ρˉ​]−1/2 rather than the unbounded sharp-interface rate. Finite molecular diffusion also relaxes small-scale mass density perturbations on a time of order (Dk2)−1, and viscosity, if included, damps small-scale velocity. Weak diffusion motivates the thin-layer picture; it does not justify extrapolating the discontinuous dispersion relation to arbitrary n.

A small Reynolds number, Re=ρUL/η≪1, means that inertia is small compared with viscous forces. It can arise from small speed, small length or large dynamic viscosity. Examples are the locomotion of a microswimmer and the slow sedimentation of a small particle in a viscous liquid.
To neglect local acceleration as well, an externally imposed time scale T must satisfy ρL2/(ηT)=L2/(νT)≪1. This holds for a steady flow or a sufficiently slowly varying quasi-steady Stokes approximation. Small Reynolds number alone is insufficient for that extra step. For an incompressible flow with constant material coefficients and uniform gravity, the Navier-Stokes equation then reduces to the Stokes equation:
0=−∇p−ρgz^+η∇2u,∇⋅u=0.
(50)
Taking the divergence and using commuting spatial derivatives gives
0=−∇2p+η∇2(∇⋅u),∇2p≃0.​
(51)
Taking the curl, which annihilates the pressure gradient and uniform gravitational force, gives
0=η∇2(∇×u),∇2ω≃0.​
(52)
These are the Harmonic pressure and vorticity in Stokes flow results.
A concrete counterexample to inferring both solely from small Reynolds number is Stokes' second problem: an oscillating plate with arbitrarily small velocity amplitude has negligible inertia, but ∂t​ω=ν∇2ω is nonzero. Its vorticity is not a harmonic function. The quasi-steady qualification is therefore necessary.

The phrase “constant terminal velocity” must here mean rapid adjustment to a force balance at each radius. A literally time-independent speed is incompatible with that balance because U∝a2. If instead an external mechanism maintained a fixed U∗​, the distinct result would be a2(z)=a02​−2Da​z/U∗​ and zdiss​=U∗​a02​/(2Da​). In particular, holding the initial terminal speed fixed doubles the predicted rise distance; it is not the freely rising shrinking-bubble model.

(67)
Equality means dissolution exactly on arrival at the surface; dissolution strictly before arrival needs a greater initial depth. This is a formal low-Reynolds number estimate. The same values predict U(a0​)≃0.033ms−1 and a radius-based Reynolds number ρw​U(a0​)a0​/ηw​≃3.3; the diameter-based value is about 6.5. Neither is ≪1. Thus the numerical input does not consistently satisfy the Stokes flow assumption initially, and the 0.82m result should not be presented as an accurate oceanic prediction. Within the stated ideal model, bubble compression, contaminants and changes in dissolved-gas concentration are also excluded.
