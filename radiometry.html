Radiometry

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics
OurBigBook.com Words: 6k Articles: 40
Radiometry measures the energy and power carried by electromagnetic radiation, including light. It asks how much radiation a source supplies, where it travels, and how much reaches a receiving surface. The measurements are physical rather than weighted by the sensitivity of the human eye: equal radiant fluxes at different wavelengths need not look equally bright. [ref].
The five quantities below answer different questions about the same radiation. The International System of Units makes the distinctions visible: a watt is a joule per second, while a steradian measures solid angle. [ref].
QuantityWhat is resolved?Units
Radiant flux ΦTotal radiation power in the specified beam or collectionW
Radiant intensity IPower per outgoing solid angleWsr−1
Radiance LPower per projected area and per solid angleWm−2sr−1
Irradiance EIncident power per receiving areaWm−2
Radiant exitance MOutward emitted power per emitting areaWm−2
Resolving wavelength or frequency adds a further “per unit interval”, as in spectral radiance. The notation is consistent with NIST's table of spectral quantities. In particular, radiant flux is power, whereas the existing astronomical radiative flux is power per area. [ref].
This bookkeeping makes an optical camera's collected signal calculable from a source's radiance and the geometry of the receiving aperture. It also lets radiation measurements from different instruments be compared. For example, NIST describes separate calibrations of spectral radiance and spectral irradiance for instruments used to observe Earth. [ref].

Optical haze

Radiometry OurBigBook.com
The fraction of transmitted light scattered sufficiently far from the forward direction under a specified measurement geometry. A transparent electrode can have high total transmittance yet blur an image if scattering is strong. State the collection angles and wavelength when comparing haze measurements.

Radiometer

Radiometry OurBigBook.com
A radiometer measures electromagnetic radiation through its received power. A radio radiometer can express that power as a noise temperature, allowing an unknown source to be compared with calibrated reference temperatures.

Noise temperature (TN​)

Words: 103 Articles: 2
Radiometer OurBigBook.com
A noise temperature expresses received or internally added noise power on an equivalent temperature scale. For one matched receiving channel in the classical thermal limit, an ideal rectangular bandwidth Δν gives P=kB​TN​Δν, with Boltzmann constant kB​. An equivalent noise temperature need not equal a component's physical temperature.

System noise temperature (Tsys​)

Words: 31
Noise temperature OurBigBook.com
The system noise temperature includes all noise contributions referred to a specified receiver input. In a simple additive model with negligible intervening losses, Tsys​=TA​+Tr​, where TA​ is the antenna temperature and Tr​ the receiver noise temperature.

Receiver noise temperature (Tr​)

Words: 30
Noise temperature OurBigBook.com
The receiver noise temperature represents noise added by the receiver as an equivalent noise temperature referred to its input. Separating it from the antenna temperature prevents internal receiver noise from being mistaken for radiation from the sky.

Two-load radiometer calibration (Ts​=Tc​+(Th​−Tc​)(Ss​−Sc​)/(Sh​−Sc​))

Words: 97
Radiometer OurBigBook.com
For a stable radiometer with affine output S=g(T+Tr​), let g>0 be the fixed response coefficient and Tr​ the receiver noise temperature. Two reference loads with known equivalent input noise temperatures Tc​<Th​ give outputs Sc​ and Sh​. Subtracting their outputs eliminates Tr​; dividing source and reference differences eliminates g:
Sh​−Sc​=g(Th​−Tc​),Ts​=Tc​+(Th​−Tc​)Sh​−Sc​Ss​−Sc​​.
(1)
This two-load radiometer calibration assumes unchanged response and receiver noise temperature between readings, with the loads and source referred to the same input. It also eliminates an additional constant output offset. Physical load temperatures must first be converted to the applicable equivalent input noise temperatures when the classical thermal approximation fails. The Penzias-Wilson microwave background experiment gives an original worked example.

Radiant flux (Φ=dQ/dt)

Words: 1k Articles: 4
Radiometry OurBigBook.com
Radiant flux is the power carried by electromagnetic radiation: radiation energy transferred per unit time, measured in watts, W=Js−1. Specify what is counted: all radiation emitted by a source, radiation within one beam, or radiation intercepted by a photodetector. Writing Q(t) for the cumulative energy crossing the specified boundary gives
Φe​(t)=dtdQ​,Φe​=t2​−t1​Q(t2​)−Q(t1​)​,Q(t2​)−Q(t1​)=∫t1​t2​​Φe​(t)dt.
(2)
The first expression is an instantaneous derivative; the second is an average over the stated time interval. If 0.020J arrives during 0.010s, the mean radiant flux is 2W. The photodetector's absorbed power equals this incident radiant flux only if it absorbs all of the incident radiation.
Keeping radiant flux fixed while widening a beam reduces its irradiance, the incident power per receiving area. Radiance additionally resolves the propagation direction and has units Wm−2sr−1; an optical camera can collect different radiant fluxes from sources with the same radiance but different apparent solid angles. Luminous flux, measured in lumens, weights the optical spectrum by the human eye's standardized response instead of counting energy equally at every wavelength. Equal radiant fluxes need not give equal luminous fluxes.[ref]
Radiant power also differs from the optical power of an optical lens, which is reciprocal focal length in dioptres. Likewise, astronomical radiative flux is power per area. Stating the units prevents these shared words from hiding different physical quantities.

Radiant flux through an oriented surface (Φnet​=Φ+​−Φ−​)

Words: 254
Radiant flux OurBigBook.com
In geometrical optics, suppose radiation has a nonnegative radiance L(x,s) whose directional contributions add as power. Here s is the actual propagation unit vector, dΩ is a solid angle element, and the collecting oriented surface A has chosen unit normal n(x). Assume the following integrals exist. The outward and inward radiant fluxes are
Φ+​=∫A​∫n⋅s>0​L(x,s)(n⋅s)dΩdA,Φ−​=∫A​∫n⋅s<0​L(x,s)(−n⋅s)dΩdA.
(3)
Both quantities are nonnegative; their difference is the signed net radiant flux. The dot product supplies the cosine of the angle with the unit normal, hence the projected area. For incoming radiation the outward unit normal gives a negative dot product; the minus sign makes the collected power positive. Measuring the incoming angle from a source-facing unit normal instead gives the familiar positive cosθ. The units check is Wm−2sr−1×m2×sr=W.
Equivalently, the incoming surface integral is Φ−​=∫A​EdA with local irradiance E; the emitted part of Φ+​ is ∫A​MdA with radiant exitance M. For a small flat patch and a narrow accepted cone, nearly constant radiance and angle give Φ≃LAcosθΔΩ. This approximation requires uniformity across the patch and cone. Direction-independent outgoing radiance over a whole hemisphere gives the different exact result Φ+​=πLA, by Lambert's cosine law and the spherical coordinates calculation in radiant exitance. Replacing the cosine-weighted integral by the hemisphere's 2π solid angle would double the answer.[ref]
These radiance formulas presume a description in additive directional power; they do not reconstruct arbitrary coherent wave interference from separate ray contributions. For general electromagnetic fields, the signed instantaneous power crossing an oriented surface is ∫A​S⋅ndA, using the Poynting vector S; optical measurements commonly average over many wave periods. A zero net radiant flux can conceal equal nonzero incoming and outgoing radiant fluxes.

Beam collection at fixed radiant flux (Φcollected​=Pmin(1,a2/b2))

Words: 230
Radiant flux OurBigBook.com
Consider an ideal parallel circular beam with uniform irradiance inside radius b, zero outside, and total radiant flux P=2W. A centred circular collector of radius a, perpendicular to the light rays, intercepts
Eb​=πb2P​,Φcollected​=Eb​πmin(a,b)2=Pmin(1,b2a2​).
(4)
For b=1cm and a=0.5cm, Eb​=20000/πWm−2≃6366.20Wm−2 and the collected radiant flux is 0.50W. Expanding the beam to b=2cm while keeping P fixed divides irradiance and the same collector's radiant flux by four: Eb​≃1591.55Wm−2 and Φcollected​=0.125W. A collector covering either entire beam still receives 2W.
Tilting a flat collector of actual area A by an angle θ to the beam direction gives Φcollected​=Eb​Acosθ if its entire projected area remains inside the uniform beam. At 60∘ the small collector's radiant flux halves. A collector large enough to intercept the whole beam after tilting still receives P: the footprint on its surface grows as 1/cosθ and cancels the irradiance factor. This model ignores diffraction, beam-edge structure, and changes in absorption with incidence angle; it counts incident rather than absorbed power.

Spectral radiant flux (Φe,λ​, Φe,ν​)

Words: 256
Radiant flux OurBigBook.com
Spectral radiant flux resolves radiant flux by wavelength or frequency:
Φe​=∫0∞​Φe,λ​(λ)dλ=∫0∞​Φe,ν​(ν)dν.
(5)
Its units are Wm−1 per metre of wavelength or WHz−1 per hertz of frequency. A density quoted per nanometre must be integrated with wavelength intervals in nanometres; its numerical value is 10−9 times the per-metre density. The frequency and wavelength spectral radiance conversion applies equally to spectral radiant flux, through the change of variables formula:
Φe,ν​(ν)=Φe,λ​(c/ν)ν2c​,
(6)
with c the speed of light in vacuum and Φe,λ​ expressed per metre. The Jacobian determinant preserves integrated power rather than the height or location of an optical spectrum's peak.
For two explicitly synthetic optical spectra, let x=λ/(1nm) and quote the following densities in mWnm−1:
fA​(x)={0.020(1−∣x−500∣/100),0,​400≤x≤600,otherwise,​fB​(x)={0.010,0,​600≤x≤800,otherwise.​
(7)
The triangle under fA​ and the rectangle under fB​ both have area 2mW: 200×0.020/2=200×0.010=2. Their band radiant fluxes differ: 450–550nm contains 1.5mW of A, while 650–750nm contains 1.0mW of B. These are specified functions, not measured source spectra.
The dark-subtracted electric current of a linear photodiode is
i=∫R(λ)Φe,λ​(λ)dλ,[R]=AW−1,
(8)
using its spectral responsivity. The figure's stipulated R gives iA​=0.60mA and iB​=1.00mA despite equal total radiant flux. Symmetry places A's mean wavelength at 500nm and B's at 700nm; a linear R averages to its value at that mean. A single electric current therefore cannot determine an unknown broadband radiant flux without additional spectral information or a sufficiently flat calibrated response.

Calibrated radiant-flux measurement

Words: 341
Radiant flux OurBigBook.com
An optical power meter makes the definition operational: collect a specified beam on a calibrated photodiode, measure its electric current, and infer incident radiant flux using spectral responsivity. In the manufacturer-authored Model 1830-R Series Optical Power Meters User's Manual by Newport Corporation, §5.5.1 specifies a concrete sequence: connect the detector and its calibration module, select watt mode and the source wavelength, block the beam and press ZERO, then unblock it and read the display. The stored background includes dark current and ambient light present during zeroing. For narrowband radiation,
Φindicated​=R(λ)iilluminated​−izero​​.
(9)
The electric current difference divided by spectral responsivity has units A/(AW−1)=W. For a stipulated R=0.40AW−1 and difference 0.20mA, the inferred radiant flux is 0.50mW; these numbers illustrate the inference and are not reported measurements. The entire beam must hit the active area, and §5.6 calls for correct wavelength, background subtraction, and operation below detector saturation. If an optical attenuator is fitted, §5.4 requires the calibrated detector–attenuator pair and the matching meter setting.
The calibration itself must connect electric current to independently known incident power. NIST's Spectroradiometric Detector Measurements: Ultraviolet, Visible, and Near-Infrared Detectors for Spectral Power by Thomas C. Larason and others describes this chain. A cryogenic radiometer, an electrical substitution radiometer, compares temperature changes from absorbed light with those from known Joule heating (§5.2). Transfer and working detectors carry that watt scale to a spectral comparator (§5.1). A source, monochromator, focusing optics, shutter, beam splitter, and monitor photodiode illuminate a standard and test detector in turn; calibrated current-to-voltage electronics and voltmeters provide the readout (§§3.2.2–3.2.4, 4.3.1). At each wavelength, subtract blocked-beam signals and normalize each detector signal by the simultaneously sampled monitor signal. Comparison with the standard yields the test detector's spectral responsivity. NIST uses two working standards and alignment normal to the beam (§6.1.1).
Controls distinguish a smaller collected radiant flux from a faulty conversion: block and unblock the beam to check the baseline; check that its footprint fits the active area; verify the calibrated optical attenuator configuration; and test proportional response within the specified operating range. Source drift faster than monitor sampling is not removed. NIST discusses finite spectral bandwidth, stray light, temperature, and spatial response variations (§§3.1.1, 6.2, 7.2). A broadband optical spectrum requires the weighted integral in spectral radiant flux, rather than dividing by an arbitrary single spectral responsivity. These limitations explain why the meter's displayed watts need a stated collection boundary, spectral assumptions, and calibration conditions.

Radiant intensity (I=dΦ/dΩ)

Words: 110 Articles: 1
Radiometry OurBigBook.com
Radiant intensity describes how a source distributes its radiant flux over directions. A small cone of solid angle ΔΩ carries approximately IΔΩ when I varies little across it; integrating I over all emitting directions recovers the total radiant flux. For a source with isotropy emitting 4πW over a full sphere, I=1Wsr−1 and a 0.01sr cone carries 0.01W. A planar source satisfying Lambert's cosine law instead has direction-dependent radiant intensity, even though its radiance is direction-independent.

Point emitter

Words: 45
Radiant intensity OurBigBook.com
An ideal point emitter emits radiant flux from one location. It approximates a physical source when its emitting dimensions are small relative to the distances relevant to the calculation. With isotropy, its radiant intensity is I=Φ/(4π) for total radiant flux Φ. An extended source generally requires integrating contributions from different emitting positions.

Radiance (L)

Words: 2k Articles: 10
Radiometry OurBigBook.com
Radiance resolves both position and direction: it is radiant flux per projected area and per solid angle. For a surface element of area dA, and a small outgoing cone dΩ at angle 0≤θ<π/2 to its outward unit normal,
d2Φ=L(x,s)cosθdAdΩ,[L]=Wm−2sr−1.
(10)
Here x locates the patch and s is a unit vector along the outgoing direction. The factor dAcosθ is the patch's apparent area viewed along that direction. The same local definition applies to transmitted or received radiation, choosing the positive projected area for the crossing direction. [ref].
For a planar patch of area A with spatially uniform, direction-independent radiance L, integrating over the patch gives
I(θ)=LAcosθ.
(11)
Take L=10Wm−2sr−1 and A=0.020m2. The radiant intensities are I(0)=0.20Wsr−1 and I(60∘)=0.10Wsr−1. A sufficiently narrow 0.010sr cone therefore carries approximately 2.0mW or 1.0mW, respectively. Both views have the same radiance L: the oblique view presents half the projected area. This is Lambert's cosine law, not a loss of radiance with viewing angle.
Radiance is useful because it separates a source's local directional output from the geometry of an observation. Integrating over outgoing directions gives radiant exitance; integrating incoming radiance at a receiver gives irradiance. In the stationary vacuum setting of vacuum conservation of specific intensity, a resolved uniform source retains its radiance along each light ray, while the radiant flux received from the whole source can decrease as its apparent solid angle shrinks.

Projected area (A⊥​=Acosθ)

Words: 88
Radiance OurBigBook.com
The projected area of a planar patch viewed from a direction is the area of its perpendicular shadow onto a plane normal to that direction. If n and s are its unit normal and viewing unit vector, then A⊥​=A∣n⋅s∣ by the dot product. On the outward side, this is Acosθ. For a rectangle tilted around one edge, the width perpendicular to that edge contracts by cosθ and the edge length stays fixed, proving the formula. At grazing view A⊥​=0; interpret the radiance definition by a limit rather than dividing by zero.

Spectral radiance (Lν​, Lλ​)

Words: 2k Articles: 8
Radiance OurBigBook.com
Spectral radiance is radiance per unit frequency or wavelength interval. Its astronomical synonym specific intensity is usually written Iν​=Lν​ or Iλ​=Lλ​; this Iν​ is a spectral radiance, distinct from the radiant intensity I in radiometry. For positive small intervals,
d3Φ=Lν​cosθdAdΩdν=Lλ​cosθdAdΩdλ.
(12)
The two expressions refer to the same spectral slice, with the positive widths related through frequency ν and wavelength λ. For frequency measured in hertz, Hz=s−1, the units are [Lν​]=Wm−2sr−1Hz−1. For wavelength measured in metres, [Lλ​]=Wm−2sr−1m−1. Integrating over a spectral band gives its radiance, and then multiplying by the collecting projected area and accepted solid angle gives a small uniform beam's radiant flux. A spectral measurement therefore retains information that the band-integrated power alone loses.
For an ideal blackbody, Planck's law gives Lν​=Bν​(T) and Lλ​=Bλ​(T) at temperature T, independent of outgoing direction. Its existing frequency and wavelength peaks of Planck's law illustrate why the coordinate matters. The simpler flat-band calculation below isolates that same change of variables without the thermal physics.

Frequency and wavelength spectral radiance conversion (Lν​=Lλ​(c/ν)c/ν2)

Words: 332
Spectral radiance OurBigBook.com
In vacuum, ν=c/λ, where c is the speed of light. Increasing wavelength decreases frequency, so the ordinary signed differentials satisfy dν=−cλ−2dλ. A spectral slice's positive contribution to radiance must be unchanged. The change of variables formula therefore gives the absolute Jacobian determinant
Lν​(ν)=Lλ​(c/ν)​dνdλ​​=Lλ​(c/ν)ν2c​,Lλ​(λ)=Lν​(c/λ)λ2c​.
(13)
These formulas use Lλ​ per metre and Lν​ per hertz. The factor c/ν2 has units ms=mHz−1, which converts the former density into the latter. If ℓnm​ denotes the numerical density per nanometre in Wm−2sr−1nm−1, its per-metre value is 109ℓnm​. Conversely, the per-nanometre value is 10−9 times the per-metre value. Likewise, a density per terahertz is 1012 times its per-hertz value because 1THz=1012Hz.
For a concrete stipulated spectrum, let Lλ​=C=109Wm−2sr−1m−1 between λ1​=500nm and λ2​=600nm, and zero outside. This is exactly 1Wm−2sr−1nm−1 throughout the band. Using the exact International System of Units value c=299792458ms−1, the corresponding frequency bounds are
ν1​=λ2​c​=499.654096666…THz,ν2​=λ1​c​=599.584916THz.
(14)
The transformed spectral radiance is Lν​=Cc/ν2 on this interval. At ν1​ it is approximately 1.200830743×10−12Wm−2sr−1Hz−1, and at ν2​ it is 8.339102380×10−13 in the same units. These are respectively 1.200830743 and 0.833910238 per terahertz. Thus a flat wavelength density becomes a decreasing frequency density; relabelling the horizontal axis alone would miss both the shape and the units. [ref].
The equality of band radiances has a short exact proof:
∫λ1​λ2​​Cdλ=C(λ2​−λ1​)=100Wm−2sr−1,
(15)
∫ν1​ν2​​ν2Cc​dν=Cc(ν1​1​−ν2​1​)=C(λ2​−λ1​).
(16)
The reversed bounds and the Jacobian determinant preserve the same spectral measure. Each half-band, 500–550nm and 550–600nm, carries 50Wm−2sr−1, although the corresponding frequency widths are about 54.508 and 45.423THz.
A spectrum's peak is consequently a statement about a particular density and coordinate. The frequency and wavelength peaks of Planck's law differ for precisely this reason. Integrated radiance over a specified physical band supplies a comparison that survives the change of variables.

Vacuum conservation of specific intensity

Words: 227
Spectral radiance OurBigBook.com
In a transparent, stationary vacuum with Euclidean geometry and observers at rest in the same frame, the radiative transfer equation has zero absorption and emission, so dIν​/ds=0 along a light ray. The same specific intensity reaches the observer without geometric dilution. More generally, Iν​/ν3 is a Lorentz invariant; collisionless propagation preserves this ratio along a ray even with gravitational or cosmological redshift, rather than preserving Iν​ at shifted frequencies. [ref].
The elementary geometry is instructive. Two facing surface elements separated by distance s exchange spectral radiant flux
d3Φ=Iν​s2cosθ1​cosθ2​​dA1​dA2​dν.
(17)
The receiver subtends dΩ2​=cosθ2​dA2​/s2 at the source. Describing that same traveling beam at the receiver instead uses dΩ1​=cosθ1​dA1​/s2. Both descriptions have identical geometric factors, so conservation of its radiant flux gives equal specific intensities at the two ends. This compares the same propagation direction at both ends, not emission in the opposite direction.
A uniform source remains equally bright per resolved direction under these assumptions, but occupies a smaller apparent solid angle farther away. For a small source of projected area A⊥​ at distance d and a small receiver facing it, irradiance is approximately E=LΩsource​≃LA⊥​/d2. The radiant flux intercepted by receiver area Ad​ is approximately EAd​. With L=10Wm−2sr−1, A⊥​=0.020m2 and Ad​=10−4m2, distances 10 and 20m give E≃0.002 and 0.0005Wm−2, and received radiant fluxes of 0.20 and 0.050μW. The inverse-square law concerns collected radiation from the whole source; L remains 10 at either distance.

Limb darkening

Words: 1k Articles: 5
Spectral radiance OurBigBook.com
Limb darkening is the decrease of emergent specific intensity toward the apparent edge of a stellar disc. A transit depth then depends on the intensity sampled by the planet as well as its projected area; the uniform-disc area ratio is only an approximation.
For a spherical star of radius R⋆​, let r be distance from the centre of its projected disk and let θ be the angle between the outward unit normal and the line of sight. The angular coordinate is
μ=cosθ=1−(r/R⋆​)2​.
(18)
Disk centre has μ=1; the apparent edge approaches μ=0. A resolved image measures specific intensity in each direction. Limb darkening means that this specific intensity itself falls toward the edge; the projected area factor used when collecting radiant flux is a separate geometric effect.
A simple explanation assumes a horizontally uniform plane-parallel atmosphere, a sufficiently deep optically thick medium, negligible scattering of light, and thermal absorption and emission in local thermodynamic equilibrium. Let τν​ be inward vertical optical depth at fixed frequency ν. An oblique light ray has line of sight optical depth τν​/μ, so it encounters the same attenuation at a smaller vertical optical depth. The formal solution of the radiative transfer equation gives
Iν​(0,μ)=∫0∞​Sν​(τν​)e−τν​/μμdτν​​,0<μ≤1.
(19)
Here Sν​ is the source function; under these assumptions it is the Planck function Bν​[T(τν​)]. If temperature increases inward, an oblique view preferentially samples cooler layers and hence lower specific intensity. [ref]
The weight e−τν​/μ/μ is an exponential distribution in vertical optical depth, with expected value μ and standard deviation μ. Thus “seeing down to optical depth one” refers to the line of sight, and represents a broad sampling of layers rather than a sharp emitting surface. For a source function that is linear throughout the atmosphere, the Eddington-Barbier relation is exact:
Sν​(τν​)=aν​+bν​τν​⟹Iν​(0,μ)=aν​+bν​μ=Sν​(μ).
(20)
This follows by integrating the constant and linear terms separately. A constant source function instead gives constant specific intensity, as in the blackbody limit of an isothermal atmosphere. An outward-rising temperature or important scattering of light changes the argument. Close enough to the actual edge, a plane-parallel atmosphere also loses validity because of curvature. Neither one limb-darkening coefficient nor a universal dependence on wavelength follows from this simple model.
Linear limb-darkening law (I(μ)=Ic​[1−u(1−μ)])
Words: 221 Articles: 1
Limb darkening OurBigBook.com
The linear limb-darkening law parametrizes angular specific intensity by a stipulated limb-darkening coefficient u:
I(μ)=Ic​[1−u(1−μ)],Ic​=I(1),0≤u≤1.
(21)
Here I denotes specific intensity at one frequency, or radiance integrated over a specified interval of frequency or wavelength; it does not denote radiant intensity. Within this stipulated range, u=0 gives a uniform disk and I(0)/Ic​=1−u. The linear limb-darkening law is linear in μ, while its radial profile I(r)=Ic​[1−u+u1−(r/R⋆​)2​] is curved. In the linear source function example above, u=bν​/(aν​+bν​) when aν​,bν​≥0 and aν​+bν​>0.
For the illustrative choice u=0.6, the specific intensity at r/R⋆​=0, 0.8, and the limiting edge is respectively Ic​, 0.76Ic​, and 0.4Ic​. These are synthetic values, not measurements of the Sun. Real optical spectra and stellar atmospheres need not obey a linear limb-darkening law; more flexible profiles can be necessary for accurate exoplanet transit photometry. [ref]
Limb-darkening coefficient (u)
Words: 47
Linear limb-darkening law OurBigBook.com
The parameter u in the linear limb-darkening law specifies its contrast between disk centre and the limiting edge. Its interpretation requires the chosen law and interval of frequency or wavelength. A limb-darkening coefficient fitted to an exoplanet transit may trade off against the transit impact parameter and planet radius; it is not a direct measurement of one layer's temperature.
Limb-darkened disk flux (I=Ic​(1−u/3))
Words: 133
Limb darkening OurBigBook.com
For a circular stellar disk whose specific intensity is independent of azimuth, its mean specific intensity over projected area is
I=πR⋆2​1​∫disk​IdAproj​=2∫01​I(μ)μdμ.
(22)
The polar coordinates element is dAproj​=2πrdr after angular integration, and rdr=−R⋆2​μdμ. Substitution of the linear limb-darkening law gives
I=2Ic​∫01​(1−u+uμ)μdμ=Ic​(1−u/3).
(23)
For a distant observer at distance D≫R⋆​ receiving light normally to the detecting plane, the observed radiative flux is
fν​≃D2πR⋆2​​Iν​=π(DR⋆​​)2Iν,c​(1−uν​/3).
(24)
It is spectral irradiance, with units Wm−2Hz−1 when Iν​ is per hertz. The local outward radiative flux per actual surface area is instead Fν​=2π∫01​Iν​(μ)μdμ=πIν​, a spectral radiant exitance. In a spherical model with the same stellar atmosphere everywhere, the luminosity is Lν​=4πR⋆2​Fν​ and fν​=Lν​/(4πD2), consistent with the inverse-square law. Compare disks at fixed Ic​ or fixed total observed radiative flux explicitly: increasing u lowers the total in the first comparison, while the second requires a larger Ic​.
Small-planet transit weighting (δ≃k2I(μp​)/I)
Words: 346
Limb darkening OurBigBook.com
A dark, opaque circular planet of radius Rp​ blocks specific intensity over its overlap with the projected star. With k=Rp​/R⋆​ and no transit dilution, the instantaneous exoplanet transit depth is exactly
δ=πR⋆2​I∫overlap​IdAproj​​.
(25)
When k≪1, the entire planetary disk is inside the stellar disk, and specific intensity varies little across that disk, small-planet transit weighting reduces to
δ≃k2II(μp​)​=k21−u/31−u+uμp​​,μp​=1−z2​,z<1−k.
(26)
Here z is the projected centre separation divided by R⋆​. The numerator samples the local specific intensity; the denominator normalizes by the whole star's mean specific intensity. The small-size argument and the distinction between full overlap and ingress are explicit in the original analytic treatment. [ref]
For a short approximately straight crossing at constant transverse velocity v⊥​, write x=v⊥​t/R⋆​ with t=0 at mid-transit, and let b be the transit impact parameter. Then z2=x2+b2 and μp​=1−b2−x2​. With stipulated u=0.6 and k=0.05, a central crossing has mid-transit exoplanet transit depth approximately 0.003125, whereas b=0.8 gives 0.002375. A uniform disk would give k2=0.0025 for either fully overlapping crossing. The brighter central chord produces a deeper dip and the off-centre chord a shorter crossing at the same v⊥​.
At fixed z<1 away from the edge, a two-dimensional Taylor's theorem expansion over the symmetric planetary disk cancels the first derivatives; the first correction to the blocked radiant flux is of order k4 if the second derivatives remain bounded. This error estimate is not uniform at the edge, where the radial derivative of the linear limb-darkening law diverges. Ingress, egress, a grazing exoplanet transit, and a crossing of a starspot require the overlap integral or a suitable more complete model. Inferring k=δ​ without the local-to-mean specific intensity ratio can bias the estimated planet radius.
Solar limb-darkening drift-scan experiment
Words: 185
Limb darkening OurBigBook.com
A motivating measurement used the McMath solar optical telescope at Kitt Peak. The Sun's image drifted across a 10mm-high, 0.2mm-wide optical slit; a double-pass spectrograph selected wavelength intervals from 0.0015nm wide at 303nm to 0.007nm at 1099nm. An EMI 9750 QB photodetector or silicon photodiode supplied a digitized signal: 1kHz samples were summed into 4096 positions per crossing, including dark and off-limb sky readings. [ref]
Controls included daily realignment of optical telescope mirrors, wavelength settings between spectral lines insensitive to the rotational Doppler shift, selection of detectors without detectable hysteresis, and measured electronic nonlinearity corrections below 0.1% of maximum signal. Limb-position calibration combined centres of gravity of the edge derivative profiles with almanac semidiameters; the preliminary edge shifts were consistent with astronomical seeing of approximately 5 arcseconds full width at half maximum. [ref]
The decreasing centre-to-limb readout gives a specific intensity profile, constraining stratification of the photosphere under a radiative transfer model. Astronomical seeing, instrumental scattering of light, and solar granulation limit this inference; brightness alone does not uniquely determine temperature versus geometric height. These observations need not follow the stipulated linear limb-darkening law.

Radiant exitance (M=Me​=dΦemitted​/dA)

Words: 163
Radiometry OurBigBook.com
Radiant exitance is total outward emitted radiant flux per actual emitting area, measured in Wm−2. Integrate radiance over the outward hemisphere, keeping the projected area factor:
M=∫Ωoutward​​L(θ,φ)cosθdΩ.
(27)
If the radiance is direction-independent, the spherical coordinates element is dΩ=sinθdθdφ, so
M=L∫02π​dφ∫0π/2​cosθsinθdθ=πL.
(28)
The weighted solid angle is πsr, although the whole hemisphere covers 2πsr. Directions near grazing contribute less because of the projected area. This geometric factor is the bridge between Lambert's cosine law and the total output of a diffuse patch. [ref].
For the uniform patch with L=10Wm−2sr−1, radiant exitance is M=10πWm−2. Its area 0.020m2 gives total radiant flux Φ=MA=0.20πW≈0.628319W over the outward hemisphere. This total differs from its radiant intensity I(0)=0.20Wsr−1, which describes only one direction.
For an ideal blackbody, integrate Planck's law over frequency as well: M=Me​=j∗=σT4 by the Stefan–Boltzmann law. An ideal 6000K emitting patch of area 1mm2 emits about 73.4881W outward. Irradiance counts incoming radiation separately; for purely emitted output the net outward exchange per area is M−E. Reflected output must also be included if the surface reflects incident radiation.

Irradiance (E=dΦincident​/dA)

Words: 125 Articles: 1
Radiometry OurBigBook.com
Irradiance measures incident radiant flux per actual receiving area. Its units Wm−2 match radiant exitance, but it counts the incoming side of the radiation exchange. Measure incoming angles from the unit normal pointing toward the incident hemisphere. Then
E=∫Ωincident​​Lincoming​(θ,φ)cosθdΩ.
(29)
For 2W distributed uniformly over 0.010m2, E=200Wm−2. If the receiving plane is tilted 60∘ in the same broad uniform parallel beam, the incident power per actual area is halved to 100Wm−2. This receiving-surface cosine factor is geometric and does not assume Lambertian reflectance. Together, irradiance and radiant exitance describe how radiation supplies and removes energy at a surface, while radiance preserves the directional information.

A parallel beam incident at elevation α has horizontal irradiance Fh​=F⊥​sinα. Its path optical depth in a plane-parallel atmosphere is τv​/sinα. Confusing these projections changes both attenuation and intercepted power.

Radiometric absorptance (a=Pabs​/Pin​)

Words: 117
Radiometry OurBigBook.com
Radiometric absorptance is the fraction of incident radiant flux converted to heat in the specified receiver: Pabs​=aPin​, with 0≤a≤1 for a passive receiver. Specify the entrance boundary, wavelength, incidence angle and receiver temperature. Radiation reflected or transmitted out of that receiver is not absorbed power. A cavity can improve radiometric absorptance by giving light repeated opportunities for wave absorption; a dark coating alone does not establish a=1.
For broadband light, spectral radiant flux matters: Pabs​=∫a(λ)Φe,λ​(λ)dλ. A single effective radiometric absorptance therefore depends on the incident optical spectrum as well as on the receiver. If the stated incident boundary lies before a window of known power transmission τw​, the receiver absorbs aτw​Pbefore​ when other optical losses are absent. Window loss and receiver radiometric absorptance are separate corrections.

Electrical substitution radiometer

Words: 1k Articles: 6
Radiometry OurBigBook.com
An electrical substitution radiometer measures radiant flux by replacing absorbed optical power with calibrated Joule heating. At fixed temperature, feedback control holds the receiver equally warm with the beam blocked or admitted: after corrections, absorbed optical power equals the reduction in supplied electrical heating. Incident radiant flux additionally requires radiometric absorptance and entrance-path corrections. This connects radiometry to electrical standards.[ref]
Picture a small absorbing cavity connected to a colder reference block by a radiometer thermal link. A heater supplies Joule heating, a thermometer senses the receiver, and feedback control adjusts the heater. When light supplies some of the heating, the controller supplies less. The readout is the change in heater power, rather than the beam's brightness or its number of photons. NIST's institutional description gives a concrete receiver with a conical interior, absorbing coating and weak Kapton support, operated with a receiver heater and thermometer.[ref]

Electrical substitution heat balance (Pabs​=Ph,d​−Ph,l​)

Words: 643 Articles: 2
Electrical substitution radiometer OurBigBook.com
An explicit lumped model makes the cancellation understandable. Treat the receiver as having uniform temperature T, heat capacity C, heater power Ph​ deposited in the receiver, net parasitic input Ppar​, and outward heat transfer Qout​(T,Tb​) to a reference at temperature Tb​. With incident radiant flux Pin​ and radiometric absorptance a, conservation of energy gives
CdtdT​=Ph​+aPin​+Ppar​−Qout​(T,Tb​).
(30)
The dark state has no admitted beam. Compare settled dark and light states, denoted d and l; each has dT/dt=0. Subtracting their balances gives
Pabs​=aPin​=ΔPh​+ΔQout​−ΔPpar​,ΔPh​=Ph,d​−Ph,l​,
(31)
where ΔQout​=Qout,l​−Qout,d​ and ΔPpar​=Ppar,l​−Ppar,d​. At identical T and Tb​, with unchanged surroundings and parasitic heating, both correction differences vanish. Thus Pabs​=ΔPh​, and Pin​=ΔPh​/a. Notice the signs: extra stray thermal radiation admitted with the beam also reduces heater power, so it must be subtracted from that reduction before attributing the result to the intended beam.
Measure the heater's own terminal voltage Vh​ and electric current Ih​: for steady electrical heating, Ph​=Vh​Ih​=Ih2​Rh​, where Rh​ is its electrical resistance. A supply voltage that includes wire voltage drops counts Joule heating in the leads too; only the portion deposited in the receiver belongs in this balance. Changes in that deposited lead heat belong in ΔPpar​. Optical-electrical heating non-equivalence adds a further instrument-specific correction when the uniform-temperature model is insufficient.[ref]

Radiometer thermal link (Qlink​≃G(T−Tb​), τ=C/G)

Words: 97
Electrical substitution heat balance OurBigBook.com
A radiometer thermal link is the intentional path for thermal conduction from receiver to reference block. Near a chosen operating point, write Qlink​≃G(T−Tb​) with local conductance G in WK−1. A weaker link increases the steady temperature rise per added power, δT≃δP/G, but also increases the uncontrolled relaxation time τ=C/G when the link dominates other heat transfer. These are local linear approximations: actual thermal conductivity depends on temperature, and active feedback control introduces its own response. NIST explicitly describes the tradeoff between weaker links and smaller receiver heat capacity.[ref]

Fixed-temperature electrical substitution example (Pin​=(2−1.005)/0.995mW=1mW)

Words: 312
Electrical substitution heat balance OurBigBook.com
Stipulate a model receiver held at T=5.4K with a reference at Tb​=5.0K. Choose G=5mWK−1, so outward thermal conduction is G(T−Tb​)=2mW. Ignore other heat transfer, take Ppar​=0 in both states, and stipulate perfect optical/electrical thermal equivalence. These values specify an original example; they are not measurements or claimed performance of a NIST instrument.
With the beam blocked, supply Ph,d​=2mW. With light admitted, the required heater power is Ph,l​=1.005mW. For stipulated radiometric absorptance a=0.995 at the chosen single wavelength,
Pabs​=2−1.005=0.995mW,Pin​=0.9950.995mW​=1.000mW.
(32)
The illuminated receiver still gets 1.005+0.995=2mW of heating. The remaining 0.005mW of incident radiant flux escapes without heating it. Calling the measured heater reduction “incident power” would underestimate the stipulated incident radiant flux by 0.5%.
Two variations expose different pitfalls. First, if admitting the beam also adds 0.010mW of stray heating while the same 0.995mW is absorbed from the intended beam, the heater falls to 0.995mW. The apparent reduction is now 1.005mW; subtracting ΔPpar​=0.010mW recovers the beam's absorbed power. Second, keep the original heater readings but stipulate a light-state receiver offset δT=+1mK at fixed reference temperature. The link carries an extra GδT=0.005mW; the inferred absorbed power becomes 1.000mW, and incident radiant flux becomes 1.000/0.995≃1.005025mW. This second variation deliberately violates exact fixed-temperature substitution.
The heater can supply only nonnegative power in this model. Consequently an absorbed beam above the available dark heater power cannot be compensated at the chosen setpoint merely by reducing the heater. The operating point, link, or admitted radiant flux must change; zero heater demand is a range boundary, not evidence of greater radiant flux sensitivity.

Optical-electrical heating non-equivalence

Words: 138
Electrical substitution radiometer OurBigBook.com
Optical-electrical heating non-equivalence means that equal absorbed optical and electrical powers do not necessarily give identical thermometer responses. Light and the heater may deposit heat in different places, producing different temperature gradients and different routes of thermal conduction to the support. Holding one thermometer reading fixed need not hold every part of the cavity at the same temperature. This differs from incomplete radiometric absorptance: one concerns where absorbed heat goes, the other how much incident light becomes absorbed heat.
A useful accounting convention is
Pin​=aΔPh​+ΔQout​−ΔPpar​+δne​​,
(33)
where the signed correction δne​ is determined by characterization of the instrument and represents the residual departure from the lumped electrical substitution heat balance. It is not a universal extra source of energy. Varying the beam location or heater location, comparing equivalent temperature excursions, and checking repeatable dark returns can test the assumptions; no single small correction proves that every other correction vanishes.

Cryogenic radiometer

Words: 394 Articles: 1
Electrical substitution radiometer OurBigBook.com
A cryogenic radiometer uses low-temperature operation to improve electrical substitution radiometry through particular material and thermal properties. Lower heat capacity of a copper receiver permits a substantial cavity without proportionately slow relaxation; cold shields reduce unwanted thermal radiation, and heater leads with superconductivity can suppress resistive lead heating. These features do not make radiometric absorptance exactly one or abolish optical-electrical heating non-equivalence.[ref]
Cold surroundings matter as much as a cold receiver. In a simplified gray-surface model, net outward thermal radiation is ϵσA(T4−Ts4​), using emissivity ϵ, area A, shield temperature Ts​ and the Stefan-Boltzmann constant σ. Cold shields reduce the incoming term as well as the receiver's emission; cooling the receiver alone while leaving it exposed to warm surroundings does not remove incoming radiation. An entrance window still provides an optical and thermal path. Vacuum reduces gas-mediated heat transfer, while an intentional radiometer thermal link remains necessary. This model explains specific losses, rather than promising a universal improvement factor or perfect accuracy.

Cryogenic laser-power substitution experiment

Words: 243
Cryogenic radiometer OurBigBook.com
In NIST's 2001 comparison, the Laser-Optimized Cryogenic Radiometer held its cavity near 5.4K and controlled the heat sink separately. Opening the beam reduced heater power at fixed cavity temperature; an AC bridge read the temperature. Tests used stabilized, spatially filtered lasers at 488, 514 and 633nm, with horizontal input and a movable photodiode transfer detector in the cavity's optical plane. The paper contrasts this with the High Accuracy Cryogenic Radiometer's optical heating followed by two electrical heating cycles and interpolation.[ref]
The two laboratories compared calibrations of the same transfer detectors, and checked their spatial response. Alignment differences and transported dust limited that comparison; its agreement did not prove universal equivalence. Its correction tables retain window transmission, cavity radiometric absorptance, alignment, electrical readout and optical-electrical heating non-equivalence. The heat sink was controlled, but §3 does not specify its numerical setpoint: the 5.0K reference in the preceding example is stipulated.[ref]
For a reproducible substitution measurement, record the beam's wavelength and entrance boundary, receiver and reference temperatures, heater terminal voltage and electric current, dark readings before and after illumination, and the settling rule. Check beam collection at the aperture, window transmission, radiometric absorptance, deposited lead heating, and sensitivity to beam position. Repeatability alone misses a stable correction error. Photometric calibration can subsequently use this radiant flux scale, but photometry still needs the appropriate spectral visual weighting; the cold receiver does not itself measure luminous flux.
