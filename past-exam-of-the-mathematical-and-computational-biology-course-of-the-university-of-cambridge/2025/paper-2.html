To invert the Burrows-Wheeler transform, begin with six empty rows. At every iteration prepend the supplied last-column characters in their fixed row order, then sort the resulting rows in lexicographic order. After k iterations the rows contain the length-k prefixes of the sorted cyclic rotations. For example, the first two iterations give
T1​T2​​=($,B,C,E,M,X),=($M,BE,CB,EX,MC,X$).​
(4)
Each iteration works because sorting the preceding character together with the already reconstructed prefix restores the ordering of the next-longer prefixes. Six iterations give the completed matrix
B=​$BCEMX​MEBXC$​CXE$BM​B$XMEC​EM$CXB​XCMB$E​​.
(5)
The original linear string is the unique row ending in the terminator, hence
S2​=MCBEX$​.
(6)
As a check, the reconstructed matrix's final column is precisely the supplied Burrows-Wheeler transform.

c OurBigBook.com
Use one-based row numbers, and write F and L for the first and last columns. The last-to-first mapping matches the kth occurrence of a character in L to its kth occurrence in F: equal characters retain the ordering of their following suffixes. This lets us extend an interval of matching suffixes one character to the left without reconstructing the full strings.
Start with the last query character. Rows 5,6,7 of F contain C, so the interval of suffixes beginning with C is [5,7]. Within those rows, the A entries of L are at rows 5 and 7. They are respectively the first and second A entries in all of L, and therefore map to rows 2 and 3 of F. These are exactly the suffixes beginning with AC. Reading their supplied positions gives matches starting at positions 11 and 3, or positions 3 and 11 in increasing genomic order.
The same calculation can be written as backward search. Let C(c) count characters strictly smaller than c, and let Occ(c,r) count occurrences of c in L[1:r], with Occ(c,0)=0. Prepending c changes an inclusive interval [l,r] to
[l′,r′]=[C(c)+Occ(c,l−1)+1, C(c)+Occ(c,r)].
(7)
Here C(A)=1, Occ(A,4)=0 and Occ(A,7)=2, giving [l′,r′]=[2,3].

Build an FM-index: store the Burrows-Wheeler transform, cumulative character counts C, and a structure supporting fast rank queries for Occ(c,r). Then each step of backward search uses two rank queries instead of rescanning the last column. A wavelet tree or suitably checkpointed counts can supply these queries while keeping memory manageable. The counting cost is proportional to query length times the rank query cost, rather than repeatedly scanning the whole genome.[ref]
To recover genomic positions, store a sampled suffix array and follow the last-to-first mapping to a sampled position, adding the number of steps taken. More samples trade storage for faster position recovery. Index character counts and sample positions, rather than storing the quadratic-size full cyclic rotation matrix.

With no deletion states allowed, each match state must emit once in every aligned sequence. Thus the four gap-free alignment columns, 1,2,5,7, become M1​,M2​,M3​,M4​. Columns 3 and 4 form one optional run, represented by a single looping insertion state I2​; column 6 requires a separate insertion state I3​ after M3​. Two insertion states are sufficient and minimal in an ordered profile hidden Markov model: the two optional regions are separated by a compulsory emission and occupy different positions in the profile.
Estimate each transition probability by the number of observed uses of that transition divided by all transitions leaving its source state. The complete arrow diagram is given below; repeated labels denote the same state, so the second and third lines supply the branches of the first line. Start and End are silent:
Start1​M1​1​M2​1/4​M3​1/2​M4​1​End,M2​3/4​I2​3/4​M3​,I2​1/4​I2​,M3​1/2​I3​1​M4​.​
(8)
Three aligned sequences visit I2​, giving three exits to M3​, while one sequence emits twice there, giving one self-transition. Thus its exit and loop probabilities are 3/4 and 1/4, respectively. Two sequences visit I3​ and two bypass it. All absent transitions have probability zero.
The probability mass functions of the emitting states, estimated from all characters assigned to each state with no added counts, are
stateM1​M2​I2​M3​I3​M4​​A1/201/4001/4​T001/2100​G1/43/41/4003/4​C1/41/40010​​.
(9)
In particular, pooling the two optional columns into I2​ pools four emissions: two T, one A and one G. This is a generative Hidden Markov model, so it can generate other run lengths and combinations as well as the training sequences; reproducing each training column deterministically is not its purpose.

Use a uniform independent DNA background, so each nucleotide has probability 1/4. Conditional on length five, its sequence probability is 4−5=1/1024. The requested score, often called log-odds in sequence analysis, is a log-likelihood ratio against this background:
log2​4−5P(CGTTA)​=log2​427​≈2.75489 bits​.
(12)
Using natural logarithms instead gives ln(27/4)≈1.90954. Identically distributed independent positions do not, by themselves, require equal base frequencies. If the intended background instead has specified frequencies qA​,qT​,qG​,qC​, the general answer is
logqC​qG​qT2​qA​27/4096​.
(13)
The numerical value above uses the conventional equal-frequency random DNA model.
Taking a logarithm only after multiplying probabilities does not prevent floating-point underflow: the product may already have rounded to zero, and its logarithm is then −∞. Accumulate logarithms before forming tiny products; where alternative paths must be added, use log-sum-exp in the forward algorithm or rescale its intermediate probabilities.

Let T be the time since the two sampled mitochondrial genomes shared their ancestor. Under the stipulated one-change-per-difference approximation, both descendant lineages contribute, giving a total evolutionary duration 2T. Therefore the per-site, per-lineage mutation rate estimate is
μ​naive​=2(14.5×106)0.29​=1.00×10−8 site−1year−1​.
(20)
This is not a whole-genome event rate; multiplying by the number of usable sites would give that rate. Without equal lineage rates, the pairwise data identify their sum rather than either rate separately.
The calculation assumes an accurate sequence alignment of corresponding ancestral sites, negligible errors in DNA sequencing, and an appropriate divergence-time calibration for the sampled mitochondrial genomes. It assumes a constant molecular clock on each lineage, comparable rates across sites for this simple average, and no extra differences introduced by incorrectly aligned insertions or deletions. It treats observed nucleotide substitutions as a record of mutations; interpreting a substitution-based rate as the underlying mutation rate additionally requires a suitable neutral model rather than strong filtering by natural selection. Independence between sites is useful for a simple sampling model and uncertainty calculation. The supplied time must refer to the common ancestor of these sequences: a species split need not coincide exactly with a sampled gene's ancestral split.

The printed “otherwise” branch requires correction. Its factor 3/4 is the probability of ending at any of the three bases different from the initial base, not of ending at one specified different base. Assigning that probability to each of three alternatives would make a row of the transition matrix sum to 5/2−(3/2)e−μt, which exceeds one for t>0. This distinction is present in the source PDF, not introduced by the transcription.
For a consistent Jukes-Cantor model, let each ordered change from one nucleotide to a specified different nucleotide have instantaneous rate q. Its continuous-time Markov chain has diagonal generator entries −3q and off-diagonal entries q. If ps​(t) is the probability of still having the same base as at time zero, its Kolmogorov equations give
ps′​(t)=−3qps​(t)+q[1−ps​(t)]=q−4qps​(t),ps​(0)=1.
(21)
Solving the linear ordinary differential equation gives
ps​(t)=41​+43​e−4qt.
(22)
Set μ=4q to match the exponent used in the question. Symmetry then gives the properly normalized transition probabilities
Pij​(t)=⎩⎨⎧​41​(1+3e−μt),41​(1−e−μt),​i=j,i=j.​​
(23)
Summing the three different-base outcomes yields
P(different base at t)=43​(1−e−μt)​.
(24)
The assumptions are a time-homogeneous Markov process, the same change rate between every distinct ordered base pair, identical rates across independent sites, and no insertions or deletions. The stationary distribution is uniform over the four bases; starting the process in that distribution is not required for this conditional calculation.
There is also a parameter-convention issue. The actual total mutation rate out of a base is r=3q=3μ/4. Thus the exponent parameter μ in (b)–(d) is 4/3 times the event rate used in the elementary counting interpretation of (a). Keeping this conversion explicit avoids changing the meaning of “mutation rate” halfway through the calculation.

Let p(a) be the true seroprevalence at age a, and λ(a) the force of infection, meaning the instantaneous rate of a first infection among people still uninfected. In an irreversible serocatalytic model, people begin seronegative and retain detectable antibodies after infection. Their seronegative fraction s(a)=1−p(a) obeys
dads​=−λ(a)s(a),s(0)=1.
(29)
Hence
p(a)=1−exp[−∫0a​λ(u)du]​.
(30)
Equivalently, where differentiation is possible, λ(a)=p′(a)/[1−p(a)]. This identifies the force of infection as a hazard function and 1−p(a) as the survival function for remaining uninfected. For constant exposure λ, p(a)=1−e−λa. A single calendar-time survey reflects each birth cohort's accumulated exposure; interpreting the same age curve as a timeless age effect requires assumptions about historical transmission.

Assume a constant annual force of infection λ>0 across ages and calendar years. For independent sampled people with ages ai​>0 and binary results yi​, fit
Yi​∼Bernoulli(pi​),pi​=1−e−λai​.
(31)
Applying the complementary log-log link gives
ln[−ln(1−pi​)]​=ln(λai​)=β0​+lnai​,β0​=lnλ.​
(32)
Therefore fit a binomial regression with the complementary log-log link, an intercept, and lnai​ as an offset with coefficient fixed at one. Age is measured in years. An ordinary freely fitted coefficient of age, or even a freely fitted coefficient of lnai​, would describe a different exposure model. The annual force of infection estimate is
λ=eβ​0​ year−1​.
(33)
An infection-naive person's one-year infection probability is 1−e−λ, which is approximately λ only when that rate is small.
The log-likelihood being maximized is, apart from constants,
ℓ(β0​)=∑i​[yi​ln{1−exp(−ai​eβ0​)}−(1−yi​)ai​eβ0​].
(34)
A confidence interval for β0​ can be exponentiated to give a positive interval for λ. For groups of nj​ people at a common age aj​, use Yj​∼Binomial(nj​,1−e−λaj​) with the same offset. If an age band is broad, use individual ages or average 1−e−λa over the band's age distribution instead of treating its midpoint as exact. No numerical estimate is possible without the survey observations.
The biological interpretation assumes that immunoglobulin G positivity records previous infection, persists over the relevant ages, and is not substantially produced by maternal antibodies or vaccination. These are conditions to check, rather than conclusions from the fitted curve. Use known-positive and known-negative serum controls to assess the assay threshold, and validate specificity against relevant other infections. Cross-reactive antibodies to other flaviviruses can produce positive dengue virus serology, so a positive result need not be a marker specific to dengue virus exposure.[ref]
If independently established assay diagnostic sensitivity and diagnostic specificity are Se and Sp, the observed positive probability is
pi,obs​=Se(1−e−λai​)+(1−Sp)e−λai​.
(35)
Use that observation model in the binomial likelihood; it is generally no longer the simple intercept-plus-offset complementary log-log link fit. With only the stated survey, one cannot freely estimate Se, Sp, antibody loss and an arbitrary infection history simultaneously. A single binary result also does not identify how many subsequent infections a person has experienced.

From an initially empty centre, occupancy at a finite time is Poisson distributed with mean N(t)≤25, so the stationary criterion also bounds the fixed-time overflow probability during initial filling. A probability of ever exceeding capacity over an unlimited future is a different question: this irreducible recurrent process will eventually exceed every finite capacity with probability one. If instead arrivals are refused when all places are occupied, that changes the model to an loss network, whose blocking probability is not the unrestricted occupancy tail calculated here.

Use degree-preserving edge rewiring, often called a double-edge swap. Select two edges (u,v) and (x,y) with four distinct endpoints, then replace them by (u,y) and (x,v), choosing the reconnection symmetrically. Reject a proposal that would create a self-loop or an already present edge if the target is a simple graph. Each endpoint loses one edge and gains one, so every degree of a vertex, as well as the numbers of vertices and edges, is preserved exactly.
Repeat many such random proposals to generate reference networks. With symmetric proposals, retaining rejected steps as self-transitions, the uniform distribution on the reachable degree-preserving realizations is stationary. Adequate mixing must be checked; a few successful swaps do not establish an independent random sample. Degree preservation alone does not preserve connectedness, so impose and check that extra restriction if the graph distance comparison requires connected networks.

The Louvain method approximately maximizes network modularity. Start with each vertex in its own community. Repeatedly visit each vertex, evaluate moving it into each neighbouring community, and accept the move with the greatest positive modularity gain. When no such local move improves the score, contract each community into a new weighted vertex, summing inter-community edge weights and retaining internal edges as self-loops. Repeat local optimization and contraction until no further gain is obtained, then map the communities back to the original vertices.[ref]
For an undirected unweighted graph with m edges, degrees of vertices ki​ and community labels ci​, the objective is
Q=2m1​∑i,j​(Aij​−2mki​kj​​)1{ci​=cj​}.
(54)
The term ki​kj​/(2m) subtracts the expected connectivity in a degree-based network null model. If all vertices are put in one community, the observed and expected total weights cancel, so Q=0. A partition with excess internal connectivity can have Q>0, and merging away that structure reduces the score. The modularity objective and positive-gain rule prevent arbitrary merging merely to increase cluster size. They do not forbid a single community in every possible network: if there is no better modular partition, one community may be an appropriate result. The method is a greedy heuristic, so it does not guarantee the global maximum of Q.

For the same concentration gradient, the front-to-back difference scales with cell length. A much smaller bacterial cell samples a correspondingly smaller spatial signal, while stochastic arrival and binding of individual chemoattractant molecules make receptor measurements noisy. For illustration, a 2 μm cell in the 0.5 cm capillary would experience only 0.04 nM across its length, compared with 0.4 nM for the longer cell. This illustrates a size penalty; it does not assume that bacterial and human detection thresholds are identical.
Many swimming bacteria, including Escherichia coli, use temporal sensing in bacterial chemotaxis: they compare recent receptor stimulation with an adapted baseline as they move. Increasing attractant suppresses reorientation and prolongs runs; decreasing attractant increases tumbling. Repeated runs and reorientations therefore generate a biased random walk up the gradient, without requiring a simultaneous comparison between the front and back. Chemotaxis feedback regulation supplies the adaptation needed to remain sensitive to changes rather than responding only to absolute concentration.
A discriminating experiment changes concentration rapidly and uniformly around the bacteria: a response then occurs without a spatial difference across the cell. The classic temporal-step experiments found increased tumbling after attractant decreases and smoother swimming after increases.[ref] In a new experiment, compare up-steps and down-steps with constant-concentration and sham-mixing controls, holding temperature and medium composition fixed. Measure run durations and tumble frequency, rather than only average swimming speed. Such a test supports temporal comparison but does not by itself identify every molecular component or show that all bacterial species use the same mechanism.
