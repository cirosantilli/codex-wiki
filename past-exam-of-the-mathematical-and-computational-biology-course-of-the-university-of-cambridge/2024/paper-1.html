Keep the true parameter fixed and imagine repeatedly collecting four independent and identically distributed observations under the same model. Compute the maximum-likelihood estimate, its estimated standard error, and the Wald confidence interval afresh each time. The intended coverage statement is that approximately 95% of those random confidence intervals contain the fixed true parameter, provided the normal approximation is adequate.

This is a stage-structured population model with a census once per maturation interval. Only adults present at census t contribute to the juvenile cohort at census t+1; each contributes r new juveniles on average. Thus r is an effective fecundity, including any survival of offspring needed to reach the next census. Juveniles present at t do not contribute a separate birth term during this interval.
The term cyt​ consists of juveniles that survive and mature into adults by the next census, so c is their survival-to-maturity fraction. The term (1−d)zt​ consists of surviving adults; d is the adult mortality fraction per interval. All surviving juveniles leave the juvenile compartment in one step, adults remain adults, and there is no immigration or density dependence. The reproduction term is applied to adults counted at the start of the interval, rather than only to those surviving to its end.

A BLOSUM substitution matrix is estimated from conserved, ungapped blocks of related protein sequences. Closely related sequences are clustered to reduce overrepresentation, and aligned amino acid pairs from different clusters are counted. Let qij​ be their normalized observed pair frequency, and eij​ the frequency expected from independent draws at the background amino acid frequencies. With unordered pairs, eii​=pi2​ and eij​=2pi​pj​ for i=j.
For BLOSUM62, scores are rounded half-bit logarithms of these odds:
sij​≃2log2​(qij​/eij​)​.
(35)
A positive entry means that the aligned pair occurs more often in related sequences than the independent-background model predicts; a negative entry means less often. Adding these scores accumulates evidence across aligned positions. A score is neither a percentage identity nor a direct substitution probability.[ref]

A diagonal substitution matrix score measures enrichment over a background match, rather than simply rewarding an identical letter. tryptophan residues are comparatively rare, so a W–W match has a small chance background frequency pW2​. The number specifies the sequence-identity threshold used when clustering related sequences before deriving a BLOSUM substitution matrix. The gain comes from avoiding a full dynamic programming table for every target. BLAST searches for short, sufficiently high-scoring word matches between query and database, then extends promising seeds. Modern versions reserve expensive gapped refinement for candidates passing earlier filters. FASTA sequence search similarly starts from short word matches, collects promising runs along alignment diagonals, and refines or joins selected regions. Word lookup and database indexing make candidate discovery inexpensive compared with considering every pair of positions.[ref][ref]
These are heuristic algorithms: a good local sequence alignment can be missed if it has no acceptable seed, contains many interruptions, or is filtered before extension. Longer words and higher seed-score thresholds usually speed the search but reduce sensitivity; shorter words and looser thresholds recover weaker similarities at greater cost. Repetitive or compositionally biased regions can create many uninformative hits and erode both speed and specificity. No guarantee remains of finding the optimal sequence alignment for the chosen scoring scheme.
Thus the practical work depends on word length, thresholds, database composition and the number of hits. The speed-up is substantial on typical data, but does not establish a universal improved worst-case bound over O(mn): an input producing many candidate hits can still require extensive refinement.

A network null model is an ensemble representing a specified baseline against which to assess an observed biological network. It should preserve the features that would otherwise confound the test: at least the number of vertices and edges, and often the degree sequence, direction, edge type, or relevant spatial constraints. It should randomize the feature whose biological organization is under investigation. An ensemble that preserves that very feature cannot test whether the feature is unusual.
For example, test whether a measured protein-protein interaction network contains more closed three-protein interaction groups than can be explained by the interaction counts of individual proteins. Define vertices as the measured proteins and an undirected edge as a reproducible detected interaction, using a fixed assay threshold and treatment of duplicates. Preselect the triangle count as the test statistic. Generate many simple random graphs by degree-preserving edge rewiring, rejecting self-loops and duplicate edges, with enough accepted swaps and separation to obtain a well-mixed reference sample. This retains the observed degree sequence while disrupting higher-order association.
Compute the same triangle count for every reference graph. Compare the observed count with this distribution; an empirical upper-tail p-value can be reported as (1+#{Tnull​≥Tobs​})/(M+1) for M suitably sampled reference graphs. Report the size of the excess as well as its tail frequency, and check sensitivity to the assay threshold and any required connectivity constraint. A significant excess is evidence beyond the chosen degree baseline, while assay bias or spatial organization omitted from that baseline can still explain it.
A generative network model specifies a mechanism for producing networks, such as growth with preferential attachment, and asks what structures that mechanism creates. A network null model is defined by its role as a comparison baseline. These roles can overlap: the same random mechanism can be a proposed biological explanation in one analysis and a deliberately simplified null in another.

For the Erdős-Rényi model G(n,p), choose the number n of vertices and an edge probability 0≤p≤1. Visit every unordered pair of distinct vertices once and include its edge independently with probability p. This construction gives a simple graph with expected edge count p(2n​) and mean vertex degree p(n−1).
Increasing p at fixed n makes the graph denser, increases typical vertex degree and local clustering coefficient, and tends to shorten graph distances and improve connectivity. Increasing n at fixed p also increases the mean vertex degree; to retain a sparse comparison one instead decreases p approximately as 1/n.
Its degree distribution is binomial distribution, approximately Poisson distribution in the sparse large-n regime, so it is not a scale-free network. Connected realizations can have short average shortest path length, but their clustering coefficients are not elevated above the random baseline they themselves define. Thus they are not small-world networks under the high-clustering convention, although “small world” is sometimes used more loosely to mean only short distances.

For the Watts-Strogatz model, choose n vertices on a ring, an even initial vertex degree K<n, and a rewiring probability p. Connect each vertex to its K/2 nearest neighbours on each side. Consider each original clockwise edge once: with probability p, retain its starting endpoint and replace its other endpoint uniformly, excluding self-loops and duplicate edges. The edge count remains nK/2, although individual vertex degrees change.
Increasing n at fixed K lengthens unrewired-ring graph distances. Increasing K adds edges and shortens distances. Increasing p introduces shortcuts, sharply reducing average shortest path length at first, then progressively reducing the clustering coefficient by destroying local triangles.
The small-world network regime occurs at intermediate p with sufficient shortcuts, substantial clustering, and K≪n. At p=0 distances are long; near p=1 the clustering advantage disappears. The degree distribution remains concentrated around K, so this is not a scale-free network.[ref]

For the Barabási-Albert model, choose a connected seed graph on m0​ vertices, the number m≤m0​ of new edges per added vertex, and a final size n. A complete seed with m0​≥2 suffices. Add one vertex at a time, attaching it to m distinct existing vertices selected with weights proportional to their current vertex degrees. Exclude duplicate edges and self-loops.
Increasing n creates a larger network and permits older hubs to grow. Increasing m makes the graph denser, with asymptotic average vertex degree 2m, and tends to shorten graph distances. The seed affects early structure and the oldest hubs; standard linear preferential attachment gives a robust large-size tail exponent.
Its asymptotic degree distribution is P(k)∝k−3, so it is a scale-free network. Hubs produce short average shortest path length, but elevated clustering coefficients are not automatic: m=1 with a tree seed gives a triangle-free tree, and clustering decreases with size in the standard model. The strict clustered small-world network property is therefore not guaranteed.[ref]
