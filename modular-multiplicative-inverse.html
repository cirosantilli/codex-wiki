Modular multiplicative inverse (a−1(modn))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 176
For integers a and n>1, a modular multiplicative inverse is a congruence class b satisfying ab≡1(modn). It exists exactly when gcd(a,n)=1. Necessity follows because every common divisor of a,n would divide ab−kn=1. Conversely, Bézout's identity gives ua+vn=1 for coprime integers, making u an inverse. This is the basic bridge from greatest common divisor calculations to reversible modular arithmetic.
For example, 5⋅7−2⋅17=1, so 5−1≡7(mod17). The modular congruence 5x≡3(mod17) therefore has the unique solution class x≡21≡4(mod17). Multiplication by 5 sends all seventeen residue classes to different classes; multiplying by 7 reverses it. The inverse criterion and its algorithmic construction are presented in Ankur Moitra, MIT 18.200, 2024, modular inverses and Euclid's algorithm.
By contrast, 5 has no modular inverse modulo 15. Its products have only residues 0,5,10. Consequently 5x≡3(mod15) has no solution, while 5x≡10(mod15) has five solution classes, x≡2,5,8,11,14. Dividing by a noninvertible residue would discard this distinction. In a prime field Fp​, every nonzero residue is invertible; a general integer modulus instead gives a quotient ring that can have zero divisors.
