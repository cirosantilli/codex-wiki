Nuclear shell model

 Home Physics Nuclear physics Atomic nucleus
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 19
Why should adding one neutron be easy for one atomic nucleus and much less favorable for its neighbor? In the nuclear shell model, nucleons occupy discrete energy levels in a nuclear mean field. Filling all available states below a sufficiently large gap produces a nuclear shell closure. This connects nuclear separation energies with the counting of quantum states, and connects the spin of an odd atomic nucleus with its last unpaired nucleon.
The starting point is an approximate Hamiltonian operator
H=∑i=1A​[2mi​pi2​​+Ui​(ri​)−ai​(ri​)ℏ2Li​⋅Si​​]+Vres​,
(15)
where Ui​ is a confining potential energy, ai​ specifies nuclear spin-orbit splitting, and Vres​ contains interactions left out of the nuclear mean field. This is an effective organization of the nuclear many-body problem, rather than a derivation of the nuclear force. In a spherical approximation, addition of angular momentum combines the orbital angular momentum l with spin s=1/2 to give j=l±1/2; for l=0, only j=1/2 exists.
A level labelled nlj has 2j+1 magnetic substates mj​=−j,−j+1,…,j. The Pauli exclusion principle permits one neutron in each such state and, separately, one proton. Thus a j=7/2 level holds eight neutrons, or eight protons; it does not hold sixteen identical neutrons. Here n=1,2,… counts radial levels of the same l, starting with the lowest; it is not the hydrogenic principal quantum number. The letters s,p,d,f,g,h,i mean l=0,1,2,3,4,5,6.
