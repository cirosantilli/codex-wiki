Derivative criterion for monotonicity (f′≥0⟹f nondecreasing)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Real analysis Calculus Monotonic function
OurBigBook.com Words: 354 Articles: 1
Let I be a real interval, and let f:I→R be differentiable in its interior and continuous at any endpoints belonging to I. If the derivative satisfies f′(x)≥0 throughout the interior, then f is a nondecreasing function on I. For x<y in I, the mean value theorem supplies c∈(x,y) with
f(y)−f(x)=f′(c)(y−x)≥0.
(109)
Changing ≥0 to ≤0, >0, or <0 gives a nonincreasing function, strictly increasing function, or strictly decreasing function, respectively. The endpoint continuity assumption lets the mean value theorem apply even when x or y is an endpoint. [ref].
Conversely, if a nondecreasing function on a real interval is differentiable at an interior point x, then f′(x)≥0: the difference quotient (f(x+h)−f(x))/h is nonnegative for either sign of h. A differentiable nonincreasing function has derivative f′(x)≤0. Strict monotonicity still only forces these weak signs: the strictly increasing function x3 has derivative zero at zero. The sign of the derivative helps locate intervals of monotonicity when sketching a graph. [ref].
The real interval hypothesis matters because a connected subset of the real line contains every point between its inputs. On a disconnected domain of a function, positive derivatives need not give a nondecreasing function. For example, take
D=(−2,−1)∪(1,2),f(x)={x,x−10,​−2<x<−1,1<x<2.​
(110)
Here f′(x)=1 everywhere in D, but −3/2<3/2 while f(−3/2)=−3/2>−17/2=f(3/2). Each component has a strictly increasing function, yet their outputs are ordered the wrong way across the gap. This function is neither a nondecreasing function nor a nonincreasing function on all of D; the mean value theorem cannot cross the missing interval.
