Nambu superconductivity and chiral symmetry analogy

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum field theory Spontaneous symmetry breaking
OurBigBook.com Words: 441
Yoichiro Nambu's superconductivity work led to the spontaneous symmetry breaking ideas recognized by his share of the 2008 Nobel Prize in Physics.[ref] His 1960 treatment made the Meissner effect response consistent with gauge invariance by including collective pair contributions alongside the quasiparticles. The resulting Ward identity relates the interaction correction to the current and the self-energy; retaining the quasiparticles alone omits part of the response.[ref]
The individual Nobel lecture explains the bridge to physics: the superconductivity gap suggested a dynamically generated fermion mass, and the accompanying collective excitation suggested a light pion. It also notes that the long-range Coulomb interaction turns the charged phase response into a plasmon. A neutral global phase mode and a charged material therefore need different low-momentum descriptions.[ref]
In 1961, Yoichiro Nambu and Giovanni Jona-Lasinio supplied a four-fermion model with a self-consistent mass and a massless pseudoscalar bound state: an idealized pion. Their original constituents were nucleon fields. Their introduction explicitly distinguishes the zero-mass neutral collective excitation from its charged plasma counterpart and allows approximate chiral symmetry as the explanation of a nonzero pion mass. This is a model of dynamical symmetry breaking, rather than a microscopic derivation of Quantum chromodynamics.[ref]
In the modern two-light-quark account, chiral symmetry breaking leaves isospin intact and produces three Goldstone bosons in the exact non-anomalous chiral limit. Small quark masses explicitly break that chiral symmetry, so the observed pions are pseudo-Goldstone bosons. The chiral protection of a fermion mass identifies the distinct obstruction to inserting an explicit fermion mass into a chirally symmetric equation. The useful lesson is that massive individual excitations and much lighter collective excitations can coexist because they describe different motions of an interacting state.
A concrete experimental motivation for treating the electromagnetic response carefully is the Field-cooled Meissner experiment. MIT's realization cools lead in a solenoid field using helium gas. A nearby thermometer follows the cooling; a test coil feeds an integrator and oscilloscope. Expulsion gives a pulse, warming reverses it, and switching off the field while the sample is normal checks its sign. A change in alternating-field response alone can reflect normal electrical conductivity. This readout tests magnetic flux expulsion, not the spectrum or existence of a neutral Goldstone boson.[ref]
For a global symmetry in field theory, different orientations can be physically distinct states. A local gauge symmetry instead includes redundant descriptions of the same physical state. A gauge-dependent field expectation alone is consequently insufficient as a physical criterion for spontaneous symmetry breaking. The Higgs mechanism changes the physical mode content when the gauge field is dynamical; the global Goldstone theorem alone does not prove that mechanism. In particular, the massless direction of a gauge-fixed scalar potential need not appear as a separately observable massless particle.
