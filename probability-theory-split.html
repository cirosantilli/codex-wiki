Probability theory

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics
OurBigBook.com Words: 182k Articles: 3k
Probability theory describes uncertainty through a probability space: possible outcomes form a sample space, observable questions are events, and a probability measure assigns them numbers between zero and one. The Kolmogorov axioms make those assignments consistent. They let us combine information about several events, update it through conditional probability, and study random variables without listing every possible outcome separately.
For example, roll two dice whose results are independent random variables, each with a discrete uniform distribution on {1,…,6}. There are 36 equally likely ordered outcomes. Only (1,6),(2,5),…,(6,1) have sum seven, so that event has probability 6/36=1/6. The sum itself is not uniformly distributed: sum two has only one outcome. Choosing a probability distribution for the elementary outcomes is part of the model; equal likelihood does not follow merely from counting the named possibilities.
The subject becomes especially useful when complete outcome lists are enormous or infinite. Independence turns a joint calculation into simpler pieces, expected value summarizes a random variable, and the law of large numbers explains how averages can become predictable even when individual observations are uncertain. The central limit theorem describes the scale and shape of many remaining fluctuations. These are distinct claims: a small variance of an average does not make every observation close to its expected value.
There is a satisfying unity here: dice, hidden causes, and Brownian motion all use the same rules for events and conditional probability. A model still needs justification. Its exact deductions describe what its assumptions imply; comparison with observations belongs to statistical inference.
