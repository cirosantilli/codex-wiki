Chernoff information (C(P,Q))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Information theory Binary hypothesis testing in information theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 744 Articles: 4
Chernoff information measures how quickly repeated independent evidence can distinguish two fixed probability distributions. Suppose a statistical hypothesis test observes n independent and identically distributed random variables, with common law P under one simple hypothesis and Q under the other. Give the two hypotheses fixed positive prior probabilities, and let pn∗​ be the smallest achievable average error probability. For distinct positive probability mass functions on a common finite alphabet,
limn→∞​−n1​lnpn∗​=C(P,Q),C(P,Q)=−min0≤s≤1​ln∑x​P(x)sQ(x)1−s.
(64)
Here ln is the natural logarithm, so the error exponent is measured in nats per observation. This optimal rate is unchanged by choosing different fixed positive prior probabilities; the best decision rule and finite-sample error can still change.[ref]
The interpretation is pn∗​=exp(−nC(P,Q)+o(n)): a larger Chernoff information means fewer observations are needed at the leading exponential scale. It does not assert the exact finite-sample identity pn∗​=e−nC(P,Q). The finite-sample Chernoff error bound and the explicit Chernoff information for complementary Bernoulli distributions make this distinction concrete. This is useful when designing repeated measurements or comparing sensors whose output probability distributions differ under two candidate conditions.
For probability mass functions with zero entries, define the affinity for 0<s<1 and use C(P,Q)=−lninf0<s<1​∑x​P(x)sQ(x)1−s, taking endpoint limits when needed. This avoids an ambiguous 00 convention. Identical laws give C=0; disjoint probability supports give C=∞, because one observation separates them perfectly. The displayed finite-rate theorem concerns fixed laws with common positive support, not laws that change with n or arbitrarily dependent repeated observations.
