Reynolds number (Re=ρUL/μ=UL/ν)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics Dynamic viscosity
OurBigBook.com Words: 249
The Reynolds number Re=UL/ν compares inertial and viscous effects. Here U is a characteristic velocity, L a characteristic length, μ the dynamic viscosity, ρ the mass density, and ν=μ/ρ the kinematic viscosity. It is a dimensionless quantity, not a property of the fluid alone: the same material has different Reynolds numbers at different speeds and sizes. Specify the chosen scales when reporting it.
The comparison comes from the Navier-Stokes equation. For velocity variations on scale L, convective acceleration is of order U2/L, while the viscous term is of order νU/L2. Their ratio is UL/ν. Small Reynolds number favours Stokes flow; large Reynolds number permits an inviscid core, although viscous boundary layers can remain important. Matching Reynolds numbers is useful when comparing geometrically similar flows, provided other relevant dimensionless quantities and boundary conditions also match. [ref]
In the constriction example under incompressible flow, use the mean axial velocity and pipe diameter, L=D=2R. Taking ρ=1000kgm−3 and a stipulated dynamic viscosity μ=0.001Pas gives
Re1​=0.0011000(0.20)(0.020)​=4000,Re2​=0.0011000(0.80)(0.010)​=8000.
(11)
Halving the radius at fixed volumetric flow rate doubles this diameter-based Reynolds number, because mean velocity increases fourfold. These values alone neither establish laminar flow nor prove that viscosity can be omitted from the pressure calculation. Increasing μ to 0.10Pas with the same imposed flow gives 40 and 80, but strengthens viscous effects; small Reynolds number does not justify Bernoulli's principle.
The name recalls Osborne Reynolds's 1883 experimental investigation of water motion and resistance in pipes. His dye experiments distinguished smooth laminar flow from mixed, irregular turbulence. [ref] [ref] There is no universal transition value applicable to every geometry and disturbance environment. [ref]
