For five points, partition the unit square into four squares of side 1/2. Assign boundary points to just one neighbouring piece. By the pigeonhole principle, two of the points lie in one piece, whose diameter is its diagonal, (1/2)2+(1/2)2​=2​/2.
For eight points, use seven smaller-diameter pieces rather than eight rectangles. Start with the convex pentagon
P=conv{(0,0),(1/2,0),(1/2,1/4),(1/4,1/2),(0,1/4)}.
(17)
Take its four reflected copies at the corners of the square. The remaining pieces are the left triangle with vertices (0,1/4),(1/4,1/2),(0,3/4), its right-hand reflection, and the central diamond with vertices (1/4,1/2),(1/2,1/4),(3/4,1/2),(1/2,3/4). These seven polygons cover the square with disjoint interiors. To verify coverage directly, for 0≤y≤1/4 the two bottom pentagons split the horizontal section at x=1/2. For 1/4≤y≤1/2, the five successive sections have endpoints 0,y−1/4,3/4−y,y+1/4,5/4−y,1 and belong respectively to the left triangle, bottom-left pentagon, diamond, bottom-right pentagon and right triangle. Reflection gives the top half.
The diameter of a finite convex hull is the greatest distance between its vertices: if two points are convex combinations of vertices, their difference is a convex combination of vertex differences, and the triangle inequality bounds its norm by the largest such distance. Checking the five vertices of P gives diameter (1/2)2+(1/4)2​=5​/4; reflections preserve that value. Each remaining triangle and the diamond has diameter 1/2, which is smaller. After assigning shared boundaries to one piece, eight points in these seven pieces force a pair in a common piece. Hence some pair is at distance at most 5​/4.
Both arguments are instances of a pigeonhole distance bound from a partition.
