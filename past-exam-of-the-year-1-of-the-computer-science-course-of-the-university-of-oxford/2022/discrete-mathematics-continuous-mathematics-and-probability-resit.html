Let A and B be the finite sets of objects satisfying the two properties. The rule of sum says that mutually exclusive alternatives have additive counts: if A∩B=∅, then ∣A∪B∣=∣A∣+∣B∣. Without disjointness, the corresponding inclusion-exclusion principle is
∣A∪B∣=∣A∣+∣B∣−∣A∩B∣.
(1)
The rule of subtraction counts objects satisfying the first property but not the second:
∣A∖B∣=∣A∣−∣A∩B∣.​
(2)
In particular, if the objects with the second property form a subset of those with the first, the count is ∣A∣−∣B∣. These conditions prevent either double counting an overlap or subtracting objects that were not counted initially.

The rule of product says that if a construction has r stages, with exactly aj​ choices at stage j after every permitted earlier history, then it has ∏j=1r​aj​ possible outcomes. The later choices need not be identical sets after different histories; their numbers must have the stated values.
For a permutation of n distinct objects, choose the first object in n ways, the second in n−1 ways, and continue until only one remains. Thus the number is
n(n−1)⋯1=n!.​
(3)
Every ordered arrangement is produced exactly once by its successive choices, so this counts permutations without omissions or repetitions. For n=0, the unique empty arrangement agrees with 0!=1.

An allocation is a tuple (x1​,…,xk​) of nonnegative integers with ∑j​xj​=n, where xj​ is the number in labelled box j. Empty boxes are allowed. Encode the allocation by n stars and k−1 bars: put x1​ stars before the first bar, x2​ between the first two bars, and so on. Adjacent bars represent an empty intermediate box; a bar at an end represents an empty end box.
Reading the group sizes recovers exactly one allocation from every such string, so this is a bijection. There are n+k−1 positions and we choose the k−1 bar positions. Hence stars and bars gives
(k−1n+k−1​).​
(4)
No extra factor for ordering the objects is present, because the objects are indistinguishable. For k=1 the string has no bars and gives the single allocation of all objects to that box; for n=0 all boxes are empty, also a single allocation.

The statement f(n)=O(g(n)) in Big O notation means that some C>0 and integer N satisfy ∣f(n)∣≤C∣g(n)∣ for all n≥N. The statement f(n)=Ω(g(n)) in big omega notation means that some c>0 and N satisfy ∣f(n)∣≥c∣g(n)∣ for all n≥N.

c∣g(n)∣≤∣f(n)∣≤C∣g(n)∣(n≥N).​

Suppose first that f=O(g). Choose C>0 and N so that ∣f(n)∣≤C∣g(n)∣ for n≥N. There are only finitely many natural numbers below N. Because g(n)=0 at each of them, all ratios ∣f(n)∣/∣g(n)∣ in this finite prefix are finite. Let
c=max({C}∪{∣g(n)∣∣f(n)∣​:n<N}).
(7)
Then ∣f(n)∣≤c∣g(n)∣ both in the prefix and in the tail, hence on the entire domain of a function. This proves the finite-prefix extension of a big O bound.
Conversely, a bound valid for every natural number is certainly valid eventually. The purported real constant cannot be negative because g never vanishes; if it is zero, enlarge it to a positive constant. The definition of Big O notation then applies. The nonvanishing assumption is what permits the finite exceptions to be absorbed into one constant.

The pigeonhole principle states that if more than r objects are placed in r boxes, with r≥1, at least one box contains two or more objects. Equivalently, a function from a finite set of more than r elements to a set of r elements cannot be injective: if every box held at most one object, there would be at most r objects altogether.

For five points, partition the unit square into four squares of side 1/2. Assign boundary points to just one neighbouring piece. By the pigeonhole principle, two of the points lie in one piece, whose diameter is its diagonal, (1/2)2+(1/2)2​=2​/2.
For eight points, use seven smaller-diameter pieces rather than eight rectangles. Start with the convex pentagon
P=conv{(0,0),(1/2,0),(1/2,1/4),(1/4,1/2),(0,1/4)}.
(17)
Take its four reflected copies at the corners of the square. The remaining pieces are the left triangle with vertices (0,1/4),(1/4,1/2),(0,3/4), its right-hand reflection, and the central diamond with vertices (1/4,1/2),(1/2,1/4),(3/4,1/2),(1/2,3/4). These seven polygons cover the square with disjoint interiors. To verify coverage directly, for 0≤y≤1/4 the two bottom pentagons split the horizontal section at x=1/2. For 1/4≤y≤1/2, the five successive sections have endpoints 0,y−1/4,3/4−y,y+1/4,5/4−y,1 and belong respectively to the left triangle, bottom-left pentagon, diamond, bottom-right pentagon and right triangle. Reflection gives the top half.
The diameter of a finite convex hull is the greatest distance between its vertices: if two points are convex combinations of vertices, their difference is a convex combination of vertex differences, and the triangle inequality bounds its norm by the largest such distance. Checking the five vertices of P gives diameter (1/2)2+(1/4)2​=5​/4; reflections preserve that value. Each remaining triangle and the diamond has diameter 1/2, which is smaller. After assigning shared boundaries to one piece, eight points in these seven pieces force a pair in a common piece. Hence some pair is at distance at most 5​/4.
Both arguments are instances of a pigeonhole distance bound from a partition.

Assume f and g are continuously differentiable near the point. At a regular feasible point, meaning g(x)=0 and ∇g(x)=0, a differentiable constrained local extremum satisfies the Lagrange multiplier equations
∇f(x)=λ∇g(x),g(x)=0.
(22)
