Check each partial order axiom. For every (a,b), a≤a and b≥b, so S is a reflexive relation. If both (a,b)S(c,d) and (c,d)S(a,b), the first coordinates satisfy a≤c≤a and the second coordinates satisfy b≥d≥b. Therefore a=c and b=d, which proves S is an antisymmetric relation.
Finally, if (a,b)S(c,d) and (c,d)S(e,f), then a≤c≤e and b≥d≥f. Hence (a,b)S(e,f), making S a transitive relation. All three axioms hold, so S is a partial order. It is a product order, with the usual order on the first coordinate and the reversed order on the second.

A subset is determined uniquely by deciding, for each of the n elements, whether to include it. Each decision has two possibilities. By the rule of product, there are 2⋅2⋯2=2n decision strings. The correspondence between such binary strings and subsets is a bijection, so ∣P(U)∣=2n. The all-zero string corresponds to the empty subset.

For q=1,…,5, choose the first q elements of {1,2,4,8,16}. Every sum of a subset has a unique binary representation, so distinct subsets cannot have the same sum. To see this directly, take the largest power of two belonging to exactly one of two proposed subsets. It exceeds the sum of all smaller powers, and therefore cannot be cancelled by their difference. Thus these chosen sets are dissociated sets and do not have the two-sum property.
If cardinality zero is included, the empty set has only one subset, so it cannot supply two distinct ones either. There is a counterexample at every cardinality from 0 through 5.

Fix any seven-element subset S. Its power set supplies 27=128 pigeons. Map each subset to its integer sum. Since the elements are positive, all such sums lie between 0 and ∑s∈S​s. The latter is at most the sum of the seven largest available elements,

A direct search over every q-element subset must inspect (qn​) candidates. Testing one candidate by generating its subset sums creates 2q values; storing previously seen sums detects a collision. Generating them incrementally needs O(2q) arithmetic operations, or O(q2q) for the straightforward independent calculation of each sum. Thus the simple overall search has cost on the scale of (qn​)2q, apart from integer-arithmetic and data-structure costs.
The important time complexity distinction is that fixed q gives polynomial growth in n, of degree q, but this can still become impractically large. If q increases with n, both the candidate count and the internal power set can grow exponentially. For example, near q=n/2 the binomial coefficient is already exponentially large. An exhaustive implementation can therefore encounter a combinatorial explosion; the fixed-q estimate Θ(nq) must not be described as exponential in n.

Use the standard spacing convention for machine epsilon: the next representable floating-point number above 1 is 1+2−40. In a normalized binary representation this corresponds to 41 significant binary digits, including the leading digit. The spacing throughout [1,2) is therefore 2−40.
With rounding to nearest, the associated unit roundoff is half that spacing, u=2−41; for a normal result the relative round-off error is bounded by u. Some texts instead call u machine epsilon. Here 2−40 denotes the spacing scale, and the iteration counts below use that convention. It describes representation precision, not a guarantee that an arbitrarily evaluated function has a relative error this small.

Separate inactive and active optimization constraints. At an interior feasible point with h>0, a differentiable local minimum must satisfy ∇f=0. Retain only solutions lying in the interior.
On the boundary h=0, if ∇h=0, apply the Lagrange multiplier condition. Using the optimization Lagrangian L=f−λh, the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions are
∇f=λ∇h,h≥0,λ≥0,λh=0.
(25)
The sign follows from feasible motion into h>0: the directional derivative along ∇h at a minimum cannot be negative. Thus a boundary candidate has h=0 and a nonnegative Lagrange multiplier. Boundary points with ∇h=0 must be examined separately, because the usual constraint qualification fails there.
Solve the interior and boundary systems, check membership in the feasible set, and establish which candidates are minima using second-order tests or a direct global comparison. The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions alone are not sufficient for general nonconvex functions or feasible sets. On an unbounded feasible set, also check whether an infimum is attained and whether escaping sequences can improve the objective.
