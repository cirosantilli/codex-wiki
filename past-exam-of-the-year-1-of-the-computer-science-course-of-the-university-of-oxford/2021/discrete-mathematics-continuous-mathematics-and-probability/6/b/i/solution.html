A practical choice is a BFGS method, using exact gradients and a line search. At dimension d=100, its d-by-d inverse-Hessian matrix approximation is small, whereas forming a million rank-one contributions to the exact Hessian matrix is expensive. If F(x)=∑i​e∥x−ai​∥2, the explicit derivatives are
∇F=2∑i​e∥x−ai​∥2(x−ai​),HF​=∑i​e∥x−ai​∥2(4(x−ai​)(x−ai​)T+2I).
(38)
The positive-definite Hessian matrix and coercivity show that the objective has one global minimizer.
For numerical stability one can minimize Φ=logF instead, which has exactly the same minimizer. Put ri​=∥x−ai​∥2, wi​=eri​/∑j​erj​, and aˉ=∑i​wi​ai​. Then the logarithmic gradient of an exponential squared-distance sum gives
g(x):=∇Φ(x)=2(x−aˉ),∇2Φ=2I+4∑i​wi​(ai​−aˉ)(ai​−aˉ)T⪰2I.
(39)
Evaluate the weights as eri​−rmax​/∑j​erj​−rmax​ to avoid floating-point overflow. A quasi-Newton method step is
xn+1​=xn​−αn​Hn​g(xn​),
(40)
where Hn​ is the positive-definite inverse-Hessian matrix approximation updated by BFGS, and αn​ is chosen by a line search, for example one satisfying the Wolfe conditions. Under the standard smoothness and nondegeneracy assumptions at this strongly convex minimizer, full-memory BFGS has local superlinear convergence. An exact Newton's method in optimization would have local quadratic convergence, but pays for those much more expensive exact second derivatives.[ref]
