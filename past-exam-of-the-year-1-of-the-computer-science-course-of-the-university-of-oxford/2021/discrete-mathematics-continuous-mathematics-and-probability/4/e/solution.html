For negative x, the mean value theorem gives f′(x)=3+xf′′(ξx​). In particular,
f′(−2)≤−1<0,f′(−1)>0.
(21)
Since f′ is continuous and strictly increasing, it vanishes at exactly one point xm​∈(−2,−1). The positive second derivative makes f a strictly convex function on the entire real line. Its derivative changes from negative to positive at xm​, so xm​ is the unique global minimum. One may also use f(x)≥x2+3x−5→∞ to verify coercivity.
In the endpoint-bracketing form of golden-section search, begin with [−2,−1], a width-one interval known to contain this unique minimum, and place the two standard interior probes. The attainable position accuracy is different from that for a simple root. Around xm​, Taylor's theorem gives
f(xm​+h)−f(xm​)=21​f′′(ξ)h2.
(22)
Here the minimum value is nonzero and of order one: the lower quadratic bound gives f(xm​)≥−29/4, while f(xm​)≤f(−1)<−13/2. Ordinary relative round-off error in these function values therefore hides changes of order ϵ, once h2 has that size. The function-value resolution near a nondegenerate minimum is consequently of order
∣h∣∼ϵ​=2−20≈9.54×10−7.​
(23)
Each golden-section search contraction multiplies the bracket width by ρ=(5​−1)/2. Using 2−20 as the nominal target width gives
N≥−logρ20log2​=28.81…,N=29 contractions.​
(24)
Unknown evaluation-error constants can change the actual stopping point; 29 is the standard scale-based count. Requiring 2−40 position accuracy from these function-value comparisons would disregard the quadratic flatness of the minimum. The same precision effect is documented for one-dimensional numerical minimization.[ref]
