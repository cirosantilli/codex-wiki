Fix any seven-element subset S. Its power set supplies 27=128 pigeons. Map each subset to its integer sum. Since the elements are positive, all such sums lie between 0 and ∑s∈S​s. The latter is at most the sum of the seven largest available elements,
10+11+⋯+16=91.
(13)
There are therefore at most 92 possible sums, fewer than the 128 subsets. The pigeonhole principle forces two distinct subsets to have the same sum. Every seven-element subset has the two-sum property. This is the subset-sum pigeonhole bound. The weaker estimate ∑s∈S​s≤7⋅16=112 would also suffice, since 128>113.
