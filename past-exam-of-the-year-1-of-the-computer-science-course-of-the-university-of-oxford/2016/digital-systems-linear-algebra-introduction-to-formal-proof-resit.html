Use a four-state Moore machine. In state A wait for an outer-sensor activation; B combines the outer-only and both-sensors phases; C records that the visitor has reached the inner-only phase; I emits the one-cycle count pulse. Only I has INC = 1.
The complete state transition table, with input order x1​x0​, is
state    00    01    11    10    INC
 A        A     B     B     A      0
 B        A     B     B     C      0
 C        I     B     B     C      0
 I        A     B     B     A      1
For a normal entry the states are A→B→B→C→I; retreat after the first sensor instead gives A→B→A with no pulse. Repeated samples at one sensor remain in the corresponding phase. State I always exits on the next edge, so its output lasts exactly one clock period; an outer activation on that next edge goes directly to B, allowing the next visitor to start immediately.
This follows the question's single-visitor entry model and merges the two outer-active phases as its hint requests. Inputs are sampled synchronously; the table also specifies deterministic recovery for other sampled combinations. It is not a specification of a bidirectional occupancy counter for arbitrary overlapping entry and exit traffic.

The Gram-Schmidt process constructs the vectors in order:
vj​=xj​−∑i<j​⟨qi​,xj​⟩qi​,qj​=∥vj​∥vj​​,j=1,…,k.
(12)
The empty sum at j=1 is zero. Each subtraction removes the projection onto one previous unit vector, so vj​ is orthogonal to their span. Linear independence of the inputs ensures vj​=0 in exact arithmetic, and normalization gives unit length.
In floating-point arithmetic, subtracting nearly equal projections can amplify roundoff and cause loss of orthogonality, especially for nearly dependent columns. Modified Gram-Schmidt, reorthogonalization, or Householder QR factorization can improve numerical behaviour; no implementation can remove the underlying sensitivity of nearly dependent input data.

In the column convention used for QR factorization, an m×k real matrix has orthonormal columns when QTQ=Ik​. This says that each column has unit norm and distinct columns have zero inner product. If Q is square, then QTQ=I provides a left inverse; a square matrix with a left inverse is invertible, so Q−1=QT, and also QQT=I. Such a square matrix is an orthogonal matrix. A rectangular matrix with orthonormal columns generally has no two-sided inverse.

For an m×n matrix, its row space is the span of its rows, a subspace of Rn; its column space is the span of its columns, a subspace of Rm. Its null space is kerA={x∈Rn:Ax=0}. The rank of a matrix is the dimension of its column space, equal to the dimension of its row space. The rank-nullity theorem gives dimkerA=n−rankA.

Implication introduction assumes ϕ temporarily, derives ψ, and discharges that assumption to conclude ϕ→ψ. Disjunction elimination starts from ϕ∨ψ and derives the same conclusion κ in two separate branches, one assuming ϕ and the other assuming ψ.
The boxes delimit subproofs: a locally assumed formula is available inside its box but cannot be used unconditionally outside it. Discharging a box records exactly which dependency has been removed. The proofs below use a vertical bar to mark each active box.

In a finite domain, an existential quantifier says that at least one candidate satisfies ϕ. Reason with a fresh name v for such an unspecified witness. If the same conclusion κ follows using only ϕ(v) and the original premises, without depending on which candidate v names, then κ follows from the existential premise itself.
This is exactly existential elimination in boxed form: the sequent Γ,ϕ(v)⊢κ is the branch inside the box. The eigenvariable v must not occur free in the original undischarged context or in the exported conclusion κ. Otherwise the proof could smuggle in information about a specially chosen individual or export a fact about a witness whose name has just left scope. Freshness does not mean that the domain contains an additional object; it means that its temporary name carries no other assumptions.

1  ∃y ∀x R(x,y)      premise
2  | ∀x R(x,b)       assumption, b fresh
3  | R(a,b)          ∀-elim 2, with a arbitrary
4  | ∃y R(a,y)       ∃-intro 3
5  | ∀x ∃y R(x,y)    ∀-intro 4
6  ∀x ∃y R(x,y)      ∃-elim 1,2–5
In both proofs a is absent from the original premise. In the second it is also absent from the witness assumption, whose x is bound; universal generalization is therefore legal inside that box. The exported conclusions contain no free b.
The converse is not valid. On the domain {0,1}, interpret R(x,y) as x=y. For each x, choose y=x, so ∀x∃yR(x,y) holds. Neither single choice of y equals both 0 and 1, so ∃y∀xR(x,y) fails. This is the distinction between a pointwise witness and a uniform witness.

The proposed proof is invalid: its existential-elimination step exports Q(x0​) while the witness name x0​ still occurs free in that conclusion. Introducing an existential quantifier later does not retrospectively repair that scope error.
Introduce the existential quantifier before discharging the witness box:
1  ∀x (P(x) → Q(x))   premise
2  ∃x P(x)            premise
3  | P(a)             assumption, a fresh
4  | P(a) → Q(a)      ∀-elim 1
5  | Q(a)             →-elim 4,3
6  | ∃x Q(x)          ∃-intro 5
7  ∃x Q(x)            ∃-elim 2,3–6
The conclusion of existential elimination now has no free occurrence of a, and the name occurs in neither original premise, satisfying the eigenvariable condition.
