The halting problem asks, given the encoding ⟨M,w⟩ of a Turing machine and an input string, whether that machine eventually halts on that input. Its formal language is
HALT={⟨M,w⟩:M halts on w}.
(72)
Suppose a Turing machine H decided this formal language. Construct another Turing machine D that, on an input encoding ⟨M⟩, runs H on ⟨M,⟨M⟩⟩. If H answers that M halts on its own encoding, D enters an infinite loop. If H answers that M does not halt, D halts. Handling malformed encodings by immediate rejection makes D well-defined on arbitrary strings.
Now run D on its own encoding. If H says that D halts, the construction makes D loop; if H says that D does not halt, the construction makes D halt. Either answer contradicts the assumed correctness of H. Therefore
HALT is not a decidable language.​
(73)
The contradiction concerns a decider that must give a correct terminating answer for every encoded machine and input. Simulating M does detect a halt when one occurs, but cannot in general certify that a running computation will continue forever.
