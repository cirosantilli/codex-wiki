First, the product order is a partial order. Reflexivity and transitivity hold in each coordinate. If (p,q)≤R​(p′,q′) and (p′,q′)≤R​(p,q), antisymmetry in P,Q gives p=p′ and q=q′, proving antisymmetry in the product.
Let D be a nonempty directed set in P×Q. Its coordinate projections
DP​={p:∃q(p,q)∈D},DQ​={q:∃p(p,q)∈D}
(60)
are directed sets. For example, if p1​,p2​∈DP​, choose (p1​,q1​),(p2​,q2​)∈D. Directedness supplies (p3​,q3​)∈D above both, so p3​∈DP​ is a common upper bound of p1​,p2​ inside DP​. The argument for DQ​ is identical.
By the assumed directed-complete partial order property, p∗​=supDP​ and q∗​=supDQ​ exist. Every (p,q)∈D satisfies p≤P​p∗​ and q≤Q​q∗​, so (p∗​,q∗​) is an upper bound of D. If (u,v) is any other upper bound, then u bounds DP​ and v bounds DQ​. Hence p∗​≤P​u, q∗​≤Q​v, proving leastness. Thus
supD=(supDP​,supDQ​),P×Q is directed-complete.​
(61)
The printed definition of a directed set also permits D=∅.[ref] Under that convention, the hypotheses require P,Q to have least elements, namely the least upper bounds of their empty subsets. Their pair is the least element of the product and is sup∅ there. This handles the empty case as well; under the convention restricting directed sets to nonempty sets, only the preceding nonempty argument is needed.
