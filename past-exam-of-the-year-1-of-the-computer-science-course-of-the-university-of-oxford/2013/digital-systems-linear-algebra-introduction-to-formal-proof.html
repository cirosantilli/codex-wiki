A disjunctive normal form, also called a sum of products, is an OR of AND terms, each made from Boolean literals such as a or aˉ. For example,
f(a,b,c)=ab+aˉc.
(1)
Here juxtaposition means logical conjunction, + means logical disjunction, and a bar means negation. The expression need not be canonical: a term can omit variables, and need not name a single row of the truth table.
Implement each product by an AND gate and combine the products with an OR gate. Supply complemented inputs with NOT gates. Thus
sum of products⟷AND layer followed by OR layer.​
(2)
This is the usual two-level combinational logic realization, counting complemented inputs as available. If NOT gates are counted, a complemented input can traverse three gates. The two-level depth statement also assumes sufficiently large gate fan-in: using only two-input logic gates requires trees for long products or sums. Constant, single-product and single-literal Boolean functions may need fewer levels, so the two-level construction is a general bound, not a claim that every expression requires exactly two layers.

A serial-in parallel-out shift register is a chain of digital registers driven by the same clock signal. At each active clock edge, the first stage captures the input and every later stage captures the preceding stage's old value. Its parallel outputs expose the successive delayed samples simultaneously.
Let an​ be the current sample and, immediately before its sampling edge, let h1​=an−1​,…,hk−1​=an−k+1​. Feed these signals to combinational logic implementing f. Then
zn​=f(an​,h1​,…,hk−1​)=f(an​,an−1​,…,an−k+1​).​
(3)
Only k−1 stored history stages are needed if the current input is used directly. Alternatively, a k-stage shift register exposes all k samples after the edge. A separate output digital register can capture the first arrangement at the edge, giving a fully sampled output. Initialize the history, or regard the first k−1 cycles as a filling interval.

Use two history D flip-flops. Write a=an​, b=an−1​ and c=an−2​ for the signals just before the active clock edge; update the history by b+=a and c+=b. Two or more of the three samples are one exactly when some pair are both one, so the majority function is
zn​=ab+ac+bc.​
(4)
Three two-input AND gates and one three-input OR gate implement this disjunctive normal form. If a registered output is wanted, feed the expression to the D input of another D flip-flop and capture it at the same edge as the history update. All right-hand sides use the pre-edge values, so no extra sample of delay is introduced.
The three terms are necessary in a minimal sum of products: the input triples 110, 101 and 011 must each be covered, and a single product covering two of them would also cover a triple having only one one. Thus the minimum is three products, each with two Boolean literals.

The unconstrained cases can be treated as don't-care conditions during Boolean function minimization. One particularly small, deliberately asymmetric choice is
q+=ab+cq.​
(6)
Use the same two history D flip-flops and one output D flip-flop, with this expression at the output D input. It requires two two-input AND gates and one OR gate.
To verify every required case, first set q=0. If zero occurs at least twice among a,b,c, then ab=0, so q+=0 as required. If all three samples are one, then ab=1, so q+=1. The remaining triples have exactly two ones and hence exactly one sample agreeing with the old zero; either output is allowed.
Now set q=1. If at least two samples are one, either c=1 or a=b=1, giving q+=1. If all three samples are zero, both terms vanish, giving q+=0. The remaining triples have exactly one one and are the allowed don't-care conditions. Thus the expression meets both requirements, including every constant-input triple.
For minimality, consider these required one-entries in (a,b,c,q) order: 1110, 1101, 1011 and 0111 have no common literal. Therefore a product covering all four would be the constant one, which violates required zero-entries. Also no single-literal product is safe: each of a,b,c,q is one at some required zero-entry, and each complemented literal is one at 0000. Hence at least two products with at least two Boolean literals each are needed. The displayed expression attains two products and four literals.

The opcode occupies bits 31:26, the arithmetic logic unit operation is wired directly to 15:10, and an immediate operand uses all of 9:0. A three-register instruction needs fifteen register-selection bits, whereas an immediate instruction must retain independent source and destination selectors outside 9:0.
Thus put rs​ and rd​ in the ten bits 25:16, and place rt​ in five of 9:0, where it can overlap the unused immediate operand of a register instruction. One valid instruction encoding is
rs​=I[25:21],rd​=I[20:16],rt​=I[9:5].​
(8)
Bits 4:0 are unused for a three-register instruction. In an immediate instruction, rt​ has no effect because its read value is deselected by the ToAlu multiplexer. In a store, rs​ selects the address and rt​ selects the data; rd​ must be zero to suppress the incidental register write. These observations follow the original datapath, particularly its direct vals​ memory-address connection and the connection from the ToAlu output to memory write data.[ref]

A hard disk drive pays substantial seek time to position the head and rotational latency to reach the desired sector. After that positioning, neighboring bytes stream past the head quickly. A larger transfer therefore amortizes positioning and command overhead over many bytes.
The physical sector also needs framing, synchronization and error-correcting redundancy. Transferring one byte at a time would spend an excessive fraction of the medium and controller work on that overhead. A controller buffer and a block transfer can efficiently deliver the sector to computer memory, often using direct memory access.
Thus
larger transfers amortize positioning, framing and command costs.​
(14)
The choice is a compromise: excessively large blocks waste transfer time for small requests and can increase internal fragmentation in a file system.

Reusing a block-oriented file system preserves existing operating-system interfaces, tools, consistency procedures and on-disk formats. It also permits common buffering and caching policies. Large transfers can still amortize software and controller overhead, and flash memory itself has page-program and erase-block constraints, so a block device abstraction is often useful even without mechanical motion.
The costs are that a fine-grained change may require reading and rewriting a larger logical block, and a small computer file can waste part of its final file data block. Structures optimized to reduce seek time need not be best for non-volatile memory. For flash memory, repeated metadata updates and erasing or relocating larger regions can increase physical writes and consume finite program/erase endurance. A translation layer can hide these constraints but adds mapping space and work.
Consequently
compatibility and reuse trade against wasted space, extra writes and missed device-specific optimizations.​
(15)
The balance depends on the actual medium: byte-addressable non-volatile memory and page/erase-based flash memory have different physical constraints.

This is linked file allocation. A file header holds the first and last disk block numbers, and the successor field in each block extends the chain. The header therefore does not impose a fixed small maximum number of blocks per file.
To state a numerical bound, suppose a physical block has B bytes, the successor-or-final-count field occupies h bytes, and N distinct data blocks are both addressable and available. A full block then holds C=B−h payload bytes. If the final count can represent every value up to C,
L≤NC=N(B−h).​
(16)
A smaller computer file can use an arbitrary number of full payload blocks followed by a partial block, so allocation need not be contiguous. With a p-bit disk block number, N≤2p, reduced by reserved addresses and blocks consumed by other data and metadata. This gives the address-width bound as well as the actual device-space bound.
There is no positive minimum payload size: an empty computer file can be represented by an empty-chain convention or a zero-count terminal block. The question does not specify which representation is used or the relevant field widths, so it does not determine an absolute byte limit or a particular empty-file encoding. Its description presupposes a convention that distinguishes a successor value from a terminal byte count.

The predecessor disk block number turns the forward chain into a doubly linked list. Starting from the header's last block, one can traverse backwards; removing or detaching the current block can find its predecessor directly rather than scanning from the beginning. When modifying a chain, the affected predecessor and successor links must both be updated consistently.
The file number identifies the owner of each file data block. A file-system consistency checker can scan the medium, group blocks by owner, detect a cross-link between different files, and help reconstruct chains or headers after damage. It also lets the system check that a block reached through a chain belongs to the expected file.
Thus
predecessor: reverse traversal and editing; owner: validation and recovery.​
(17)
The extra metadata aids recovery but does not by itself guarantee file-system crash consistency.

A traditional UNIX-like file system identifies a computer file by an inode number. Its inode records file metadata, such as byte length, ownership and permissions, and contains direct file data block addresses plus indirect block pointers. A file-system directory maps a name to the inode number; the filename need not be stored inside the inode.
In the familiar direct/single/double/triple scheme, a single indirect block pointer reaches a block of data-block addresses, a double indirect block pointer reaches blocks of those addresses, and a triple indirect block pointer adds one more level. If there are d direct pointers, one pointer of each indirect level, block size B bytes and pointer width h bytes, put q=⌊B/h⌋. The index structure can name
K=d+q+q2+q3
(18)
data blocks, giving
Lindex​=B(d+q+q2+q3).​
(19)
The actual maximum is the minimum of this structural bound, the representable file offset/length bound, the addressable data-block space, and the capacity left on the device after allocating metadata and index blocks. For an unsigned ℓ-bit byte-length field, its bound is 2ℓ−1 bytes. An empty computer file is permitted and needs no data blocks in this scheme. Exact numerical limits cannot be given without the inode layout and field widths; different UNIX-like implementations choose different layouts, and some use extent trees rather than this traditional scheme.

Let a disk block number and a file number each occupy h bytes. System B spends about 3h bytes per physical block on successor/count, predecessor and owner, leaving B−3h payload bytes. It also needs its per-file header. In the traditional inode scheme, file data blocks are entirely payload; indexing occupies the fixed inode and separate indirect blocks.
For N data blocks in a large densely occupied indirect tree, about N/q lowest-level index blocks, N/q2 next-level blocks and so on are needed. With q=B/h, the asymptotic indexing space is approximately
B(qN​+q2N​+⋯)≈q−1NB​,
(20)
or about Nh bytes when q is large. Thus
large-file UNIX indexing is roughly one address per data block, versus three fields in System B.​
(21)
This is a comparison of typical densely used structures. For a small file, the fixed inode dominates; near an indirect-level boundary, partly empty index blocks add overhead. Both schemes can waste part of the last block through internal fragmentation.
The main performance advantage is indexed access to an arbitrary file offset: the inode finds the required data block with at most three indirect levels, whereas System B may need to follow a long chain from one end. Index blocks can be cached and scanned in batches, leaving complete data blocks for transfers. The inode scheme also naturally supports sparse files, where absent data-block pointers represent zero-filled holes without allocating every intervening block.
There are additional costs. A cold lookup can require separate index-block reads, allocation near a boundary can require allocating and updating several index blocks, and small files can pay more fixed metadata cost. System B already has a tail pointer and can append with local chain updates, while its owner and predecessor fields can help recovery from surviving blocks. Losing a UNIX indirect block can disconnect many data blocks, and those blocks do not automatically carry System B's owner information. Thus the space and file offset access advantages do not imply uniformly cheaper updates or uniformly better recovery.

The conjunction introduction rule combines derivations of ϕ and ψ into a derivation of ϕ∧ψ. Both conclusions must be available under the current assumptions.
The disjunction elimination rule is reasoning by cases: from ϕ∨ψ, a subproof deriving the same conclusion κ under ϕ, and another subproof deriving κ under ψ, conclude κ. It discharges the two case assumptions, not any surrounding premises.
The implication introduction rule derives ϕ→ψ by temporarily assuming ϕ and deriving ψ; it then discharges that occurrence of the assumption ϕ.
Thus
the boxes delimit temporary assumptions, their dependent subproofs, and their discharge.​
(54)
A conclusion inside a box cannot simply be reused outside it without an appropriate inference rule. Any other undischarged assumptions continue to constrain the result. These are exactly the rules printed in Figure 1.[ref]
