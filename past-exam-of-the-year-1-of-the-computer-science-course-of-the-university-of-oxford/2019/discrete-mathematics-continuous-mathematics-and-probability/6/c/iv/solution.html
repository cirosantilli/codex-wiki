At d=106, gradient descent remains a plausible large-scale choice; dense Newton method and full-memory BFGS are generally impractical. A dense d×d double-precision matrix alone contains 1012 entries, about 8 TB. Dense factorization also has order 1018 arithmetic work. Full BFGS avoids factorization but retains quadratic storage and update work, so it does not solve that scaling problem.
Gradient descent uses linear working storage in d, though the cost of reading the data and evaluating the gradient is still O(nd) for dense A. Sparsity or streaming can be important. Limited-memory BFGS, a distinct limited-memory variant, stores only a fixed number m of recent update pairs and uses O(md) storage and update work, making it a realistic alternative to full BFGS.[ref]
The available Hessian matrix factorization also permits a matrix-free Hessian-vector product:
Hv=AT(Δ(Av)).
(51)
It costs O(nd) for dense data without forming H. A suitable iterative, safeguarded Newton method can exploit this structure, so the conclusion rules out the dense implementation rather than every possible second-order algorithm.
These methods seek a local solution and require convergence checks appropriate to nonconvex optimization. The formula does not even ensure a finite minimizer exists: with one positive coefficient and nonzero a, the positive exponential loss approaches zero along an unbounded direction without attaining it. Dimension alone cannot supply a convergence guarantee.
