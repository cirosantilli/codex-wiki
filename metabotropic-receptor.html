Metabotropic receptor

 Home Biology Protein Receptor (biochemistry)
OurBigBook.com Words: 920 Articles: 3
A metabotropic receptor couples ligand recognition to intracellular signal transduction, which can regulate a separate ion channel, alter enzyme activity or change gene expression. In the usual neurotransmitter context, metabotropic receptors are G protein-coupled receptors: receptor activation is relayed by a G protein. There need not be a change in membrane potential at all. [ref]
Two routes should be distinguished. An activated G protein can regulate an ion channel locally at the cell membrane, without an obligatory diffusible second messenger. Alternatively, it can regulate an enzyme that changes the availability of a second messenger, such as cAMP or inositol trisphosphate. Downstream proteins then change channel opening, phosphorylation or other cellular activities. The receptor, G protein, second messenger and final target are distinct components; an activated G protein is a relay protein, rather than itself a small-molecule second messenger.[ref]
Do not turn this useful chemical synapse classification into the claim that every receptor is a GPCR, or that every indirect response must pass through a G protein. For example, the insulin receptor is an enzyme-linked cell-surface receptor whose intracellular catalytic activity initiates protein phosphorylation. Intracellular receptors supply another category. “Metabotropic” commonly names the GPCR branch in neuroscience; the broader idea of biochemical coupling does not make all non-channel receptors structurally identical. [ref]
Separating recognition from execution allows signal amplification and branching. A persistently activated receptor can activate several relay proteins; a regulated enzyme can produce many second messenger molecules. As an explicitly invented count example, suppose one activated receptor activates four relay proteins during one interval, and each relay activates a distinct enzyme that produces fifteen second messenger molecules during the next interval. With sufficient substrates and no loss, this gives 4×15=60 molecules. Those counts do not specify a physiological gain or response duration. A directly gated ion channel also passes many ions while open, so amplification is not an exclusive property of metabotropic receptors: always name the input and output being compared. [ref]
A particularly instructive electrophysiology experiment separated a chemical trigger from the ion channel carrying its eventual electric current. In 1987, Hirono, Ito and Sugiyama injected messenger RNA from rat brain into frog egg cells, then tested acquired responses to neurotensin and muscarinic acetylcholine stimulation. Under voltage clamp at −60mV, neurotensin elicited transient, oscillatory inward electric currents after a delay of several tens of seconds. This delay refers to that response and preparation. Reversal potential measurements implicated increased permeability to chloride anions, rather than a pore specific to the applied ligand. Both neurotensin and acetylcholine responses were inhibited by pertussis toxin, which interferes with susceptible G proteins, and by intracellular EGTA, which binds free calcium. Injecting inositol trisphosphate mimicked the responses; a large prior dose of inositol trisphosphate reduced subsequent responses to either extracellular signal. These interventions implicate a shared intracellular relay and calcium-dependent channel opening.[ref]
The informative comparison with the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor experiment is mechanistic: an extracellular ligand can produce an inward electric current either by directly gating the pore or by mobilizing an intracellular second messenger and stored calcium that activate another pore. Intracellular inositol trisphosphate bypasses reception, whereas EGTA tests the need for free calcium. However, neither inhibition nor mimicry alone identifies every molecular connection: a perturbation can affect several targets, and an injected mediator can activate a pathway without proving that it is the only natural route. The egg cell's endogenous machinery and the mixed messenger RNA preparation also limit extrapolation to an intact neuron. The experiment establishes functional coupling in its preparation, rather than the exact molecular composition or timing of every metabotropic receptor.
Even G protein-dependent channel regulation need not involve a lengthy second messenger cascade. Pfaffinger and colleagues reported in 1985 that acetylcholine-evoked potassium channel activation in embryonic chick atrial cells required intracellular GTP, a guanine-containing nucleotide, and was abolished by pertussis-toxin pretreatment. Changes in cyclic nucleotides, including cAMP, did not account for the channel response. The report discusses intrinsic activation delays of roughly 30–100ms, illustrating relatively rapid metabotropic receptor action. These observations support a G protein coupling step without cyclic-nucleotide second messengers; they do not by themselves exclude every possible intermediary or prove the specific subunit-channel interaction.[ref]

The model's useful feature is the delayed buildup created by sequential relaxation, not its arbitrary numbers. Actual kinetics also depend on ligand delivery, relay deactivation, neurotransmitter clearance and the final target. A local G protein route can act promptly, while an ionotropic receptor can remain active during continued exposure or feed a longer-lasting intracellular response. The beauty of metabotropic receptor coupling is its flexibility: the same recognition event can adjust electrical responsiveness and other cellular tasks through different targets. To classify an observed response, combine timing with molecular identity and controlled perturbations.
