Deletion-contraction recurrence for the chromatic polynomial (PG​(t)=PG−e​(t)−PG/e​(t))

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory Graph coloring Chromatic polynomial
OurBigBook.com Words: 253
Deleting an edge removes one colour constraint. Edge contraction describes exactly the newly admitted colourings that violate that constraint. If e joins distinct vertices u,v of a simple graph G, write G−e for the graph with that edge deleted and G/e for the graph with u,v identified and e removed. Parallel edges produced by the contraction may be reduced to one edge, since they impose the same condition on a proper vertex coloring. Then
PG​(t)=PG−e​(t)−PG/e​(t).
(15)
To see why, divide the proper vertex colorings of G−e into two classes. Those with different colours at u,v are exactly the colourings of G. Those with equal colours at u,v correspond one-to-one to colourings of G/e: the merged vertex receives their common colour. Subtracting the second class leaves the first.
For the triangle, delete AC to get a three-vertex path graph, or contract AC to get two vertices joined by an edge after removing the redundant parallel edge. Consequently,
PK3​​(t)=t(t−1)2−t(t−1)=t(t−1)(t−2).
(16)
At t=3, this says 6=12−6. Of the twelve path colourings pictured above, six give A,C the same colour; these are precisely the ones ruled out when we restore AC.
This recurrence is useful because it reduces a constrained counting problem to smaller graphs. A graph on n vertices with no edges has PG​(t)=tn, since every vertex chooses its colour independently. Repeated edge deletion and edge contraction reduce to this case and show that the counting function is a polynomial. The same recurrence gives its leading coefficients. The method is conceptually simple, although the number of recursive cases can still grow rapidly.
