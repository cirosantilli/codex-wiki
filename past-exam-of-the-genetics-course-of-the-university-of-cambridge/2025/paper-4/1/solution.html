Visible divergence and reproductive incompatibility measure different outcomes of evolution. A few changes in a gene regulatory network can transform a conspicuous phenotype, whereas differences in the machinery of meiosis or biological development can prevent reproduction without greatly changing adult appearance. Neither membership of the same genus nor morphological similarity specifies divergence time or the extent of reproductive isolation.
First separate barriers. premating isolation can arise through differences in courtship, habitat or breeding season even when a forced cross produces fertile offspring. postzygotic isolation concerns the survival or fertility of the offspring themselves. Thus fertile captive hybrids do not refute substantial reproductive isolation in nature: they may never encounter suitable mates, may have intermediate ecological requirements, or may be unattractive to both parental biological populations. Under the biological species concept, the relevant issue is effective gene flow among natural biological populations, not the success of one laboratory mating. Conversely, a laboratory failure can reflect husbandry rather than an intrinsic incompatibility.
Rapid visible divergence is especially plausible when sexual selection changes plumage or other signals, or natural selection changes a feeding structure. Such changes can involve a small part of the genome, leaving most interactions required for fertility compatible. In Darwin's finches, genome comparisons associated an ALX1 region with beak shape and also revealed interspecific gene flow. This is an example of conspicuous adaptive differentiation coexisting with exchange of alleles, rather than evidence that all avian hybrids are fertile.[ref] Continued gene flow can itself help maintain compatibility through much of the genome while divergent natural selection preserves differences at selected genetic loci.
For cryptic incompatibility, consider a Dobzhansky-Muller incompatibility. Two lineages start with compatible alleles a,b. One evolves A in the presence of b, while the other evolves B in the presence of a. Each change is tolerable in its own genetic background, but the combination A,B disrupts function in hybrids. This requires neither lineage to pass through a low-fertility intermediate. Such epistasis can affect spermatogenesis, chromosome pairing, or interactions between nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, while leaving the adult body plan nearly unchanged. The relevant changes can arise through natural selection, genetic drift, or conflict over inheritance; adaptive divergence in external form is not required.
A particularly informative mammalian experiment concerns PRDM9, which helps specify recombination hotspots. In a particular house-mouse cross, changing its DNA-binding region to the corresponding human sequence restored chromosome pairing and male fertility. The experiment implicates compatibility between a genetic recombination regulator and its binding sites, rather than visible morphological divergence. Crucially, this was a rescue in a specified cross and genetic background, not a demonstration that PRDM9 explains all mammalian speciation.[ref]
Comparative work on birds lacking PRDM9 found relatively stable recombination hotspots associated with functional genomic features.[ref] This suggests a possible difference in the routes to incompatibility: turnover of PRDM9 and its targets is unavailable in those lineages. The inference is mechanistic, however; hotspot stability by itself does not demonstrate that avian hybrids will be fertile.
Differences in karyotypes provide another mechanism. A balanced chromosomal rearrangement can have little visible effect in either parent yet cause faulty pairing or unbalanced gametes in a heterozygous hybrid. However, a different chromosome count alone does not prove sterility: the arrangement and segregation behavior matter. Differences in genomic imprinting may also disturb placental development or embryonic growth in mammalian crosses, sometimes producing direction-dependent outcomes. Parent-of-origin growth abnormalities and disrupted genomic imprinting in particular rodent hybrids provide evidence that this mechanism can matter.[ref] These are hypotheses to test in the relevant lineages, not a universal explanation for the bird–mammal comparison.
The sex of the offspring matters. Haldane's rule predicts that, when one sex of first-generation hybrids is absent, rare or sterile, it tends to be the heterogametic sex: typically XY males in mammals and ZW females in birds.[ref] Recessive incompatibilities on a sex chromosome can be exposed when there is no corresponding second copy; rapid evolution of reproductive functions can contribute too. Therefore a report of fertile avian males is insufficient without testing females. The rule predicts a sex bias in a given cross, not that birds as a class have weaker reproductive isolation.
To discriminate these mechanisms, compare multiple independently diverged pairs, match their estimated divergence times using phylogenetics, and avoid treating taxonomic rank as a clock. Perform reciprocal crosses with parental within-lineage controls; measure fertilization, embryonic survival, both sexes' fertility, and second-generation and backcross success. A fertile first generation can conceal hybrid breakdown after genetic recombination disrupts coadapted combinations. Pair karyotype analysis and observations of meiosis with genome mapping and carefully controlled rescue experiments. Field measurements of mating and ancestry then determine whether compatibility translates into gene flow. The contrast is explained by different rates and genetic targets of visible divergence and reproductive divergence, with sex, cross direction, ancestry and assay conditions determining the observed outcome.
