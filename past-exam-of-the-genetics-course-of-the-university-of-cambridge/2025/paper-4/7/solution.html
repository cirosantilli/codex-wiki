DNA repair preserves usable inherited information, while genetic recombination can improve the combinations in which that information is transmitted. Their evolution need not have the production or prevention of diversity as an ultimate goal. natural selection favors consequences for survival and reproduction in particular contexts; novelty is neither automatically beneficial nor something an organism must maximize.
mutation creates new sequence variants; genetic recombination usually rearranges variants already present. A chromosomal crossover can produce a new haplotype without changing the allele frequency at either of two genetic loci. Conversely, DNA repair can restore sequence integrity after damage without eliminating the inherited differences between individuals. In particular, a normal alternative allele is not an error merely because it differs from another individual's sequence. These operations address different features of inheritance.
Unrepaired damage can block DNA replication or transcription, break a chromosome, or generate a harmful mutation. DNA repair thus has immediate benefits to a biological cell and can reduce the future burden of deleterious changes in descendants. In multicellular organisms, somatic mutation can compromise tissue function, while germline mutation changes what offspring inherit. The relevant balance includes the biochemical costs of DNA repair, the probability and severity of damage, and the possibility of DNA repair errors. natural selection for increased fidelity can become weak relative to genetic drift when an additional improvement is extremely small; perfect fidelity need not evolve. Some error-prone damage responses can also improve immediate survival even if surviving descendants carry more mutations.
The biochemical relationship is complementary. homologous recombination repairs a DNA double-strand break by using a related intact sequence as a template. In meiosis, programmed breaks can be repaired using a homologous chromosome, sometimes producing chromosomal crossovers and sometimes noncrossover products. Biochemical identification of Spo11 linked a component of the meiotic machinery to programmed break formation, demonstrating that meiotic diversification can begin with a controlled DNA-damage-and-repair process.[ref] Crossovers also help establish the physical connections needed for reliable segregation in many meiotic systems. This immediate transmission function can favor the machinery independently of any later benefit from a novel haplotype. DNA repair with sister chromatids in another context can preserve sequence without the same exchange between parental homologues.
There are also population-level benefits of reshuffling. Suppose two favorable alleles, A and B, occur in different lineages as Ab and aB. With no exchange, their lineages compete and either favorable allele can be lost when the other lineage prevails. genetic recombination can create AB, allowing selection to combine their benefits; it can also separate a favorable allele from linked harmful changes. This is the core of the Fisher-Muller hypothesis and relates to Hill-Robertson interference, the reduction in the efficacy of natural selection caused by associations among selected genetic loci. Recreating low-load genotypes can also slow Muller's ratchet in finite biological populations.

For xAb​=xaB​=1/2 and the other two zero, D=−1/4. At r=0.1, the next frequencies are xAB′​=xab′​=0.025 and xAb′​=xaB′​=0.475. Each favorable allele still has frequency 1/2: the process creates both the favorable combination and its unfavorable counterpart. Subsequent natural selection determines their fates. If the original associations are already advantageous, breaking them can instead impose a recombination load.
In controlled yeast experimental evolution, sexual and asexual treatments differed in their rates and genomic routes of adaptation, with the sexual treatment more efficiently separating beneficial from deleterious changes.[ref] A mechanistic test should control the costs and bottlenecks of the sexual cycle, compare otherwise matched genetic backgrounds, and use reconstructed changes to distinguish genetic linkage from intrinsically different effects of a mutation. Such results establish a benefit under the tested population regime, not an unconditional advantage of sex in every environment. genetic recombination can disrupt favorable epistasis, and the costs of mating are distinct from those of crossover formation.
For an explanation of the evolution of the machinery itself, the association with its bearer matters. A recombination modifier must on average increase the representation of its own descendants, perhaps through improved segregation or through association with combinations that selection favors. Merely increasing long-run adaptability of the species is insufficient. Similarly, a mutator allele can spread in an asexual lineage by hitchhiking with a beneficial mutation it helped generate despite producing many harmful ones; exchange can weaken that association. The advantages of fidelity and of exchange consequently depend on genetic linkage, population size, environmental change and the current supply of beneficial variants.
DNA repair can regulate which exchanges are permitted as well. DNA mismatch repair corrects copying errors and can reject exchange between sufficiently diverged sequences. In bacteria, disabling mismatch-repair functions greatly relaxed a barrier to genetic recombination between divergent lineages.[ref] An experiment separating these roles should compare DNA repair of copying errors with exchange between templates of controlled divergence, using a restored DNA repair gene as a rescue. DNA repair and genetic recombination can therefore be parts of the same evolved solution: preserve functional sequence, transmit chromosomes reliably, and reshuffle existing alleles when doing so improves reproductive success.
