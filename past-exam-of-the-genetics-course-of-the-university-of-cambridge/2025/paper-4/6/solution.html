phenotypic plasticity is the capacity of a genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments. It can change an individual's performance within a generation, before any change in allele frequency. phenotypic plasticity can buy time for evolutionary adaptation and generate new targets for selection, but an induced response is neither necessarily beneficial nor evidence of inherited evolutionary change.
A reaction norm makes the distinction precise. Write an individual's phenotypic trait as z=a+bC, where C is an environmental cue and the genotype helps determine the intercept a and responsiveness b. Differences in a represent different baseline phenotypic traits; differences in b represent genetic variation in sensitivity, a form of genotype-environment interaction. If the phenotype maximizing genotype fitness in the later selective environment E is z∗=kE, an informative cue can move individuals nearer that optimum immediately. Appropriate changes in developmental timing or physiology can therefore prevent demographic collapse and preserve the variation and numbers needed for subsequent natural selection.
The importance of cue reliability follows from a simple model. Suppose genotype fitness loss is proportional to squared mismatch, the cue and environment have finite second moments, and we ignore the costs of responding. Minimizing E[(a+bC−kE)2] over a,b, with Var(C)>0, gives
b∗=kVar(C)Cov(E,C)​,a∗=kE[E]−b∗E[C].​
(7)
This is a model calculation, not a universal optimal reaction norm. It shows why reliable prediction of the selective environment can favor responsiveness, whereas an uninformative cue gives b∗=0. A response evolved under one cue–environment relation can become harmful when that relation changes. Temporal delays, irreversible biological development, limited response speed and energetic costs further constrain the advantage. Stress-induced loss of growth, for example, is not evidence of adaptive phenotypic plasticity unless it improves an appropriate measure of genotype fitness relative to a feasible alternative.
phenotypic plasticity can affect later evolution in several ways. It can expose previously unexpressed genetic variation when a novel environment changes developmental pathways. It can let a lineage enter a new habitat where natural selection then improves performance. It can also reduce the mismatch so effectively that natural selection on the underlying constitutive phenotypic trait weakens, slowing genetic change. Even maladaptive initial phenotypic plasticity can intensify natural selection for compensatory change, provided the biological population survives. Thus a rapid phenotypic response, rapid genetic adaptation and long-term diversification are three distinct outcomes, not interchangeable measures of success.
genetic accommodation is evolutionary change in an environmentally responsive phenotypic trait or its regulation. It can change the mean, threshold, magnitude or specificity of the response and can preserve or increase phenotypic plasticity. genetic assimilation is the particular case in which a formerly induced phenotype becomes expressed without the original inducing condition. Neither process requires the environment to write a beneficial change directly into the inherited DNA; natural selection or imposed breeding can sort existing and newly arising variation affecting the response. Assimilation need not occur when environments continue to fluctuate, because maintaining a responsive reaction norm may remain advantageous.
The classic experimental demonstration selected fruit flies for a wing-vein phenotype induced by heat treatment, eventually obtaining expression without that treatment.[ref] The key observation is the changed relation between environment and phenotype across selected generations. It demonstrates the feasibility of genetic assimilation, not that losing the vein was an adaptive response in nature, nor that acquired bodily modifications were directly copied into the genome. A modern replication should include heat-exposed unselected lines, untreated lines, replicated selection lines, controlled rearing and genotype fitness assays, and should distinguish segregating variation from a new major-effect mutation.
For diversification, phenotypic plasticity can permit use of several resources or produce alternative specialist forms. Repeated exposure to a new resource can increase the frequency with which one form is expressed, giving natural selection more opportunity to refine it. In spadefoot tadpoles, experiments compared the competitive performance of an induced carnivore form among biological populations differing in how often they produce it; the association between expression frequency and competitive refinement supports one prediction of plasticity-led evolution.[ref] Such comparisons do not directly observe the ancestor or establish that every difference arose after induction rather than from prior genetic divergence.
Alternative forms can lead to ecological speciation if specialization causes habitat-associated mating, divergent natural selection, and reduced gene flow. A mating preference coupled to the adopted habitat could help turn ecological divergence into premating isolation. But the opposite outcome is also plausible: one flexible genotype can succeed in both habitats, reducing divergent natural selection and maintaining gene flow. A difference in reaction norms is consequently not itself evidence of speciation. A plastic radiation can persist without producing new species, and a radiation can occur through ordinary heritable divergence with little contribution from phenotypic plasticity.
Testing the causal historical claim requires more than comparing present-day induced and constitutive forms. Use related lineages and explicit ancestral-state uncertainty, raise multiple genotypes in crossed environments, and measure both reaction norms and genotype fitness. Split-family rearing controls ancestry; cross-fostering and controlled generations help identify maternal carry-over; experimental manipulation of the cue separates induction from resource quality. Replicate evolution with and without access to the cue and quantify survival, subsequent genetic response and reproductive barriers. An extant sister lineage is an imperfect proxy for an ancestor, and correlations between phenotypic plasticity and species richness can reflect reverse causation or ecological opportunity. phenotypic plasticity contributes most convincingly when a demonstrated cue-dependent genotype fitness benefit precedes heritable refinement, and any proposed diversification is independently connected to reduced gene flow.
