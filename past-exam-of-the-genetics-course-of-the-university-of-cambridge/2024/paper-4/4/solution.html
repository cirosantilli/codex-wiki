Evolutionary developmental biology has connected differences in form to changes in the developmental processes that construct it. Its central advance is a mechanistic bridge: homologous developmental systems can generate diverse morphology through changes in deployment, interaction and timing, rather than each lineage requiring an entirely new toolkit.
Conservation is itself informative. Homologous patterning genes and signalling pathways occur in animals with very different anatomies, so the presence of a familiar gene cannot alone explain a new structure. Differences can arise through altered expression domains, developmental timing, dosage, protein function or the interactions among tissues. Heterotopy, heterochrony and heterometry describe spatial, temporal and quantitative changes; they are descriptions to be linked to a molecular cause, not explanations by themselves.
Modular cis-regulation supplies a concrete route to change. An enhancer active in one developing structure can evolve while other enhancers preserve expression elsewhere. In sticklebacks, recurrent deletions of a pelvic enhancer of Pitx1 are associated with pelvic reduction. This distinguishes loss of a particular expression domain from complete loss of the multifunctional transcription factor. The study combined population variation and functional evidence rather than inferring mechanism from a sequence difference alone.[ref]
Cross-species enhancer tests can establish stronger causality. Work on the ZRS enhancer of Sonic hedgehog showed altered limb-enhancer function in snake lineages; replacing the corresponding mouse enhancer with snake sequence produced a limb phenotype. Such experiments connect regulatory sequence to developmental output. Their interpretation remains context dependent: a sequence is being tested with the transcription factors, chromatin and tissues of the recipient animal, rather than reconstructing the complete ancestral organism.[ref]
The field also explains why the production of variation is structured. Developmental modularity can permit a local change with limited effects elsewhere, while pleiotropy and tissue interactions constrain accessible outcomes. Co-option recruits an existing regulatory programme into a new context. Gene duplication can allow functional divergence, although duplication by itself does not show which copy later caused a morphological change. Mechanical forces, cell behaviour and environmental sensitivity connect molecular changes to the resulting tissue geometry.
There are important limits. A developmental perturbation producing a resemblance is not necessarily the mutation that evolved historically. Expression correlations can be consequences of altered morphology, and viable laboratory effects do not establish a fitness advantage in nature. The fossil record and phylogeny are needed to order changes, population genetics to distinguish selection from drift, and ecology to explain which outcomes spread. Coding changes, regulatory changes, altered cell numbers and interactions among many small effects all contribute; there is no universal rule that morphology evolves only through enhancers.
The field has therefore advanced understanding substantially by replacing some pattern-based explanations with experimentally testable causal chains. It complements rather than replaces population-genetic and ecological accounts: development explains how variation is produced, and those accounts help explain its sorting and historical persistence.
