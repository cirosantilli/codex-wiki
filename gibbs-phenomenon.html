Gibbs phenomenon

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Fourier series
OurBigBook.com Words: 296
The Gibbs phenomenon is the persistent overshoot near a jump discontinuity in ordinary truncated Fourier series. For this square wave, retain M nonzero odd modes:
QM​(x)=π4​∑k=0M−1​2k+1sin((2k+1)x)​.
(17)
As M grows, the first peak approaches the jump while its height tends to approximately 1.17897974. This is an excess of about 0.17898 above the upper level 1, or 8.949% of the jump size 2. The narrower region does not imply a vanishing maximum error. At the jump itself every QM​(0)=0. These observations are compatible with pointwise convergence and L2 convergence but exclude uniform convergence to the discontinuous square wave. [ref].
For this particular sum, its derivative is QM′​(x)=(2/π)sin(2Mx)/sinx away from multiples of π, so the first positive peak is exactly at x=π/(2M). There,
QM​(2Mπ​)=M2​∑k=0M−1​πuk​sin(πuk​)​,uk​=Mk+1/2​.
(18)
This is a midpoint Riemann sum tending to 2Si(π)/π, where Si is the sine integral. It gives a short explanation of the nonzero limiting excess.
The phenomenon matters whenever a sharp boundary is represented by a limited set of Fourier harmonics: a visible ripple can be a property of the approximation rather than an oscillation in the original profile. Josiah Willard Gibbs reported the phenomenon in his 1899 letter “Fourier's Series” in Nature, volume 59, page 606. This is a documented historical report, without a claim of first discovery. [ref].
