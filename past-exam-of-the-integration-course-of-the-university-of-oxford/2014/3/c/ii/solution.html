Define the joint function by an unambiguous piecewise prescription:
H(x,y)={(x−y)β−1f(y),0,​0<y<x<1,otherwise in (0,1)2.​
(29)
It is nonnegative and Lebesgue measurable: on the open triangle the power factor is continuous and f(y) is measurable for the product sigma-algebra, and the zero extension is measurable. This also resolves the diagonal singularity and avoids evaluating a noninteger power of a negative number outside the triangle. The source's inclusion of y=x in its indicator set changes only a null set; set the value there to zero.
The function g(x)=∫01​H(x,y)dy is measurable by Tonelli's theorem. Apply that theorem to exchange the nonnegative integrals, with infinity allowed until the estimate proves otherwise:
∫01​g(x)dx​=∫01​f(y)(∫y1​(x−y)β−1dx)dy=β1​∫01​(1−y)βf(y)dy≤β1​∫01​f(y)dy<∞.​
(30)
The inner integral is (1−y)β/β because β>0. The weight is at most one because 0<y<1. Since g≥0, this proves
∥g∥1​=β1​∫01​(1−y)βf(y)dy≤β1​∥f∥1​,g∈L1((0,1)).​
(31)
In particular g is finite almost everywhere; any exceptional infinite values may be replaced by zero to obtain an everywhere finite representative. This is the L1 bound for a Volterra power-kernel integral.
An everywhere-finite conclusion would be stronger and can fail when 0<β<1, even for integrable f. For example, put x0​=1/2 and γ=(1+β)/2, and take f(y)=(x0​−y)−γ for 0<y<x0​ and zero otherwise. Since γ<1, f is integrable. But
g(x0​)=∫0x0​​(x0​−y)β−1−γdy=∞,
(32)
because β−1−γ=(β−3)/2<−1. This does not contradict Lebesgue integrability, which requires finiteness only almost everywhere.
