A measurable space is a pair (Ω,F) where F is a sigma-algebra of subsets of Ω: it contains Ω, is closed under complements relative to Ω, and is closed under countable unions. A measure on it is a function μ:F→[0,∞] with μ(∅)=0 and countable additivity:
μ(⋃n=1∞​An​)=∑n=1∞​μ(An​)
(1)
whenever the measurable sets An​ are pairwise disjoint. Infinite values are allowed. The triple (Ω,F,μ) is a measure space.
Because E∈F, the family FE​ contains E. If A∈FE​, then E∖A=E∩(Ω∖A) belongs to F and is a subset of E, so it belongs to FE​. If each An​∈FE​, their countable union belongs to F and remains a subset of E. Thus FE​ is a sigma-algebra on E, with relative complements taken in E, not in Ω.
Equivalently,
FE​={E∩B:B∈F},
(2)
the trace sigma-algebra on the measurable set E. The measurability of E is what makes this equal to the family in the question.
The function ν takes values in [0,∞] and has ν(∅)=0. For any pairwise disjoint An​∈FE​, countable additivity of μ gives
ν(⋃n​An​)=μ(⋃n​An​)=∑n​μ(An​)=∑n​ν(An​).
(3)
Therefore ν is a measure on (E,FE​): it is the restriction of a measure to a measurable subset.

For x<y, finite additivity on the disjoint pieces of the half-line gives
h(y)−h(x)=m(E∩(x,y]).
(9)
By monotonicity of a measure and the Lebesgue measure of an interval,
0≤h(y)−h(x)≤m((x,y])=y−x.
(10)
Exchanging the two points if necessary proves
∣h(y)−h(x)∣≤∣y−x∣(x,y∈R).​
(11)
Thus the cumulative measure of a finite-measure set is a Lipschitz function with Lipschitz constant at most one, hence is uniformly continuous: given ε>0, the choice δ=ε works simultaneously at every point.
To locate its endpoint limits, the measurable sets E∩(−∞,N] increase to E, so continuity from below of a measure gives h(N)→m(E). The measurable sets E∩(−∞,−N] decrease to the empty set and all have finite measure, so continuity from above of a measure gives h(−N)→0. Since h is nondecreasing,
limx→−∞​h(x)=0,limx→∞​h(x)=m(E).
(12)
For 0<c<m(E), choose R>0 with h(−R)<c<h(R). The intermediate value theorem on [−R,R] gives t with h(t)=c. Then
A=E∩(−∞,t] is Lebesgue measurable and m(A)=c.​
(13)
The endpoint values c=0,m(E) can also be achieved by A=∅,E. This proves divisibility of Lebesgue measure for arbitrary finite-measure Lebesgue measurable sets on the real line, including unbounded ones. The non-atomic measure structure permits prescribed fractions of measure; a measure concentrated at one point would not have this property.

Fix y=0. The function x↦f(x,y) is continuous on [−1,1], because its denominator is bounded away from zero, and it is an odd function of x. Its integral over this symmetric interval is therefore zero. At y=0 the entire section is zero, including the specified value at the origin. Thus the inner x integral exists for every y and is identically zero. The same reasoning with x and y exchanged gives
∫−11​(∫−11​f(x,y)dx)dy=∫−11​(∫−11​f(x,y)dy)dx=0.​
(30)
To test Lebesgue integrability in two dimensions, examine the absolute value near the origin. For 0<ε<1, the annulus ε<x2+y2​<1 lies inside the square. In polar coordinates,
∣f(rcosθ,rsinθ)∣=r4∣cosθsinθ∣​,dxdy=rdrdθ.
(31)
The change of variables formula on this annulus, where the integrand has no singularity, gives
∫[−1,1]2​∣f(x,y)∣dxdy​≥∫ε1​r−3dr∫02π​∣cosθsinθ∣dθ=2ε−2−1​⋅2=ε−2−1.​
(32)
The lower bound tends to infinity as ε↓0. Hence
∫[−1,1]2​∣f∣=∞,f∈/L1([−1,1]2).​
(33)
Moreover reflection in either coordinate axis exchanges the positive part and negative part while preserving Lebesgue measure. Both parts therefore have infinite integral, so a signed double Lebesgue integral would involve the undefined expression ∞−∞.
There is no contradiction with Fubini's theorem: its absolute integrability hypothesis is missing. This is an example of finite iterated integrals without joint integrability, with every individual one-dimensional section integrable despite the two-dimensional singularity.

Define the one-dimensional convolution kernel
k(t)={sint/∣t∣3/2,0,​t=0,t=0.​
(34)
It is a Borel measurable function, and f(x,y)=k(x−y)g(y). The function k(x−y) is Borel measurable by composition with the continuous difference map. Lifting the Lebesgue measurable function g to the second coordinate preserves measurability for the product sigma-algebra, hence also for Lebesgue measure on R2. Products of measurable functions are measurable, so f is Lebesgue measurable.
Near zero, ∣sint∣≤∣t∣; away from zero, ∣sint∣≤1. Consequently
∣k(t)∣≤{∣t∣−1/2,∣t∣−3/2,​0<∣t∣≤1,∣t∣>1.​
(35)
Both bounds are integrable on their respective regions by power singularity integrability. In particular,
∥k∥1​≤2∫01​t−1/2dt+2∫1∞​t−3/2dt=4+4=8.​
(36)
We use the following forms of the two product-integration theorems. On the product of measure spaces equipped with sigma-finite measures, Tonelli's theorem says that a nonnegative measurable function has equal product and iterated integrals, with infinity allowed. Fubini's theorem says that for an absolutely integrable measurable function, its sections are integrable for almost every fixed coordinate, and both orders of iterated integration equal the finite product integral. The Lebesgue measure on each factor is a sigma-finite measure, because the real line is a countable union of finite-length intervals.
Apply Tonelli's theorem first to ∣f∣, before presuming finiteness. By translation invariance of Lebesgue measure,
∫R2​∣f(x,y)∣dxdy​=∫R​∣g(y)∣(∫R​∣k(x−y)∣dx)dy=∥k∥1​∫R​∣g(y)∣dy≤8∫R​∣g(y)∣dy<∞.​
(37)
Thus f is Lebesgue integrable on R2. The triangle inequality for an integral now gives the requested estimate:
​∫R2​f(x,y)dxdy​≤∫R2​∣f(x,y)∣dxdy≤8∫R​∣g(y)∣dy.​
(38)
This is the L1 norm identity for a translated kernel product followed by a simple bound on the convolution kernel.
There is also a stronger signed conclusion. The integrable convolution kernel k is an odd function, so ∫R​k(t)dt=0. Having established absolute integrability, we may apply Fubini's theorem to f itself:
∫R2​f(x,y)dxdy=(∫R​k(t)dt)(∫R​g(y)dy)=0.​
(39)
Unlike part (a), the signed cancellation is justified by a finite integral of the absolute value. The constant eight is useful as an L1 norm bound even though this particular signed total vanishes.
