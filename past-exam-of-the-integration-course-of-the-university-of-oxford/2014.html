The monotone convergence theorem states: on a measure space (X,F,μ), let un​:X→[0,∞] be measurable functions with
0≤u1​(x)≤u2​(x)≤⋯,u(x)=limn→∞​un​(x).
(1)
Then u is measurable and
∫X​udμ=n→∞lim​∫X​un​dμ.​
(2)
The integrals and their limit may be infinite. No integrable dominator, finite measure hypothesis, or sigma-finiteness is required. The nonnegativity and increasing order are the essential hypotheses in this form of the theorem.

The dominated convergence theorem states: on a measure space (X,F,μ), let un​ be measurable functions converging almost everywhere to a measurable u. Suppose there is a nonnegative measurable function G with ∫X​Gdμ<∞ and ∣un​∣≤G almost everywhere for every n. Then u is integrable and
∫X​∣un​−u∣dμ⟶0,∫X​un​dμ⟶∫X​udμ.​
(14)
Here integrability means integrability with respect to the given measure; for Lebesgue measure it is Lebesgue integrability. Neither a finite measure space nor monotonicity of the sequence is required. The same single integrable dominator must control the whole sequence.

Fix t0​≥0. On a compact parameter interval containing all nonnegative t sufficiently close to t0​, the corresponding rectangle with x∈[0,1] is compact. The continuous function f is therefore bounded there by some M<∞, by the extreme value theorem.
For any sequence tn​≥0 tending to t0​, all sufficiently late tn​ lie in this rectangle's parameter range. For each x∈[0,1], continuity gives
f(tn​,x)⟶f(t0​,x),∣f(tn​,x)∣≤M.
(15)
The constant function M is integrable on [0,1]. The dominated convergence theorem therefore gives
F(tn​)=∫01​f(tn​,x)dx⟶∫01​f(t0​,x)dx=F(t0​).​
(16)
The sequential criterion for continuity on a real interval proves F is continuous on [0,∞). At zero this is right-continuity. This is continuity of parameter integrals on compact rectangles; the bound is local in the parameter, so f need not be bounded on the entire unbounded strip.

We prove the required differentiation under the integral sign directly. Fix t0​>0, and take a compact parameter interval J⊆[0,∞) containing t0​ in its interior. The jointly continuous partial derivative ∂t​f is bounded on the compact rectangle J×[0,1] by some M1​.

Dh​(x)=hf(t0​+h,x)−f(t0​,x)​.
(17)
For each x, the mean value theorem in the parameter gives ∣Dh​(x)∣≤M1​. The definition of the partial derivative also gives Dh​(x)→∂t​f(t0​,x). Since M1​ is integrable on [0,1], dominated convergence applied along any sequence hn​→0 yields
limh→0​hF(t0​+h)−F(t0​)​=limh→0​∫01​Dh​(x)dx=∫01​∂t​f(t0​,x)dx.
(18)
Thus
F′(t)=∫01​∂t∂f(t,x)​dx(t>0).​
(19)
At t=0, repeat the argument on J=[0,δ] with h>0. It proves that the right derivative exists and satisfies the same formula at zero. This is the appropriate meaning of differentiability on the closed half-line. In fact F′ is continuous there: apply part (i) to the continuous function ∂t​f.
The proof establishes differentiation of parameter integrals on compact rectangles under the exact regularity assumptions used here. In particular, it supplies the theorem's proof instead of assuming an interchange of a limit and an integral.

Let (X,A,μ) and (Y,B,ν) be measure spaces with sigma-finite measures, and use their product measure.
Fubini's theorem: if u is a measurable absolutely integrable function on X×Y, then its sections are integrable for almost every fixed value of the other coordinate, the resulting section-integral functions are integrable, and
∫X×Y​ud(μ×ν)=∫X​(∫Y​u(x,y)dν(y))dμ(x)=∫Y​(∫X​u(x,y)dμ(x))dν(y).​
(24)
All three integrals are finite. Values of an inner integral on an exceptional null set can be assigned arbitrarily.
Tonelli's theorem: if u:X×Y→[0,∞] is measurable and nonnegative, the same equality holds for its nonnegative product and iterated integrals, allowing the common value +∞. No advance absolute integrability assumption is needed. On a completed Lebesgue measure product, the section and measurability assertions are interpreted almost everywhere.
Thus Tonelli's theorem can first establish that ∫∣u∣<∞; after this, Fubini's theorem permits signed integration in either order.

Fix 0<x<1. On the open interval (0,x), the function y↦(x−y)β−1 is finite, positive and continuous, and f is nonnegative and Lebesgue measurable. Their product is therefore a nonnegative measurable function. Its Lebesgue integral is always defined as an element of [0,∞], with no possibility of cancellation of two infinities. Thus g(x) is well defined as a nonnegative extended integral.
The endpoint y=x need not receive its formal power value: for β<1 that value would be singular. The integral is over (0,x), and any chosen value at the endpoints has no effect because endpoints form a null set.
Finiteness is not implied by the present assumptions. For example, f(y)=1/y is an admissible nonnegative measurable function. For any fixed x, the factor (x−y)β−1 is bounded below by a positive constant on 0<y<x/2, so g(x)≥Cx​∫0x/2​dy/y=∞. The later L1 norm bound will establish finiteness almost everywhere when f is integrable. The construction is a Volterra power-kernel integral.

Define the joint function by an unambiguous piecewise prescription:
H(x,y)={(x−y)β−1f(y),0,​0<y<x<1,otherwise in (0,1)2.​
(29)
It is nonnegative and Lebesgue measurable: on the open triangle the power factor is continuous and f(y) is measurable for the product sigma-algebra, and the zero extension is measurable. This also resolves the diagonal singularity and avoids evaluating a noninteger power of a negative number outside the triangle. The source's inclusion of y=x in its indicator set changes only a null set; set the value there to zero.
The function g(x)=∫01​H(x,y)dy is measurable by Tonelli's theorem. Apply that theorem to exchange the nonnegative integrals, with infinity allowed until the estimate proves otherwise:
∫01​g(x)dx​=∫01​f(y)(∫y1​(x−y)β−1dx)dy=β1​∫01​(1−y)βf(y)dy≤β1​∫01​f(y)dy<∞.​
(30)
The inner integral is (1−y)β/β because β>0. The weight is at most one because 0<y<1. Since g≥0, this proves
∥g∥1​=β1​∫01​(1−y)βf(y)dy≤β1​∥f∥1​,g∈L1((0,1)).​
(31)
In particular g is finite almost everywhere; any exceptional infinite values may be replaced by zero to obtain an everywhere finite representative. This is the L1 bound for a Volterra power-kernel integral.
An everywhere-finite conclusion would be stronger and can fail when 0<β<1, even for integrable f. For example, put x0​=1/2 and γ=(1+β)/2, and take f(y)=(x0​−y)−γ for 0<y<x0​ and zero otherwise. Since γ<1, f is integrable. But
g(x0​)=∫0x0​​(x0​−y)β−1−γdy=∞,
(32)
because β−1−γ=(β−3)/2<−1. This does not contradict Lebesgue integrability, which requires finiteness only almost everywhere.
