Fourier's law (q=−k∇T)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Heat Thermal conduction
OurBigBook.com Words: 467 Articles: 2
Fourier's law predicts the local conductive heat flux from the local temperature gradient. In a continuum approximation with isotropic thermal conductivity k>0, its vector form and one-dimensional form are
q=−k∇T,q=−kdxdT​,Q˙​=Aqfor uniform normal flux over area A.
(130)
The heat flux q has units Wm−2, the temperature gradient has units Km−1 and the thermal conductivity has units Wm−1K−1. The rate of heat transfer Q˙​ has units W. These units distinguish a local material response from the total power crossing a surface.
The minus sign makes heat flow toward lower temperature: for k>0, q⋅∇T=−k∣∇T∣2≤0. In the worked bar in thermal conduction, dT/dx=−200Km−1 therefore gives q=+400Wm−2. Reversing the end temperatures reverses the heat flux. A zero temperature gradient gives zero conductive heat flux in this model.
Fourier's law is a constitutive relation expressing measured material behavior; it is not a consequence of conservation of energy alone. It applies locally and does not require a steady state. The straight-line bar solution additionally assumes constant thermal conductivity, constant cross-sectional area, no sources or side losses, and fixed end temperatures. Anisotropic materials require the directional response described by the thermal conductivity tensor. The scalar k used in the examples expresses the stated isotropy assumption. [ref]
Historically, Joseph Fourier's Théorie analytique de la chaleur was published in 1822. In Chapter II, article 112, he writes the heat crossing a spherical surface as the product of thermal conductivity, elapsed time, surface area and the negative radial temperature gradient; article 113 balances the incoming and outgoing heat to obtain the heat equation for a sphere. The enduring idea is this connection between a local flow rule and a global prediction of changing temperature. [ref] [ref]
