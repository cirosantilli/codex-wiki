Möbius inversion formula (g=f∗1⟺f=μ∗g)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory Arithmetic function Möbius function
OurBigBook.com Words: 323
Möbius inversion recovers an arithmetic function from its divisor sum. For arithmetic functions f,g, the following statements are equivalent, with each equality required for every positive integer n:
g(n)=∑d∣n​f(d)⟺f(n)=∑d∣n​μ(d)g(n/d).
(69)
Here d ranges over positive integer divisors of n and μ is the Möbius function. No multiplicativity assumption is needed, and both sums are finite. The right side can also be written ∑d∣n​μ(n/d)g(d) by exchanging complementary integer divisors. [ref]
The divisor sum g(n) bundles f(n) together with values at smaller integer divisors. Möbius inversion subtracts aggregates indexed by one distinct prime factor, adds those indexed by two, and continues with the alternating signs of inclusion-exclusion. A divisor containing a squared prime factor receives weight zero. The Möbius divisor-sum identity ensures that every unwanted contribution cancels exactly.
In Dirichlet convolution notation, g=f∗1 and μ∗1=ε, where 1 is the constant-one arithmetic function and ε is the Identity for Dirichlet convolution. Associativity and commutativity give the short proof:
μ∗g=μ∗(1∗f)=(μ∗1)∗f=ε∗f=f.
(70)
Convolving f=μ∗g with 1 proves the reverse implication. At n=1, the formula reduces to f(1)=g(1) because μ(1)=1.
For a worked example, take f(n)=φ(n), Euler's totient function. The totient divisor-sum identity says that g(n)=n, the identity arithmetic function:
g(12)=φ(1)+φ(2)+φ(3)+φ(4)+φ(6)+φ(12)=1+1+2+2+2+4=12.
(71)
This identity comes from classifying the integers 1,…,n by their greatest common divisor with n: the group with gcd(j,n)=h has φ(n/h) members, since j/h and n/h are coprime integers. Summing the group sizes gives n, as detailed under totient divisor-sum identity.
Now undo this aggregation with the Möbius function:
φ(12)​=μ(1)12+μ(2)6+μ(3)4+μ(4)3+μ(6)2+μ(12)1=12−6−4+0+2+0=4.​
(72)
The nonzero terms use d=1,2,3,6; d=4,12 vanish because they contain the square of the prime number 2. The four surviving integers coprime to 12 are 1,5,7,11.
This example also reveals why Möbius inversion is useful: it turns an easy count of all objects into a count satisfying a coprimality restriction. Expanding over subsets of distinct prime factors gives
φ(n)=∑d∣n​μ(d)dn​=n∏p∣n​(1−p1​).
(73)
The product is inclusion-exclusion over multiples of the prime factors. For 12, it gives 12(1−1/2)(1−1/3)=4. More generally, whenever a counting problem produces a divisor sum, Möbius inversion lets that aggregate be reversed without solving a new system of equations.
