Simple pendulum (θ¨+(g/ℓ)sinθ=0)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 5
A point mass constrained to a fixed-length massless arm under uniform gravitational acceleration is a simple pendulum. With no drag its angular equation is θ¨+(g/ℓ)sinθ=0. The small-angle approximation yields simple harmonic motion, whereas the full restoring force is nonlinear. Adding drag gives a damped pendulum.
For an ideal point mass m swinging in a vertical plane, the torque about the pivot is −mgℓsinθ. Dividing by its moment of inertia mℓ2 gives the displayed equation. Both the inertia and the gravitational torque scale with m, explaining why the point mass cancels. This derivation and the small-amplitude period are given in Ling, Sanny and Moebs, University Physics, pendulums.
Released from rest at a small angular amplitude θ0​, the linearized solution is θ(t)≃θ0​cos(g/ℓ​t), with oscillation period T0​=2πℓ/g​. For g=9.81m/s2, lengths one and four metres give T0​≈2.0061s and 4.0121s. This same scaling follows from dimensional analysis; the dynamical calculation supplies the coefficient 2π.
Finite angular amplitude changes the exact ideal period. Conservation of energy gives θ˙2=(2g/ℓ)(cosθ−cosθ0​) for release from rest. Integrating a quarter oscillation and substituting sin(θ/2)=sin(θ0​/2)sinϕ gives

Here K is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. The small-angle approximation gives a ratio tending to one; at 10∘,60∘,90∘, the ratios are approximately 1.00191,1.07318,1.18034. Thus almost constant period at small amplitude has a quantitative limit. As θ0​→π, the period diverges, connecting to the pendulum separatrix approach takes infinite time.
These predictions assume the specified ideal system. Drag changes the mechanical energy; a massive support changes the moment of inertia. The distinction is useful in experiments: a change in period can test an approximation rather than merely indicate an arithmetic mistake.
