Chemical potential (μ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 41
The chemical potential measures how a thermodynamic potential changes when matter is added under specified constraints. It plays for matter exchange the role that temperature plays for heat exchange: a difference supplies a direction, and equality characterizes thermodynamic equilibrium when the corresponding exchange is allowed. This connects diffusion, phase coexistence and chemical equilibrium through one quantity.
For a single component, the particle convention is
μparticle​=(∂N∂U​)S,V​,
(1)
where U is internal energy, N counts particles, S is entropy and V is volume. This partial derivative holds entropy and volume fixed; it measures an incremental change rather than the average energy per particle. See David Tong, Statistical Physics, section 1.4.
In chemistry the molar convention instead uses amount of substance n, measured in moles:
μm​=(∂n∂G​)T,p​,n=NA​N​,μm​=NA​μparticle​.
(2)
Here G is Gibbs free energy, T is temperature, p is pressure and NA​ is the Avogadro constant. The units are J per particle versus Jmol−1. For several chemical species, define each chemical potential by varying only its own particle count or amount of substance, with all other components' counts or amounts fixed. Many texts use μ for either convention, so the units and the variable being differentiated must identify it.
For a simple compressible system with variable component inventories, the fundamental differentials are
dU=TdS−pdV+∑i​μparticle,i​dNi​,dG=−SdT+Vdp+∑i​μm,i​dni​.
(3)
They describe changes between states in thermodynamic equilibrium with only pressure-volume work. If every component inventory is fixed, the sums vanish. The Gibbs free energy form follows by differentiating G=U−TS+pV and using dNi​=NA​dni​; this is why the two definitions describe the same chemical potential in different units.
A chemical potential can be negative. Holding entropy fixed while adding matter need not increase internal energy; Tong explains this explicitly for a classical ideal gas in Statistical Physics, section 2.2.4. Its absolute sign also depends on the reference energy: replacing U by U+ϵ0​N shifts μparticle​ by ϵ0​. A common reference shift cancels from differences for the same chemical species, which are what determine exchange. Thus “energy cost” means a signed increment, not an always positive amount that must be supplied.

Water activity (μw​=μw∘​+RTlnaw​)

Words: 55
Chemical potential OurBigBook.com
Water activity measures the chemical potential of water relative to a chosen pure-water standard state. Under ideal-vapour conditions above a flat solution, it is the equilibrium vapour pressure divided by that above pure liquid water at the same temperature. Dissolved solute usually lowers activity. Raoult's law gives the ideal dilute-solution limit; nonideal interactions require an activity model.

Standard chemical potential (μ∘)

Words: 35
Chemical potential OurBigBook.com
The chemical potential assigned to a specified standard state. With dimensionless thermodynamic activity a, the per-particle convention is μ=μ∘+kB​Tlna; a molar convention uses RT. The standard state changes both μ∘ and the activity definition without changing observable equilibrium.

Grand-canonical chemical potential of a classical ideal gas (μ=kB​Tln(nλ3))

Chemical potential OurBigBook.com
For one internal species, the grand canonical partition function of a classical ideal gas satisfies lnΞ=zV/λ3, with quantum gas fugacity z=eβμ and thermal de Broglie wavelength λ=2πℏ2/(mkB​T)​. Differentiating the grand potential gives N=zV/λ3, hence μ=kB​Tln(nλ3). An internal partition factor must be included consistently if internal states are available.

Quasi-Fermi level

Words: 101 Articles: 1
Chemical potential OurBigBook.com
A quasi-Fermi level is a local electron or hole population parameter in a semiconductor driven out of equilibrium, assuming each population is internally close enough to thermal equilibrium to use a Fermi-Dirac distribution. Separate electron and hole parameters replace one common equilibrium chemical potential. Their spatial profiles depend on transport, generation and recombination as well as electrostatics. For ideal ohmic contacts with the p voltage V relative to n, the contact electron electrochemical potentials obey μp​−μn​=−eV.

The difference between electron and hole quasi-Fermi levels under nonequilibrium conditions. For ideal selective contacts their terminal separation sets qV; poor contacts can lose part of the internal splitting.

Chemical potential equality for particle exchange (μm,A​=μm,B​)

Words: 597 Articles: 2
Chemical potential OurBigBook.com
Consider two internally equilibrated reservoirs A and B at the same imposed temperature and pressure. They may have different compositions or represent different phases. Transfer a small positive amount of substance δn of the same chemical species from A to B, holding the other inventories fixed. To first order, their total Gibbs free energy changes by
δGtotal​=−μm,A​δn+μm,B​δn=(μm,B​−μm,A​)δn.
(4)
Hence exchange toward lower chemical potential lowers Gibbs free energy. If transfer is allowed in both directions and the two reservoirs contain the exchangeable component, thermodynamic equilibrium requires equal chemical potentials for that component. See MIT 5.60, Lecture 14, pages 1–2.
An impermeable partition can maintain unequal chemical potentials; equality is a condition on an allowed exchange. Two pure samples of the same ideal gas already have equal chemical potentials at the same temperature and pressure. Different compositions at a common pressure can instead give different chemical potentials for one component through its thermodynamic activity.
Equality concerns the same exchangeable component in different places, not all different chemical species. For a permitted chemical reaction, the corresponding interior chemical equilibrium criterion is ∑i​νi​μm,i​=0, with signed stoichiometric vector entries νi​. The existing chemical equilibrium and constrained Gibbs minimization for chemical equilibrium sections develop those composition constraints.

Open-system equilibrium contacts

Unconstrained thermal, mechanical and particle-exchange contacts require equality of temperature, pressure and the chemical potential of each exchangeable species. Walls, external fields, interfaces and chemical reactions change which variations are allowed.

Chemical potential and Helmholtz free energy (μm​=(∂F/∂n)T,V​)

Words: 393
Chemical potential equality for particle exchange OurBigBook.com
Rigid compartments impose a different constraint: fixed volume rather than fixed pressure. With Helmholtz free energy F=U−TS,
dF=−SdT−pdV+∑i​μm,i​dni​,μm,i​=(∂ni​∂F​)T,V,nj=i​​.
(5)
This follows immediately by differentiating F=U−TS in the chemical potential differential above. It expresses the same chemical potential through the thermodynamic potential appropriate to fixed temperature and volume.
Consider a closed system consisting of two rigid compartments of equal volume V, maintained at a common fixed temperature T. A valve permits exchange of the same ideal gas; the partition stays in place. Initially nA​=9mol and nB​=3mol, with conserved total amount of substance ntot​=12mol. These are macroscopic moles, modeled continuously, not twelve individual particles. By the ideal gas law, pB​/pA​=nB​/nA​=1/3, so initially
μm,B​−μm,A​=−RTln3.
(6)
At 300K with the stipulated R, this is approximately −2740.311Jmol−1, favoring transfer from A to B.
At fixed T and equal V, integrating the ideal-gas chemical potential gives the total Helmholtz free energy, up to a term independent of the allocation,
Ftotal​=RT[nA​lnnref​nA​​+nB​lnnref​nB​​−ntot​]+C.
(7)
Here nref​ is a positive reference amount of substance; any omitted common term linear in nA​+nB​ is constant along this exchange. Let x=nA​/ntot​ be the dimensionless fraction in A. The relative Helmholtz free energy then takes the particularly simple form
ntot​RTFtotal​(x)−Ftotal​(1/2)​=xlnx+(1−x)ln(1−x)+ln2.
(8)
Its derivative along the allowed transfer and its second derivative are
dnA​dFtotal​​=μm,A​−μm,B​=RTln1−xx​,dnA2​d2Ftotal​​=RT(nA​1​+nB​1​)>0.
(9)
Thus the Helmholtz free energy is a strictly convex function of the allocation for positive amounts. Its unique minimum has x=1/2, or nA​=nB​=6mol, and equal chemical potentials. Initially its excess above this minimum is ntot​RT[43​ln(3/2)+41​ln(1/2)]≃3915.474J at 300K. Relaxation decreases the total Helmholtz free energy by this amount in the model; the final pressures are also equal because both compartments have the same volume.
The beautiful connection is that the direction of matter exchange and the slope of a free energy curve are the same calculation. The entropy gained by redistributing the ideal gas makes equal allocation favorable here. As with chemical equilibrium, a free energy minimum identifies the favored state, while transport properties determine how quickly it is reached. The fixed-temperature, fixed-pressure Gibbs free energy criterion is summarized in 16.4 Free Energy; the rigid experiment instead uses Helmholtz free energy.

Chemical potential of an ideal gas (μm​=μm∘​+RTln(p/p∘))

Words: 202
Chemical potential OurBigBook.com
For a pure ideal gas at temperature T and pressure p,
μm​(T,p)=μm∘​(T)+RTlnp∘p​,
(10)
where R is the molar gas constant and p∘ specifies the reference pressure of the standard state. The ratio inside the natural logarithm is dimensionless. The chemical potential difference therefore depends on a ratio of pressures, not an absolute difference. See MIT 5.60, Lecture 14, page 4.
For a chemical species in an ideal gas mixture, replace p by its partial pressure. More generally write μm,i​=μm,i∘​+RTlnai​ using its thermodynamic activity ai​ and a consistent standard state. For an ideal dilute chemical solution, ai​=ci​/c∘ with reference concentration c∘; the thermodynamic activity section describes departures from this idealization.
As a computed comparison, take identical chemical species at T=300K, with pA​=2p∘ and pB​=p∘. Stipulate R=8.314462618Jmol−1K−1. Then
μm,B​−μm,A​=RTlnpA​pB​​=−RTln2≃−1728.944Jmol−1.
(11)
Halving the pressure gives the same decrement whether starting from 2p∘ or 20p∘, provided the ideal gas approximation remains valid. This comparison uses different pressures; it is not the common-pressure reservoir setup of chemical potential equality for particle exchange. The fixed-volume example in chemical potential and Helmholtz free energy supplies an exchange experiment with unequal initial pressures.

Electrochemical potential (μ​m​=μm​+zFFar​ψ)

Words: 139 Articles: 2
Chemical potential OurBigBook.com
The molar electrochemical potential combines the molar chemical potential μm​ with the electrical contribution zFFar​ψ, where z is the signed charge number of an ion, FFar​=NA​e is the Faraday constant and ψ is electric potential. Both terms have units Jmol−1. In the particle convention the corresponding expression is μ​particle​=μparticle​+zeψ, with elementary charge e; multiplying it by the Avogadro constant gives the molar form. Transfer between locations at the same temperature and pressure is driven by differences in electrochemical potential. The membrane transport examples use it to distinguish neutral solute transfer from ion transfer.

Chemical equilibrium

Words: 1k Articles: 27
Chemical potential OurBigBook.com
At chemical equilibrium, a reversible chemical reaction has equal forward and reverse reaction rates, so its macroscopic composition stays constant. Individual molecules can keep changing identity: the reaction has not stopped. This dynamic balance is the definition described by the [ref]. Equal reaction rates also need not mean equal concentrations. A small population converting rapidly can balance a large population converting slowly.
This distinction gives two useful questions. Which composition is favored at the specified temperature and other constraints? Thermodynamics answers through chemical potentials and an equilibrium constant. How quickly does a prepared mixture approach that composition? Chemical kinetics answers through reaction rates. Knowing the destination does not determine the journey's duration. A composition that barely changes during observation may reflect extremely slow chemical kinetics, so constancy alone does not establish chemical equilibrium.
Consider the idealized elementary chemical reaction
A⇌B,kf​=0.3s−1,kr​=0.1s−1.
(13)
Assume a well-mixed closed system at fixed temperature and volume, with first-order law of mass action rates in both directions and no competing chemical reactions. Write a=[A] and b=[B] for concentrations in M=mol/L. The total a+b=C=1M is conserved because each conversion replaces one molecule by one molecule. This conservation is special to the chosen stoichiometry.
The forward and reverse reaction rates, measured as conversions per unit volume per unit time, are Jf​=kf​a and Jr​=kr​b. Hence
dtda​=−kf​a+kr​b=−(kf​+kr​)a+kr​C.
(14)
Setting this derivative to zero gives
aeq​=kf​+kr​kr​C​=0.25M,beq​=0.75M.
(15)
With a(0)=1M and b(0)=0, solving the ordinary differential equation yields
a(t)=(0.25+0.75e−0.4t/s)M,b(t)=(0.75−0.75e−0.4t/s)M.
(16)
At t=5s, these are approximately 0.3515M and 0.6485M. Initially Jf​=0.3M/s and Jr​=0. At chemical equilibrium, both are 0.075M/s, while the net reaction rate Jf​−Jr​ is zero. The persistent exchange is visible in the lower panel even after the upper panel has nearly flattened.
Why does chemical equilibrium also involve a free energy minimum? For a closed system at fixed temperature and pressure, without imposed non-expansion work, stable equilibrium minimizes Gibbs free energy over compositions allowed by conserved elemental inventories. See W. C. Carter, MIT thermodynamics notes, sections 2-1.14 and 2-1.19. This is constrained Gibbs minimization for chemical equilibrium: freely minimizing over unrelated species amounts would change the inventory. At fixed temperature and volume, the appropriate potential is Helmholtz free energy. In either setting, varying an allowed extent of reaction gives the corresponding free energy slope ∑i​νi​μi​, with molar chemical potentials and signed stoichiometric vector entries. An interior equilibrium has zero slope; stability requires a minimum. For A converting to B, this says μB​=μA​.
The beauty of this connection is that molecular exchange, a macroscopic composition, and a geometric minimum describe the same balance. In the ideal concentration model, let x=b/C and choose the standard molar chemical potential difference consistent with K=3. Up to a composition-independent constant, the fixed-volume Helmholtz free energy then obeys
nRTF​=(1−x)ln(1−x)+xlnx−xln3,
(17)
where n is the conserved total amount of substance and R the molar gas constant. Its derivative is ln[x/(1−x)]−ln3, vanishing at x=3/4. Its second derivative is 1/x+1/(1−x)>0 inside 0<x<1, so this balance is the unique minimum. The mixing entropy terms explain why the favored composition still contains A.

The linked equilibria among dissolved carbon dioxide, bicarbonate, carbonate and hydrogen ions in seawater. Chemical equilibrium of a classical spin gas (n±​=nQ​eβμe±βμB​B)

Words: 74
Chemical equilibrium OurBigBook.com
For a nondegenerate spin-one-half ideal gas with energies ∓μB​B(r), the two component chemical potentials are kB​Tln(n±​/nQ​)∓μB​B. Diffusive and spin-conversion equilibrium require one spatially constant common chemical potential, provided spin conversion is allowed. Hence the displayed densities give total number density n=2nQ​eβμcosh(βμB​B) and magnetization M=2μB​nQ​eβμsinh(βμB​B)=nμB​tanh(βμB​B). The high-temperature fixed-density limit is M≃nμB2​B/(kB​T), a Curie law. At fixed chemical potential the density itself varies with temperature, so the unnormalized magnetization need not have a pure 1/T dependence.

Chemical disequilibrium

Words: 39
Chemical equilibrium OurBigBook.com
A chemical system is out of local chemical equilibrium when reaction adjustment cannot keep pace with transport, imposed changes or external driving. Comparing the chemical relaxation time with transport and irradiation times identifies plausible departures. Disequilibrium chemistry in an exoplanet atmosphere combines this principle with mixing, horizontal advection and atmospheric photochemistry.

Zero chemical potential from number-changing reactions (μϕ​=0)

Words: 49
Chemical equilibrium OurBigBook.com
For one particle species in chemical equilibrium under 2ϕ↔3ϕ, chemical balance gives 2μϕ​=3μϕ​, hence μϕ​=0. Elastic 2ϕ↔2ϕ imposes no such constraint. In an expanding universe the number-changing reaction must remain fast compared with the Hubble parameter; after chemical freeze-out a nonzero chemical potential can develop even if elastic collisions maintain kinetic equilibrium.

Chemical relaxation time (τchem​)

Words: 124
Chemical equilibrium OurBigBook.com
The chemical relaxation time estimates how rapidly a perturbed abundance returns toward chemical equilibrium. Comparing it with the eddy mixing time identifies a chemical quench level.
For the A-to-B model in chemical equilibrium, deviations decay by exponential decay with τchem​=1/(kf​+kr​)=2.5s. After one such interval, 1/e of the initial deviation remains; reaching one percent takes ln(100)τchem​≃11.51s. Multiplying both rate constants by two preserves their ratio and the equilibrium composition, but halves τchem​ to 1.25s. The equilibrium exchange rates double to 0.15M/s. Faster exchange therefore coexists with the same final composition and the same free energy minimum.

Equilibrium constant (K)

Words: 235 Articles: 3
Chemical equilibrium OurBigBook.com
For a specified chemical reaction and standard state convention, the dimensionless equilibrium constant is the equilibrium value of the reaction quotient Q=∏i​aiνi​​, where ai​ denotes thermodynamic activity and νi​ a signed stoichiometric vector entry. Thermodynamic activities incorporate the reference state and departures from ideal behavior. See IUPAC Gold Book, equilibrium constant.
In the ideal concentration model for A converting to B, use aA​=[A]/c∘ and aB​=[B]/c∘ with the same reference c∘=1M. Their ratio gives Q=[B]/[A] and K=0.75/0.25=3. Equality K=kf​/kr​ applies to this elementary first-order pair; it is not a universal ratio of two measured rate constants for an arbitrary multistep chemical reaction.
At a specified constant temperature, with consistent standard states, the standard molar reaction Gibbs free energy is
Δr​G∘=∑i​νi​μi∘​=−RTlnK,Δr​G=Δr​G∘+RTlnQ=RTln(Q/K).
(18)
Here Δr​G is the local slope per mole of extent of reaction at fixed temperature and pressure, rather than the finite total Gibbs free energy change between two mixtures. At equilibrium Δr​G=0, while Δr​G∘ generally remains nonzero. For K=3 at 298K, it is about −2.72kJ/mol. For positive activities, Q<K favors forward conversion and Q>K favors reverse conversion; neither sign supplies a reaction rate. See MIT 5.111, Lecture 18, reaction free energy and equilibrium.
