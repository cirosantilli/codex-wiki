The anisotropic quantum harmonic oscillator is a sum of three commuting one-coordinate oscillator Hamiltonians. Substituting ψ=X(x)Y(y)Z(z) and dividing by XYZ gives a sum of three functions of independent coordinates, so each equals a separation constant. Consequently
ψnx​ny​nz​​=Xnx​​Yny​​Znz​​,E=ℏ∑i=x,y,z​ωi​(ni​+1/2).

The kinetic energy is rotationally invariant. The potential is invariant under rotations about z precisely when (i) ωx2​=ωy2​; full rotational invariance requires (ii) ωx2​=ωy2​=ωz2​. For positive oscillator frequencies these are equality of the frequencies themselves. Acting with the commutators on arbitrary test functions excludes anisotropic potentials in case (ii).
In the isotropic case let b=mω/ℏ. Then
EN​=ℏω(N+3/2),N=nx​+ny​+nz​​,ψ100​=2​π3/4b5/4​xe−br2/2,α=2ℏmω​​.

⟨x2⟩=2b3​,Δx=2mω3ℏ​​​.

C10 OurBigBook.com
A pure quantum state is a unit ray in a complex Hilbert space: normalization fixes total probability, a common phase is unobservable, and superpositions retain relative phases. Observables are self-adjoint operators. For an ideal projective measurement with spectral projector Pa​, the Born rule gives p(a)=⟨ψ∣Pa​∣ψ⟩ and the Lüders rule gives conditional state Pa​ψ/p(a)​. This preserves an eigenspace, not necessarily a unique vector when the outcome is degenerate. Between measurements, a closed system evolves unitarily according to iℏ∂t​ψ=Hψ. Composite systems use tensor products; quantum statistics additionally restrict identical-particle states. These are a framework supported by many experiments, not separate logical deductions from any one experiment.
In the photoelectric effect, monochromatic light illuminates a clean metal and a retarding potential measures the largest emitted electron kinetic energy. frequency controls the stopping energy, eVs​=hν−Φ, while in the one-photon regime intensity mainly controls the emission rate. A threshold frequency and a stopping-energy slope h/e support quantized energy exchange rather than gradual absorption of arbitrary classical-wave energy by each electron. Contact potentials, surface contamination, dark current and spectral impurities require controls. Robert Andrews Millikan tested the frequency–stopping-potential relation with careful surface and electrical control.[ref]
The Stern-Gerlach experiment sends a collimated neutral silver beam through an inhomogeneous magnetic field onto a detector. The force is approximately Fz​=μz​∂z​B in an appropriate strong-field electronic description. Two resolved components rather than a continuous classical orientation spread support discrete angular-momentum projections. Field-off beam profiles and magnetic-gradient/beam-width controls distinguish splitting from ordinary broadening. In the modern description the silver ground electronic state has J=1/2; the 1922 experiment preceded the electron-spin interpretation. Ideal sequential analyzers along different axes illustrate incompatible observables and state preparation, but were not the original apparatus.[ref]
In the Davisson–Germer experiment, accelerated electrons scatter from a nickel single crystal and an angularly scanned collector measures the scattered intensity. Changing voltage and crystal orientation changes narrow diffraction maxima. The inferred wavelength agrees with λ=h/p, with nonrelativistic p=2meV​. vacuum, crystal cleaning, energy selection and background measurements matter: adsorbed surface gas can produce additional diffraction features. This supports matter-wave amplitudes and interference, with measured intensities related to their squared magnitude; it does not mean an electron must follow a classical orbit through the apparatus.[ref] Together these experiments motivate quantized outcomes, wave amplitudes and probabilistic measurement while leaving a distinction between observed evidence and the general postulates used to organize it.

A continuous symmetry is represented by a unitary family U(ϵ)=e−iϵG/ℏ with a self-adjoint conserved generator of a continuous quantum symmetry G. If UHU†=H for all ϵ, differentiating gives [H,G]=0. Thus, for no explicit time dependence,
dtd⟨G⟩​=ℏi​⟨[H,G]⟩=0​.
(26)
translation invariance conserves the corresponding momentum component; rotational invariance about one axis conserves that angular-momentum component. A central potential conserves all components and L2, but the components need not be simultaneously measurable because [Lx​,Ly​]=iℏLz​. Time-translation invariance of a closed system gives a time-independent Hamiltonian and energy conservation.
A discrete symmetry need not have a continuously varying generator. For parity, [H,P]=0 lets stationary states be chosen even or odd, and preserves parity sectors. symmetry does not force every state to be an eigenstate of its conserved observable. Explicit time dependence adds ⟨∂G/∂t⟩ to the conservation equation, and an external symmetry-breaking potential generally permits exchange with the environment. Degenerate eigenspaces require choosing a compatible common basis rather than assuming every eigenvector already diagonalizes every commuting operator.

An ideal operational amplifier has infinite differential open-loop gain, infinite input impedance, zero output impedance and unlimited bandwidth and output capability, and zero input offset voltage and input bias current. In a stable negative-feedback circuit operating linearly, its input currents vanish and V−​=V+​. This equality is a feedback consequence, not a rule for a saturated or open-loop amplifier. Here the grounded non-inverting input makes the inverting node a virtual earth.
The inverting two-pole band-pass filter has a series resistor and capacitor input, while the feedback arm is a parallel resistor and capacitor. With Laplace variable s,
Zin​=R1​+sC1​1​,Zf​=1+sR2​C2​R2​​,H(s)=−Zin​Zf​​=−K(1+sτ1​)(1+sτ2​)sτ1​​​,K=R2​/R1​.
(31)

∣H∣2=K2(1−ω2τ1​τ2​)2+ω2(τ1​+τ2​)2ω2τ12​​.
(32)
It vanishes at zero and infinite frequency, with low-frequency ∣H∣2≃(ωR2​C1​)2 and high-frequency ∣H∣2≃1/(ωR1​C2​)2. Differentiation gives the single maximum
ω0​=τ1​τ2​​1​,∣H∣max​=Kτ1​+τ2​τ1​​​.

ω±​=2ba2+4b​±a​,Δω=ω+​−ω−​=ba​,Q=Δωω0​​=τ1​+τ2​τ1​τ2​​​≤21​​.

∣H∣max​=2R1​R2​​=2C2​C1​​(τ1​=τ2​)​.

A more selective resonant filter needs additional circuit structure. The gain above answers the component-ratio question for the sharpest available setting without assuming an impossible limit.
Real amplifiers have finite frequency-dependent gain and phase lag, so virtual earth becomes inaccurate, the response changes and stability must be checked. Gain-bandwidth limits, slew rate for large signals, output loading and amplifier saturation also matter. Component tolerances move the corners; capacitor parasitics, stray capacitance, resistor noise and input noise become significant. The ideal high-frequency tail should not be extrapolated beyond the amplifier and lumped-component operating ranges.

a OurBigBook.com
A systematic error shifts results in a reproducible or correlated way relative to the measurand. Examples include zero offsets, gain miscalibration, nonlinearity, drift, geometrical misalignment, loading, background contamination and an inadequate response model. Repeated measurements under unchanged conditions can reduce independent noise without exposing such a shift; a small standard error is not proof of measurement accuracy.
Use traceable standards and independent measurement methods, test zero and span, reverse the physical signal where possible, and vary the conditions that could cause the bias. For common-offset cancellation by signal reversal, take measurements y+​=S+b and y−​=−S+b. The half-difference estimates S while the half-sum diagnoses an unchanged offset b. Drift between the two readings would violate that cancellation, so alternate or interleave measurements. Randomizing acquisition order separates slow drift from controlled parameter changes; blanks and calibration samples test backgrounds and instrument gain. Blinding helps reduce analysis choices that follow an expected answer.
Corrections have uncertainties and may be correlated across data. Include residual offsets and gains as nuisance parameters or propagate their calibration covariance, rather than treating a common calibration error as fresh independent noise for each datum. Document assumptions and check stability with controlled perturbations. These methods suppress or quantify specified systematic effects; they cannot guarantee elimination of every unknown bias.

The likelihood is L(θ;d)=p(d∣θ) considered as a function of parameters at the observed data. It is not itself a probability distribution over θ. Bayes' theorem gives
p(θ∣d)=∫p(d∣ϑ)π(ϑ)dϑp(d∣θ)π(θ)​​.
(36)
The prior represents parameter information before these data; the denominator normalizes the posterior and can compare fully specified models with compatible prior assumptions. Detector response, backgrounds, dependence and selection must be included in the data model.
For independent measurements xi​∼N(θ,σi2​) with known errors, logL=constant−21​∑i​(xi​−θ)2/σi2​. Maximum likelihood minimizes this chi-squared and gives θ=∑i​wi​xi​/∑i​wi​, wi​=σi−2​. A Gaussian prior N(μ0​,s02​) instead yields
spost2​=∑i​wi​+s0−2​1​,μpost​=spost2​(i∑​wi​xi​+s02​μ0​​)​.
(37)
This explicitly combines data and prior precision, approaching the weighted mean when prior information is weak. Unknown calibration parameters should be jointly inferred and marginalized, not silently fixed. posterior intervals are conditional on the assumed model and prior; posterior predictive checks and sensitivity to plausible priors help expose poor fits and weakly identified parameters. The maximum-likelihood estimate and a Bayesian posterior answer different inferential questions even when their numerical centres agree.

temperature changes alter dimensions, resistances, refractive indices and offsets; gradients can generate thermoelectric voltages. Identify the dominant heat paths and the experiment's temperature coefficients before choosing insulation. Reduce convection with an enclosure or, where appropriate, vacuum; reduce conduction through supports and leads with suitable geometry and thermal anchoring; use thermal radiation shields and low-emissivity surfaces to reduce radiative exchange. vacuum alone does not remove heat conduction through wires or thermal radiation.
In first-order thermal isolation, a sample with heat capacity C and thermal conductance G to its surroundings obeys
CδT˙s​+GδTs​=GδTb​+δP,δTb​δTs​​=1+iωτ1​,τ=C/G​.
(38)
Thermal mass and insulation suppress rapid fluctuations but only delay slow ambient drift: the zero-frequency gain remains one. Active temperature control, a stable reference environment, adequate equilibration time and an appropriate sensor location address long-term stability. Self-heating produces a steady offset δTs​=δP/G; increasing insulation can worsen that offset and settling time. Check temperature gradients rather than relying on a remote thermometer.
Match materials or use differential/reference measurements to reduce expansion and resistance drift; keep dissimilar-metal junctions isothermal to suppress thermoelectric offsets. Log temperature alongside the signal and deliberately vary environmental temperature to measure residual coupling, using a physically justified correction with uncertainty. Interleave calibrations and allow equilibration after changing power. Feedback must remain stable, and heater/sensor electronics should not introduce electrical interference into the measurement.
