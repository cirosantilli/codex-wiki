spin normalization in two-dimensional free-electron state counting. Treat the mobile electrons as independent particles in a constant interior potential, with parabolic dispersion ϵ=ℏ2k2/(2m), and impose confinement or periodic boundary conditions. electron–electron interactions and the detailed periodic ionic potential are neglected, while Fermi statistics are retained. Each spatial state occupies area (2π)2/A in two-dimensional wavevector space. For spin degeneracy gs​,
N(k)=gs​(2π)2Aπk2​,g(ϵ)=dϵdN​=2πℏ2gs​Am​​.
(27)
The printed coefficient Am/(2πℏ2) is the per-spin density of states. Unpolarized spin-one-half electrons have gs​=2, doubling it. Actual graphene near its Dirac points has approximately linear dispersion, so its energy-dependent density of states is not this parabolic-model result; the plane geometry alone does not establish the free-electron dispersion.[ref]
Occupied disk and Fermi energy. At zero temperature fill the lowest states up to a disk of radius kF​. State counting gives
kF​=gs​A4πN​​,ϵF​=gs​Am2πℏ2N​​.
(28)
Thus the physical spin-degenerate result is ϵF​=πℏ2N/(Am). If N instead counts one spin species, the per-spin convention gives 2πℏ2N/(Am). Each occupied wavevector can contain one electron of each allowed spin, never two in the same complete one-particle state.
Occupation probability and chemical potential of a two-dimensional ideal Fermi gas. A single fermion state permits occupation zero or one. In a grand canonical ensemble their relative weights are 1 and e−β(ϵ−μ). Normalizing gives
p(ϵ)=1+e−β(ϵ−μ)e−β(ϵ−μ)​=eβ(ϵ−μ)+11​​.
(29)
The chemical potential is fixed by the total particle number. Here the total density of states D=gs​Am/(2πℏ2) is constant above the band bottom. Integrating the occupation gives N=DkB​Tln(1+eμ/(kB​T)), and therefore
μ=ϵF​+kB​Tln(1−e−ϵF​/(kB​T))⟶ϵF​(T→0)​.
(30)
The approach is exponentially close in this ideal two-dimensional constant-density-of-states model; a three-dimensional T2 correction should not be inserted here. A narrow interval of width of order kB​T around μ is thermally smeared.

One mass per cell. If uj​ is the displacement from equilibrium, the two springs give mu¨j​=α(uj+1​+uj−1​−2uj​). Substituting uj​=Uei(qja−ωt) yields

diatomic-chain zone-edge polarizations and dimensionality. At the edge, cosqa=0, so the heavy and light sublattices decouple: the acoustic edge frequency is 2α/M​ and the optical edge frequency is 2α/m​. Hence
Δω=2α/m​−2α/M​​.
(35)
The acoustic edge mode moves only the heavy sublattice; the optical edge mode only the light one. If M=m, the gap closes and the two branches are the zone-folded monatomic branch.
A three-dimensional crystal with two atoms per primitive cell has six branches: three acoustic and three optical. For propagation along a suitable symmetry direction these resolve into one longitudinal and two transverse branches of each type. The wavevector and Brillouin zone are three-dimensional; anisotropic force constants determine the dispersions, and polar crystals can have longitudinal–transverse optical splitting. A universal six-branch curve cannot be specified from masses alone.

The Debye model replaces the crystal by a harmonic elastic continuum with acoustic dispersion ω=vs​k. It counts three polarizations and introduces a cutoff so that the total number of vibrational modes is 3N, where N is the atom count. Periodic-box counting gives
g(ω)=2π2Vω2​(vL3​1​+vT3​2​).
(36)
For an isotropic average speed, 3/vD3​=1/vL3​+2/vT3​. With cutoff frequency ωD​ fixed by the mode count,
g(ω)=ωD3​9Nω2​(0<ω<ωD​),ΘD​=kB​ℏωD​​​.
(37)
This density of states is quadratic because a three-dimensional shell has volume proportional to k2dk and the acoustic dispersion is linear. The cutoff is a mode-counting device; it is not a claim that every actual branch stays linear to the zone boundary.
Each phonon mode has mean thermal energy ℏω/(eβℏω−1), using Bose-Einstein statistics with zero phonon chemical potential. The fixed harmonic zero-point contribution has zero temperature derivative. With x=ℏω/(kB​T),
Uth​=9NkB​T(ΘD​T​)3∫0ΘD​/T​ex−1x3​dx,
(38)
and differentiating at fixed volume gives
CV​=9NkB​(ΘD​T​)3∫0ΘD​/T​(ex−1)2x4ex​dx​.
(39)
For T≪ΘD​, extend the upper limit to infinity. Expanding 1/(ex−1)=∑j≥1​e−jx gives ∫0∞​x3/(ex−1)dx=6∑j−4=π4/15. integration by parts makes the heat-capacity integral 4π4/15, so
CV​≃512π4​NkB​(ΘD​T​)3​.
(40)
Only modes with ℏω≲kB​T are appreciably excited; their number scales as T3, explaining the power law physically. At high temperature every mode contributes kB​, giving the Dulong-Petit limit CV​→3NkB​.
The model assumes the harmonic approximation, a macroscopic three-dimensional solid and acoustic continuum behaviour for the thermally populated modes. Anisotropic sound speeds change the low-temperature coefficient rather than its cubic power. Real optical branches and nonlinear acoustic dispersion limit the full-temperature approximation; electronic excitations can add a linear term in a metal, and anharmonicity produces thermal expansion and distinctions between CP​ and CV​. At temperatures below the lowest finite-sample mode, the bulk continuum law also fails. Thus the T3 result is the low-temperature lattice contribution, not a universal total heat capacity for every solid.

For a circular structure, cylindrical symmetry reduces the scalar Fresnel-Kirchhoff diffraction integral to concentric annuli. With plane-wave illumination, radius a and distance z, the relevant Fresnel number is NF​=a2/(λz). Successive Fresnel half-period zones have radii rj2​≃jλz: their areas are approximately equal while their phases alternate by π, causing neighbouring-zone cancellation.
For Fresnel diffraction by a circular aperture, the on-axis field follows directly from the radial integral,
Ufree​Uap​​=iλz1​∫0a​eiπr2/(λz)2πrdr=1−eiπNF​,Iap​/Ifree​=4sin2(πNF​/2)​.
(41)
Odd zone counts reinforce on axis; even counts cancel in this ideal scalar model. An annular opening gives the difference of the corresponding two edge exponentials. A zone plate alternately blocks or phase-shifts zones so that otherwise cancelling contributions reinforce at a focus, with rj2​≃jλf for its principal focal distance.
Babinet's principle gives Udisk​=Ufree​−Uap​=Ufree​eiπNF​ for Fresnel diffraction by an opaque disk. Thus its ideal on-axis intensity equals the unobstructed intensity, the Arago spot, even within the geometric shadow. This concerns a narrow axial feature, not the whole shadow. Finite source size, imperfect circularity, bandwidth and alignment reduce its visibility. When NF​≪1, the circular-aperture far-field pattern becomes the Airy disk intensity pattern [2J1​(u)/u]2, with u=kasinθ and first minimum near θ=1.22λ/(2a).

Two parallel partially reflecting surfaces in a Fabry–Pérot interferometer produce a sequence of coherent transmitted beams. For equal lossless mirror intensity reflectance R and round-trip phase δ=4πndcosθt​/λ, summing the geometric series of amplitudes gives
Ei​Et​​=1−Reiδ(1−R)eiδ/2​,T=1+Fsin2(δ/2)1​,F=(1−R)24R​​.
(42)
resonances have 2ndcosθt​=mλ and unit peak transmission in this symmetric lossless model. Varying angle gives concentric equal-inclination rings; changing spacing scans the resonance wavelengths.
At normal incidence with negligible dispersion, the free spectral range is ΔνFSR​=c/(2nd). For high reflectance, the peak full width gives finesse and resolving power
F≃1−RπR​​,δνν​=mF​.

Adjacent orders can overlap in a broadband measurement, requiring order selection or prior spectral information.

For N uniformly illuminated identical slits of a diffraction grating, with width b and period d, the far-field amplitude is a single-slit transform times a finite phasor sum. At normal incidence, write β=πdsinθ/λ. Then
I(θ)=I1,0​[πbsinθ/λsin(πbsinθ/λ)​]2(sinβsinNβ​)2​.
(44)
Principal maxima obey dsinθ=mλ and have N2 times the single-slit intensity at that angle. Nearby first minima are separated from a principal maximum by δθ≃λ/(Ndcosθm​), so more illuminated slits sharpen each order. The broad single-slit envelope suppresses high angles; an order is missing if its principal maximum coincides with an envelope zero.
Differentiating the grating equation gives angular dispersion dθ/dλ=m/(dcosθm​). Comparing the separation of nearby wavelengths with the first-minimum width yields the Rayleigh resolving power
λ/δλ=∣m∣N​.
(45)
Only propagating orders with ∣mλ∣≤d exist. Different spectral orders can overlap, and finite source-slit width, limited coherence, aberrations and grating errors worsen ideal resolution. A spectrometer therefore collimates light from an entrance slit and focuses each diffracted direction onto a detector; the slit image width and illuminated grating width must both support the desired spectral separation.
