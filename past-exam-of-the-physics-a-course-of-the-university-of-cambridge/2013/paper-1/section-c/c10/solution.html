A pure quantum state is a unit ray in a complex Hilbert space: normalization fixes total probability, a common phase is unobservable, and superpositions retain relative phases. Observables are self-adjoint operators. For an ideal projective measurement with spectral projector Pa​, the Born rule gives p(a)=⟨ψ∣Pa​∣ψ⟩ and the Lüders rule gives conditional state Pa​ψ/p(a)​. This preserves an eigenspace, not necessarily a unique vector when the outcome is degenerate. Between measurements, a closed system evolves unitarily according to iℏ∂t​ψ=Hψ. Composite systems use tensor products; quantum statistics additionally restrict identical-particle states. These are a framework supported by many experiments, not separate logical deductions from any one experiment.
In the photoelectric effect, monochromatic light illuminates a clean metal and a retarding potential measures the largest emitted electron kinetic energy. frequency controls the stopping energy, eVs​=hν−Φ, while in the one-photon regime intensity mainly controls the emission rate. A threshold frequency and a stopping-energy slope h/e support quantized energy exchange rather than gradual absorption of arbitrary classical-wave energy by each electron. Contact potentials, surface contamination, dark current and spectral impurities require controls. Robert Andrews Millikan tested the frequency–stopping-potential relation with careful surface and electrical control.[ref]
The Stern-Gerlach experiment sends a collimated neutral silver beam through an inhomogeneous magnetic field onto a detector. The force is approximately Fz​=μz​∂z​B in an appropriate strong-field electronic description. Two resolved components rather than a continuous classical orientation spread support discrete angular-momentum projections. Field-off beam profiles and magnetic-gradient/beam-width controls distinguish splitting from ordinary broadening. In the modern description the silver ground electronic state has J=1/2; the 1922 experiment preceded the electron-spin interpretation. Ideal sequential analyzers along different axes illustrate incompatible observables and state preparation, but were not the original apparatus.[ref]
In the Davisson–Germer experiment, accelerated electrons scatter from a nickel single crystal and an angularly scanned collector measures the scattered intensity. Changing voltage and crystal orientation changes narrow diffraction maxima. The inferred wavelength agrees with λ=h/p, with nonrelativistic p=2meV​. vacuum, crystal cleaning, energy selection and background measurements matter: adsorbed surface gas can produce additional diffraction features. This supports matter-wave amplitudes and interference, with measured intensities related to their squared magnitude; it does not mean an electron must follow a classical orbit through the apparatus.[ref] Together these experiments motivate quantized outcomes, wave amplitudes and probabilistic measurement while leaving a distinction between observed evidence and the general postulates used to organize it.
