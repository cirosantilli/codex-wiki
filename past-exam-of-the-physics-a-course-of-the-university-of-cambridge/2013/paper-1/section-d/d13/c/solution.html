Identify the dominant heat paths and the experiment's temperature coefficients before choosing insulation. Reduce convection with an enclosure or, where appropriate, vacuum; reduce conduction through supports and leads with suitable geometry and thermal anchoring; use thermal radiation shields and low-emissivity surfaces to reduce radiative exchange. vacuum alone does not remove heat conduction through wires or thermal radiation.

Thermal mass and insulation suppress rapid fluctuations but only delay slow ambient drift: the zero-frequency gain remains one. Active temperature control, a stable reference environment, adequate equilibration time and an appropriate sensor location address long-term stability. Self-heating produces a steady offset δTs​=δP/G; increasing insulation can worsen that offset and settling time. Check temperature gradients rather than relying on a remote thermometer.
Match materials or use differential/reference measurements to reduce expansion and resistance drift; keep dissimilar-metal junctions isothermal to suppress thermoelectric offsets. Log temperature alongside the signal and deliberately vary environmental temperature to measure residual coupling, using a physically justified correction with uncertainty. Interleave calibrations and allow equilibration after changing power. Feedback must remain stable, and heater/sensor electronics should not introduce electrical interference into the measurement.
