A1 OurBigBook.com
For a neutral atom, count both the nucleons and the electrons. Protons, neutrons and electrons are all fermions, and exchanging two identical intact atoms produces the sign (−1)Nf​ from exchanging their fermionic constituents. An odd number gives a composite fermion; an even number gives a composite boson, consistently with the Spin-statistics theorem.
A lithium atom has three protons and three electrons. Lithium-6 also has three neutrons, giving 3+3+3=9 fermionic constituents; lithium-7 has four neutrons, giving 3+4+3=10. Therefore
6Li atoms are fermions,7Li atoms are bosons.​
(1)
The composite statistics of neutral atoms cannot be inferred from the mass number alone: counting only nucleons would classify the nuclei and give the opposite answers here. The atom's total spin includes its electrons as well as its nucleus.

The postulates of quantum mechanics give a framework for predicting observations from a prepared state. The experiments below motivate different features of that framework; no one experiment proves the whole set of postulates or specifies every Hamiltonian operator.
A pure quantum state is a normalized vector in a complex Hilbert space, with vectors differing by an overall quantum phase representing the same physical preparation. In position representation, ∫∣ψ(r)∣2d3r=1. The linear state space permits quantum superposition, while the Born rule assigns probability ∫Ω​∣ψ∣2d3r to a position outcome in a region Ω. The wavefunction is a probability amplitude; it is not a classical distribution of definite particle positions and momenta.
An observable is represented by a self-adjoint operator. If Q has spectral projectors Pq​, its possible measurement outcomes are its spectral values and Pr(q)=⟨ψ∣Pq​∣ψ⟩. The mean is ⟨Q⟩=⟨ψ∣Q∣ψ⟩. For an ideal repeatable projective measurement, obtaining q leaves the normalized conditional state Pq​ψ/Pr(q)​. This projects onto an entire eigenspace if the outcome is degenerate. General detectors need more general measurement in quantum mechanics models; the projective update is an idealization. Noncommuting observables need not have simultaneous sharp values, as the canonical commutation relation and Heisenberg uncertainty relation illustrate.
Between measurements, an isolated system has unitary time evolution generated by its Hamiltonian operator:
iℏ∂t∂∣ψ⟩​=H∣ψ⟩,U(t)=e−iHt/ℏfor fixed H.
(46)
This preserves normalization and superpositions. Composite systems use a tensor product of state spaces, allowing entangled states as well as product states; identical particles additionally require the bosonic or fermionic exchange symmetry appropriate to their species. Statistical mixtures are described by a density matrix, with Pr(q)=tr(ρPq​). These rules separate preparation, coherent evolution and measurement.
The photoelectric effect exposes discrete energy transfer by light. Illuminate a clean metal cathode in an evacuated tube and measure the collected electron current while varying the light frequency, intensity and a retarding voltage. The stopping potential Vs​ probes the largest emitted electron kinetic energy, whereas the photocurrent measures the rate at which charge is collected. In the ordinary single-photon regime there is a threshold frequency; above it the maximum energy grows with frequency, rather than with intensity. Changing intensity at fixed frequency mainly changes the number of emitted electrons. These observations are summarized by the Einstein photoelectric equation
Kmax​=hν−Φ=eVs​,ν0​=Φ/h.​
(47)
Here Φ is the surface work function, and the stopping voltage is understood after any photoelectric contact-potential correction. A photon delivers hν in an absorption event; emission is allowed only when that energy can pay the escape cost. This accounts for the frequency threshold and the lack of the classical waiting time associated with slowly accumulating energy in a single electron. The experiment supports energy quantization of light; it does not imply that every incident photon emits an electron or that every emitted electron has the maximum energy. Surface losses and collection geometry affect the full current-voltage curve.
The Davisson–Germer experiment demonstrates a complementary wave property of matter. An electron gun uses a heated emitter, accelerating voltage and apertures to send a narrow beam onto a nickel single crystal in vacuum. A movable collector measures scattered electric current versus direction; rotating the crystal also tests its azimuthal symmetry. The crystal gives sharp angular beams whose positions depend on voltage and crystal orientation. A prominent feature occurs near 54V accelerating voltage and roughly 50∘ detector colatitude. The nonrelativistic de Broglie wavelength predicted at that voltage is
λ=2me​eU​h​≃0.167nm.
(48)
Diffraction wavelengths inferred using the known crystal structure agree with this voltage dependence. These angular peaks arise from coherent addition of scattering amplitudes from the crystal, not from classical specular reflection of independent point projectiles. Bragg's law is 2dsinθB​=rλ for lattice-plane spacing d and angle to those planes; a detector colatitude is a different angle and must not be substituted without its geometry. The historical readout was current, not a photograph of individual electron trajectories.[ref]
The double-slit experiment makes the role of amplitudes especially direct. A coherent source illuminates two narrow slits; an observation screen a distance D away records the pattern. For slit spacing d and small screen displacement y, the path difference is approximately dy/D. The two amplitudes add before probabilities are formed:
P(y)=∣ψ1​(y)+ψ2​(y)∣2=∣ψ1​∣2+∣ψ2​∣2+2Re(ψ1∗​ψ2​).​
(49)
For equal coherent illumination the interference term varies as cos(2πdy/(λD)), giving fringe spacing λD/d beneath the single-slit diffraction envelope. Blocking one slit removes that cross term. Young's optical experiment established wave interference; later particle-resolved realizations with photons or electrons show localized detection events whose accumulated distribution has the interference pattern. Low arrival rates demonstrate that this pattern does not require different incident particles to collide with one another.
If the paths become correlated with distinguishable detector states ∣D1​⟩ and ∣D2​⟩, the cross term acquires their overlap ⟨D2​∣D1​⟩. Perfectly distinguishable path records make that overlap zero, suppressing interference even if the spatial beams still overlap. This connects quantum entanglement, measurement and loss of observable coherence. Without a path measurement, the two alternatives are amplitudes for one preparation, rather than classical mutually exclusive events whose probabilities may simply be added.
Together these experiments motivate a description in which energy transfer and detector outcomes can be discrete, yet propagation retains complex quantum phases and interference. The Born rule connects those amplitudes to repeatable frequencies of outcomes. The Schrödinger equation supplies the dynamics once a Hamiltonian operator is specified, while observable operators, state preparation and the measurement model specify what the apparatus can read.

Indistinguishable particles have the same intrinsic properties and cannot be assigned physically observable permanent labels. Exchanging their mathematical coordinates leaves all physical predictions unchanged. In ordinary three-dimensional quantum mechanics the total state, including spin, obeys
P12​Ψ=+Ψfor bosons,P12​Ψ=−Ψfor fermions.​
(50)
The Spin-statistics theorem associates integer spin with bosons and half-integer spin with fermions. Exchange does not mean physically transporting two particles along some prescribed trajectories; it is the symmetry of the many-particle state under permutation of its arguments.
For orthonormal one-particle states u,v, normalized two-particle spatial combinations are [u(1)v(2)±v(1)u(2)]/2​. They illustrate coherent exchange amplitudes. If both fermions have the same complete one-particle state, the antisymmetric combination vanishes. This is the Pauli exclusion principle: a fermionic mode has occupation zero or one. Bosonic modes can have arbitrarily large occupation. A many-fermion product basis is antisymmetrized by a Slater determinant.
It is the full spin-spatial state that must have the appropriate symmetry. For two electrons, an antisymmetric spin singlet state can accompany a symmetric spatial orbital, so opposite-spin electrons can occupy the same spatial orbital. A symmetric spin triplet instead needs an antisymmetric spatial state. Thus exclusion concerns identical complete states, not an unconditional prohibition on sharing a position or spatial orbital.
Counting allowed occupations gives Bose-Einstein statistics and Fermi-Dirac statistics. For an ideal gas in thermal equilibrium,
ni​=e(ϵi​−μ)/(kB​T)−11​for bosons,ni​=e(ϵi​−μ)/(kB​T)+11​for fermions.
(51)
Bosonic accumulation permits Bose-Einstein condensation; exclusion gives a filled Fermi sea and degeneracy pressure even at zero temperature. In the dilute classical limit the occupations are small and both expressions approach the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. Exchange effects become important when wavepackets overlap appreciably, often characterized by number density times the cube of the thermal wavelength.
Composite particles follow their total exchange statistics while remaining intact. The composite statistics of neutral atoms depend on electrons as well as nucleons, as in the two lithium isotopes above. Symmetrization can produce bunching or antibunching in joint detection probabilities even in the absence of an interaction potential; it is not an additional classical force.

Quantum tunnelling is transmission through a finite classically forbidden region, where E<V(x). The stationary Schrödinger equation permits an evanescent wavefunction inside such a barrier rather than forcing it to vanish at its entrance. Matching to propagating states beyond a barrier of finite width gives a nonzero transmitted probability current.
For a rectangular barrier of height V0​ and width a, with equal exterior potentials and 0<E<V0​, write κ=2m(V0​−E)​/ℏ. The exact rectangular-barrier tunnelling probability is
T=[1+4E(V0​−E)V02​sinh2(κa)​]−1.
(54)
For an opaque barrier its dominant dependence is e−2κa. For a slowly varying barrier, the WKB approximation generalizes this to T∼exp[−(2/ℏ)∫x1​x2​​2m(V(x)−E)​dx], apart from matching prefactors. Barrier width, particle mass and energy therefore have a strong influence. Energy is conserved in a static barrier: tunnelling does not require the particle to borrow energy or acquire a classical path with negative kinetic energy.
In alpha decay, a nuclear alpha-particle configuration is confined by the short-range nuclear interaction but separated from the exterior by a Coulomb barrier. Its energy can be positive relative to infinity while still lying below the barrier maximum. Escape by tunnelling produces alpha particles at that conserved energy, with an exponentially small decay probability per nuclear encounter. The pronounced dependence of half-life on alpha energy is a characteristic consequence of the barrier exponent, unlike a simple classical escape threshold.
In scanning tunneling microscopy, a conducting tip is brought within a nanometer-scale vacuum gap of a conducting surface and biased weakly. Electron tunnelling gives a current that depends approximately exponentially on separation, I∝Ve−2κs in a simple low-bias model. Feedback adjusts the tip height to keep current fixed while scanning, producing a surface map sensitive to both geometry and electronic states. Finite current through the non-contact gap, and its steep dependence on separation, are the direct tunnelling signatures; current is not a direct measurement of topographic height alone.
A Josephson junction provides a collective superconducting example. Two superconductors separated by a thin barrier can carry a coherent pair current I=Ic​sinφ even at zero voltage. At constant voltage the AC Josephson effect gives dφ/dt=2eV/ℏ and oscillation frequency 2eV/h. The coupled condensate quantum phases and pair transfer distinguish this from independent normal-electron transmission. These examples involve different microscopic barriers, but all require quantum coupling across a region that suppresses classical passage. Comparing weak-temperature tunnelling behavior with thermally activated escape helps distinguish the mechanisms experimentally.

A chi-squared test compares residuals with the random scatter predicted by a model. For independent measurements yi​ with known Gaussian standard deviations σi​ and predictions μi​(θ), form
Q=∑i=1N​σi2​[yi​−μi​(θ)]2​.
(55)
If the mean model and error assumptions are correct, a full-rank linear fit of p parameters gives a chi-squared distribution with ν=N−p statistical degrees of freedom. With a fully specified prediction and no fitted parameters, ν=N. Nonlinear fits usually make this an approximation. Independent external calibration is not the same as fitting a parameter to these test data.
Under the reference distribution, the mean statistic is ν and its variance is 2ν. The upper-tail p-value Pr(χν2​≥Qobs​) quantifies how unusual a discrepancy at least this large would be under the assumed model. A small value suggests model failure or incorrect uncertainties; it is not the probability that the model is true. An unusually small statistic can also reveal inflated uncertainties or correlations. A rejection threshold should be specified in advance.
For independent Poisson distribution counts, the model variance is its expected count μi​. The Pearson chi-squared statistic is ∑(yi​−μi​)2/μi​. The estimated-error alternative σi​≃yi​​ is common for large counts but can differ substantially when counts are small. In that regime the Poisson deviance or a direct likelihood calculation is preferable, with simulation if an accurately calibrated goodness-of-fit probability is required.

Bayes' theorem updates prior knowledge using the likelihood of new observations. For alternative hypotheses Hj​,
P(Hj​∣D)=∑k​P(D∣Hk​)P(Hk​)P(D∣Hj​)P(Hj​)​.​
(66)
The prior P(Hj​) describes information before the data D; P(D∣Hj​) is the sampling likelihood, and the posterior P(Hj​∣D) describes the updated probabilities. Posterior odds equal prior odds times the likelihood ratio. A likelihood is a probability model for data at specified parameters, not automatically a normalized probability distribution over those parameters.
For a continuous parameter θ, the same rule uses densities: π(θ∣D)=L(D∣θ)π(θ)/∫L(D∣u)π(u)du. Priors can encode positivity, physical limits, previous independent measurements or calibration knowledge. Their parameterization matters: a density uniform in a rate is not uniform in its logarithm. Proper priors make model evidences well defined; an arbitrary normalization in an improper prior is especially problematic for comparing models.
A counting example uses Poisson-gamma conjugacy. If a detector registers K events in exposure t with constant rate λ, L∝λKe−tλ. A gamma prior with shape α and rate parameter β, π(λ)∝λα−1e−βλ, gives
λ∣K∼Gamma(α+K,β+t),E[λ∣K]=β+tα+K​.​
(67)
The posterior mean combines the prior rate α/β with the observed rate K/t, weighted by the prior and actual exposures. The parameters must use a consistent shape-rate convention. Small counts can leave substantial prior sensitivity; abundant informative data usually reduce it for regular identifiable models.
Independent old data can become the prior for a new experiment, giving the same result as joint updating under a consistent model. The new data must not also have been used to construct that prior, or the evidence is counted twice. A credible interval has a specified posterior probability under the chosen model and prior; this interpretation differs from repeated-sampling coverage of a confidence interval. Predictions for a future observation average its sampling distribution over the posterior, incorporating parameter uncertainty. Prior sensitivity checks and a defensible likelihood are therefore part of the inference, rather than Bayes' theorem itself supplying missing physical knowledge.

A systematic error is a reproducible bias in the measurement model or procedure, such as a zero offset, incorrect gain, background contamination, misalignment, loading or temperature-dependent calibration. Repeating the same biased procedure more often reduces independent random scatter but does not remove the bias. An unknown correction also has a measurement uncertainty, even when the mechanism is systematic.
A simple instrument model is y=(1+g)x+a+d(t)+ϵ, with gain error g, offset a, drift d(t) and random noise ϵ. Comparison with calibrated standards at several input values separates an offset from a scale error and can test nonlinearity. Use standards that cover the operating range, verify units and reference values, and propagate uncertainty in the standards into the corrected result. Calibration changes the model; it does not make the corrected measurement exact.
A blank or source-off measurement estimates additive background. For independent source-plus-background count Ks​ in time ts​ and background count Kb​ in time tb​, the net-rate estimate and its counting variance are
S=ts​Ks​​−tb​Kb​​,Var(S)≃ts2​Ks​​+tb2​Kb​​.
(68)
Subtracting a measured background removes its estimated mean, not its fluctuations or its uncertainty. A long background exposure can reduce the latter, while drifting backgrounds require monitoring or a joint model.
Reversal can distinguish a wanted signal from an unchanged offset. For example, V(+I)=IR+Vth​ and V(−I)=−IR+Vth​ give R=[V(+I)−V(−I)]/(2I) when the thermoelectric offset is stable between readings. Interchanging sample and reference, reversing field direction or reversing an apparatus orientation can similarly expose sign-dependent biases, but only if the wanted and unwanted contributions transform differently.
Track slow instrumental drift using frequent references, interleaved readings or an ABBA measurement sequence. Equal-time readings in the order A, B, B, A cancel a common linear drift in the difference estimator (y1​+y2​−y0​−y3​)/2. Nonlinear drift or unequal sample conditions can survive. Randomizing run order helps prevent a time drift from imitating dependence on a controlled experimental variable.
Control physical couplings directly: stabilize temperature, minimize gradients, shield unwanted fields, align geometry, and use high input impedance when loading is the concern. Differential and bridge measurements suppress common changes when their channels are well matched. Varying an apparatus parameter or using an independent method can reveal an unsuspected bias. Report residual corrections, their uncertainties and correlations; adding more identical readings cannot justify discarding a shared calibration uncertainty.

Johnson-Nyquist noise is equilibrium electrical fluctuation caused by thermal agitation of charge carriers. A resistor can generate a fluctuating terminal voltage even when its mean current is zero. In the classical frequency range hf≪kB​T, the fluctuation-dissipation theorem relates this noise to dissipative resistance. The one-sided voltage power spectral density of an unloaded resistor is
SV(1)​(f)=4kB​TRV2/Hz.​
(69)
Equivalently its short-circuit current spectrum is 4kB​T/R. Over a flat bandwidth B the rms voltage is 4kB​TRB​. A real measurement instead integrates SV​(f)∣H(f)∣2 over positive frequency, with loading and the filter's effective noise bandwidth included. A flat spectrum does not imply an infinite physically measurable variance; the circuit and the quantum high-frequency regime limit the ideal white-noise model.
Cooling or narrowing the accepted bandwidth reduces thermal noise. Reducing resistance lowers its open-circuit voltage noise but raises its short-circuit current noise, so the appropriate choice depends on source impedance and amplifier loading. Thermal agitation is not a fluctuating externally applied voltage that can be removed by perfect shielding alone.
Shot noise arises from discrete transfer events. In the simplest shot noise counting model, equal charges q arrive independently at constant rate r. A gate of duration t contains a Poisson number K, with mean and variance rt. Therefore mean current is I=qr, and the averaged-current variance is qI/t. The corresponding one-sided low-frequency spectrum is
SI(1)​(f)=2qI,Irms,noise​=2qIB​​
(70)
for a flat accepted bandwidth. Electron current has q=e. Vacuum emission or photodetection can approximate independent arrivals; correlations in a conductor can suppress or enhance the fluctuations. Discreteness by itself does not guarantee Poisson statistics.
For a fixed mean current the ideal shot-noise formula is not proportional to temperature, whereas Johnson noise is. Increasing current raises absolute shot noise but lowers its fractional size, since 2qIB​/I∝I−1/2. Both can appear approximately white within a useful frequency band, and independent noise powers add after referring them to a common measurement point. Their spectra must use the same convention: two-sided densities are half these one-sided values. A real low-current experiment can also contain amplifier noise, low-frequency drift and correlated transfer noise, so a mean current or resistance alone does not specify its complete noise budget.
