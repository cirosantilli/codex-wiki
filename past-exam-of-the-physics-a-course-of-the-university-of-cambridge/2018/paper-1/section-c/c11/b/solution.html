A diatomic molecule has one internal bond-stretching vibrational coordinate. Near its equilibrium separation, approximate the bond by a spring of stiffness K and use the reduced mass μ=m1​m2​/(m1​+m2​). Its frequency is ω=K/μ​ and its quantum harmonic oscillator levels are ϵr​=(r+1/2)ℏω.
For independent molecules in thermal equilibrium, set x=ℏω/(kB​T)=θv​/T. The single-molecule canonical partition function is a geometric series:
zv​=∑r=0∞​e−(r+1/2)x=1−e−xe−x/2​.
(52)
Using U=−∂log(zvN​)/∂β, where β=1/(kB​T), gives
Uv​=Nℏω(21​+ex−11​),CV,v​=(∂T∂Uv​​)V​=NkB​(ex−1)2x2ex​.​
(53)
Per mole replace NkB​ by R. The zero-point energy is present in Uv​ but is temperature-independent and contributes nothing to the heat capacity.
For T≪θv​, occupation of the first excited vibrational state is exponentially suppressed: CV,v​≃NkB​x2e−x→0. The vibrational degree of freedom is frozen out. For T≫θv​, CV,v​→NkB​. This recovers classical equipartition: the quadratic kinetic term and quadratic elastic term each contribute kB​/2 per molecule.
The vibrational heat capacity of a diatomic gas must be distinguished from the total heat capacity. In a dilute gas with rotations already active and vibration still frozen, translation contributes 3R/2 and rotation R, so molar CV​≃5R/2. Once vibration is active it adds R, giving CV​≃7R/2. For an ideal gas of a fixed number of intact molecules, CP​=CV​+R, so the corresponding values are 7R/2 and 9R/2. Very low temperatures also freeze rotations, while sufficiently high temperatures bring anharmonicity, dissociation or electronic excitations; the harmonic plateau is restricted to the range where those effects are negligible.
