Johnson-Nyquist noise is equilibrium electrical fluctuation caused by thermal agitation of charge carriers. A resistor can generate a fluctuating terminal voltage even when its mean current is zero. In the classical frequency range hf≪kB​T, the fluctuation-dissipation theorem relates this noise to dissipative resistance. The one-sided voltage power spectral density of an unloaded resistor is
SV(1)​(f)=4kB​TRV2/Hz.​
(69)
Equivalently its short-circuit current spectrum is 4kB​T/R. Over a flat bandwidth B the rms voltage is 4kB​TRB​. A real measurement instead integrates SV​(f)∣H(f)∣2 over positive frequency, with loading and the filter's effective noise bandwidth included. A flat spectrum does not imply an infinite physically measurable variance; the circuit and the quantum high-frequency regime limit the ideal white-noise model.
Cooling or narrowing the accepted bandwidth reduces thermal noise. Reducing resistance lowers its open-circuit voltage noise but raises its short-circuit current noise, so the appropriate choice depends on source impedance and amplifier loading. Thermal agitation is not a fluctuating externally applied voltage that can be removed by perfect shielding alone.
Shot noise arises from discrete transfer events. In the simplest shot noise counting model, equal charges q arrive independently at constant rate r. A gate of duration t contains a Poisson number K, with mean and variance rt. Therefore mean current is I=qr, and the averaged-current variance is qI/t. The corresponding one-sided low-frequency spectrum is
SI(1)​(f)=2qI,Irms,noise​=2qIB​​
(70)
for a flat accepted bandwidth. Electron current has q=e. Vacuum emission or photodetection can approximate independent arrivals; correlations in a conductor can suppress or enhance the fluctuations. Discreteness by itself does not guarantee Poisson statistics.
For a fixed mean current the ideal shot-noise formula is not proportional to temperature, whereas Johnson noise is. Increasing current raises absolute shot noise but lowers its fractional size, since 2qIB​/I∝I−1/2. Both can appear approximately white within a useful frequency band, and independent noise powers add after referring them to a common measurement point. Their spectra must use the same convention: two-sided densities are half these one-sided values. A real low-current experiment can also contain amplifier noise, low-frequency drift and correlated transfer noise, so a mean current or resistance alone does not specify its complete noise budget.
