A dispersion relation specifies the allowed angular frequency ω(k) of a wavevector k. A single harmonic wave Acos(kx−ωt) has constant-phase surfaces moving at the phase velocity,
vp​=ω/k​.
(88)
For two nearby components, use the identity
cos(k1​x−ω1​t)+cos(k2​x−ω2​t)=2cos(2Δkx−Δωt​)cos(kˉx−ωˉt).
(89)
The slowly varying envelope moves at Δω/Δk. Taking a narrowband limit gives the group velocity,
vg​=dkdω​=vp​+kdkdvp​​​.
(90)
More generally, expand ω(k) around the central wavevector of a wavepacket. The linear term translates its envelope at vg​; the quadratic and higher terms produce group velocity dispersion and packet distortion. This interpretation needs a sufficiently narrow spectrum and weak enough absorption and distortion for an identifiable envelope to persist.
For light in a transparent material, k=n(ω)ω/c, so
vp​=nc​​,vg​=n+ωdn/dωc​=n−λ0​dn/dλ0​c​​.
(91)
The derivative is evaluated at the wavepacket's central frequency and λ0​ is the vacuum wavelength. In a nondispersive medium with constant n, the two velocities coincide and a packet can propagate without dispersion. In normal optical dispersion dn/dλ0​<0, the displayed model gives vg​<vp​.
The two velocities can differ even without material dispersion: a waveguide mode with ω2=ωc2​+c2k2 has vp​=ω/k>c and vg​=c2k/ω<c, with vp​vg​=c2. The phase velocity is not a signal speed. Near strongly absorbing resonances an anomalous derivative can also make the group velocity exceed c or become negative; reshaping then prevents its simple identification with information transport. Relativistic causality constrains the signal front, not the speed of an arbitrary moving phase or distorted intensity maximum.
