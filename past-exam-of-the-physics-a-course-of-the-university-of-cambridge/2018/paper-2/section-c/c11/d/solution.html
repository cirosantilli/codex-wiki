In a normal metal, the electrical resistivity can often be approximated using Matthiessen's rule,
ρel​(T)≃ρresidual​+ρphonon​(T).
(74)
Static impurities, defects and grain boundaries scatter conduction electrons even as T→0, giving the residual resistivity. Thermal lattice phonons add a temperature-dependent contribution through electron-phonon scattering. In a clean three-dimensional acoustic model, small-angle electron-phonon transport at low temperature gives ρphonon​∝T5 at low T; once thermal phonon populations are approximately classical, the resistivity is approximately linear in T. An electron-electron contribution proportional to T2 can be relevant in some low-temperature ranges.
Carbon in mild steel, together with associated disorder, strain and microstructural changes, adds scattering and generally raises the residual resistivity relative to purer iron. Thus a dilute alloy may have a similar broad phonon-dependent rise but a larger baseline. The sketch is a generic phonon-plus-disorder model, not measured iron data. Iron's magnetic scattering and phase changes, and real steel's phases and processing history, can add structure; the simple sum is approximate rather than an exact material law. The expected explanation is greater electron scattering from the added carbon and resulting disorder.
