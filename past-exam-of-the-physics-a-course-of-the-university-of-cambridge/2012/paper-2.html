The Arago spot is a bright axial point inside the geometrical shadow of a circular opaque object. Under coherent illumination, contributions from points around its circular edge have equal optical path to an axial observation point and interfere constructively. It is a Fresnel diffraction effect, so geometrical rays alone miss it. In the ideal paraxial plane-wave model, Babinet's principle gives Udisk​=U0​−Uaperture​=U0​eika2/(2z) on axis for disk radius a; thus the axial intensity equals the unobstructed intensity in that model. The whole shadow is not bright: off-axis phases differ and form a localized central feature. Finite source size, poor coherence or an imperfect edge reduce its visibility.

At long wavelength, the acoustic phonon branch moves the two atoms in a cell nearly in phase: uM​≃um​. In the q→0 limit this is rigid translation and ω→0. The optical phonon branch moves them oppositely, with stationary cell centre of mass: MuM​+mum​=0. Its q=0 frequency is finite because adjacent unlike atoms change their separation. For unequal masses the lighter atom has the larger optical displacement. Three-dimensional crystals have three acoustic branches and, with two atoms per primitive cell, three optical branches; longitudinal and transverse directions describe displacement relative to the wavevector. The name optical does not mean that every optical phonon branch is infrared-active: coupling also depends on effective charges and symmetry.

For a narrow packet, phase velocity is up​=ω/k and group velocity is ug​=dω/dk. Set b=σ/ρ1​. The uncovered surface has
up2​=kg​+bk,up​ug​​=2(g+bk2)g+3bk2​.
(4)
Equality occurs at k∗2​=g/b, also the minimum of up​. Numerically,
k∗​=368.9 m−1,λ∗​=1.703 cm,up​=ug​=(4gb)1/4=0.2305 ms−1​.
(5)
The long-wave gravity limit has ug​/up​→1/2; the short-wave capillary limit has ug​/up​→3/2.
A compact packet subsequently spreads because different wavenumbers have different group speeds. In the gravity regime ω≃gk​, so smaller k and longer waves move ahead. Individual crests move twice as fast as the envelope, appearing at its rear and disappearing at its front. In the capillary regime ω≃b​k3/2, shorter waves move ahead; the envelope overtakes its slower individual crests, which drift backward relative to it. Thus both the wavelength ordering within the spread packet and the crest motion relative to its envelope reverse.
For the sheet define c=aρ2​/ρ1​=8.0×10−5 m. The elastic-cover frequency ratio for water waves is
F(k)=ω0​ωs​​=[1+ck1+Bk4/(ρ1​g+σk2)​]1/2​.
(6)
At small k, F=1−ck/2+O(k2): sheet inertia lowers the frequency, while bending first enters at higher order. At sufficiently large k, bending dominates the restoring force and sheet inertia dominates the extra mass, giving F∼B/(σc)​k1/2. The curve initially dips below one, reaches a minimum, then rises and crosses one. This asymptote describes the supplied ideal dispersion law; arbitrarily large k eventually violates a thin-sheet continuum model.
For a pinned-square surface displacement mode, knm​=πn2+m2​/L with n,m≥1. The fundamental has k11​=74.05 m−1. The uncovered frequency is 4.3729 Hz and F(k11​)=0.997051, so
fs​=4.3600 Hz,Δf=−0.0129 Hz​.
(7)
This uses a square two-dimensional surface mode, not the one-dimensional π/L estimate. A clamped plate or additional hydraulic boundary conditions would require a different mode calculation.

The angle in the source ray sketch is inside the gap. Two extra reflections add a full round trip. Comparing adjacent emerging rays on a common outgoing phase plane subtracts the lateral-path contribution, so the optical path difference is 2μdcosθ, rather than 2μd/cosθ. Thus
δ=2k0​μdcosθ​,δ=2πm

at transmission maxima. The two internal reflection phases add to an integer full cycle in the stated ideal symmetric-coating model. Snell's law, μsinθ=μ0​sinθout​, converts to the external direction. A fixed internal angle defines a cone and hence an equal-inclination ring in a focal plane.
For identical lossless mirrors the first transmitted amplitude has magnitude (1−R) times the incident amplitude. Each additional round trip multiplies it by Reiδ, because R is an intensity reflection coefficient for one mirror but the product of the two amplitude reflection magnitudes. Summing the geometric series gives
Te​=​1−Reiδ1−R​​2=1+R2−2Rcosδ(1−R)2​​.

The maximum is one in this symmetric lossless limit; absorption, unequal mirrors or additional incoherent reflections change it. At fixed angle and negligible material dispersion, mλ=2μdcosθ and adjacent orders differ by one. For m≫1,
FSRλ​≃2μdcosθλ2​​,δλ≃FFSRλ​​.

order-safe angular scanning of a Fabry–Pérot spectrum should cover the approximately 38 nm spectral span within one order while making the instrumental width smaller than 1 nm. Near normal incidence, requiring the smallest approximate FSR to exceed the span gives μd≲5.3 μm, and F should exceed roughly 40. These are design bounds, not a unique optimum. One suitable choice is
μd=5.0 μm,R=0.95,F≃61.2​.

The physical spacing is d=5.0 μm/μ; an air gap gives d≃5 μm. Approximate FSRs near normal incidence are 40.1 nm at 633 nm and 44.9 nm at 670 nm, with widths about 0.65 and 0.73 nm. For a concrete angular scan choose order m=14. The 670 nm pair lies near internal θ=20.3∘ and the 633 nm line near 27.6∘; their widths within this scan remain below 1 nm. Restricting to this calibrated order window avoids the other-order 633 nm ring near 18.3∘. Index dispersion and unknown extra source lines would need calibration or order-sorting filters.
The resolving power is approximately mF: many coherent cavity round trips create a much longer effective optical delay than one traversal. A Michelson instrument with the same available physical delay has less resolving power unless its mirror travel provides a correspondingly long maximum optical path difference. Mirror separation errors spread the round-trip phase; keeping them below a linewidth requires roughly
∣Δd∣≪2μFcosθλ​​.
(12)
For this design the scale is about 6 nm in an air gap. Both surface flatness and parallelism over the illuminated area, as well as angular collimation and coating uniformity, must support the intended finesse.

The Fraunhofer approximation drops the aperture-dependent quadratic propagation phase. For illuminated extent b and observation distance R, a sufficient paraxial condition is b2/(λR)≪1, with coherent nearly plane incident illumination. Collimation followed by observation in a lens's back focal plane produces the same angular Fourier pattern without an astronomical propagation distance.
Let (x,y) label the aperture field ψΣ​ and let (x0​,y0​) be transverse coordinates of the distant detector, with R the axial separation. Expanding the propagation distance retains a common phase and the cross term −(x0​x+y0​y)/R, while dropping the aperture's quadratic term. Summing the scalar Huygens contributions gives a field proportional to the Fourier transform at (kx0​/R,ky0​/R). In one dimension, writing q=kx0​/R, ψ​(q)=∫ψ(x)e−iqxdx gives
ψ(x)=2π1​∫ψ​(q)eiqxdq​.
(13)
Remove the known propagation prefactor and any detector-plane quadratic phase before applying this inverse transform. Recovery needs the complex diffracted field, including phase; its intensity alone is insufficient for this inversion.
The whole-aperture Fraunhofer distance of a coherent array uses the full ten-dish extent, which is about b=9D+25 m=7.225 km. Thus b2/λ≃8.7×108 m, and a conventional boundary 2b2/λ is about 1.7×109 m. Far-field propagation must be of order a million kilometres or more, with a stronger inequality for accurate Fraunhofer behaviour. Applying the condition to only one dish or one spacing would miss the array extent.
For equal synchronized dish fields, the array sum is ∑j=09​e−ijk0​Dsinθ. Its intensity is
I(θ)=I1​(θ)[sinβsin(10β)​]2,β=2k0​Dsinθ​​.
(14)
Near the centre, sinθ≃θ and the single-dish envelope I1​(θ) is nearly constant, giving the requested approximation. A uniformly illuminated circular dish instead supplies the broader envelope [2J1​(πdsinθ/λ)/(πdsinθ/λ)]2, with first zero near 1.22λ/d=2.93×10−3 rad. It cannot be ignored over the entire pattern.
Principal array maxima have sinθm​=mλ/D, height 100I1​(θm​), with spacing approximately 7.5×10−5 rad=15.5 arcseconds. There are narrow sidelobes and many grating orders within the dish envelope because D≫d. The central maximum's first zeros obey 10β=±π. By reception/transmission reciprocity, the source must therefore move through
Δθ≃10Dλ​=7.50×10−6 rad=1.55 arcseconds​
(15)
from zero-order maximum to first minimum. The equatorial geometry fixes the sweep direction; no elapsed time is requested.

⟨P⟩=21​Zω2A2​.

Displacement and transverse force are continuous at a massless junction. Since Z2​=2Z1​, the displacement amplitudes are r=(Z1​−Z2​)/(Z1​+Z2​)=−1/3 and t=2Z1​/(Z1​+Z2​)=2/3. Taking the incident amplitude real, the light-string motion is
y=A[cos(kx−ωt)−31​cos(kx+ωt)]=32A​cos(kx−ωt)+32A​sinkxsinωt.

In this standing-and-travelling decomposition of a reflected string wave, the first term travels and the second stands; the decomposition is not a separation into independent incident-power fractions because these fields interfere. The reflected wave has a phase reversal. power coefficients and standing-wave ratio are
R=∣r∣2=91​,T=Z1​Z2​​∣t∣2=98​,SWR=1−∣r∣1+∣r∣​=2​.

For a uniform inserted matching section, unequal exterior impedances require a quarter-wave impedance transformer: ZM​=Z1​Z2​​ and kM​a=(2j+1)π/2. At equal tension this means middle density 2ρ0​ and an odd-quarter-wavelength length in that section. Reflections at its two ends then cancel at the design frequency. An arbitrary density or an arbitrary half-wavelength segment cannot perfectly match these unequal exterior strings; the ideal match is frequency-selective.
amplitude scaling along a slowly tapered string instead follows from matching over an adiabatic frequency range. The source fixes frequency, while conserved power and the final characteristic impedance determine amplitude. In the heavy string,

C10 OurBigBook.com
In a periodic crystal potential, atomic levels broaden into bands of allowed bloch states. Coupling between states of the same symmetry opens avoided crossings, including Bragg-coupled states in the nearly free-electron picture. Bonding and antibonding combinations provide a complementary tight-binding picture. A semiconductor has a filled valence band and an empty conduction band at zero temperature separated by a forbidden energy interval; thermal excitation, light and doping provide mobile electrons and holes. The gap depends on the crystal's periodic potential, bonding and symmetry, not on a classical collision barrier. In a direct gap, the relevant extrema coincide in wavevector.
For the stated parabolic edges, take Ev​(k)=−ℏ2k2/(2mh∗​) and Ec​(k)=Eg​+ℏ2k2/(2me∗​). Negligible photon momentum and no phonon make the transition nearly vertical in E versus k. parabolic-band optical energy sharing divides the excess photon energy 0.32 eV in inverse proportion to the effective masses:
Ke​=0.320.14+0.40.4​=0.2370 eV,Kh​=0.320.14+0.40.14​=0.0830 eV.
(20)
Thus the initial and final electron energies, relative to the valence-band maximum, are
Ei​=−0.0830 eV,Ef​=0.4170 eV​.
(21)
Their difference is 0.5000 eV. The selected ∣k∣ is about 9.33×108 m−1; either sign gives the same energy in this symmetric approximation.
A shallow donor supplies an electron in a screened impurity potential, so its localized level lies just below the conduction edge. A shallow acceptor binds a hole and has an electron-occupation level just above the valence edge; thermal ionization supplies mobile holes. The level positions are measured relative to the relevant band edge, and deep impurities need not obey this hydrogenic approximation.
For the supplied bound-carrier mass and unit effective ionic charge, dielectric screening and effective mass change the hydrogen binding energy to
Eb​=13.61220.1​ eV=9.44 meV​.
(22)
This corresponds to Eb​/kB​≃110 K and an effective Bohr radius about 6.35 nm. The binding is shallow compared with the band gap and typical atomic binding. Trivalent denotes chemical valence, not an unscreened hydrogenic charge of three. An acceptor physically binds a hole; the question's stated 0.1me​ is used as its stipulated bound-carrier mass rather than silently replacing it by the separate band-edge 0.4me​.

With periodic boundary conditions, each k state occupies volume (2π)3/V and has two spin states. Filling a sphere gives n=kF3​/(3π2) and
EF​=2me​ℏ2​(3π2n)2/3​,D(E)=2π21​(ℏ22me​​)3/2E​,D(EF​)=2EF​3n​​.

Here D is density of states per volume and per energy; the extensive g(E) is VD(E). Both spin states are already included.
For divalent magnesium, n=8.6×1028 m−3, giving
EF​=7.10 eV,D(EF​)=1.13×1047 J−1m−3=1.82×1028 eV−1m−3​.

At T≪TF​, only electrons within about kB​T of the Fermi surface change occupancy. The Sommerfeld heat capacity is
VCe​​=3π2​kB2​D(EF​)T=71.1T Jm−3K−1​.
(25)
Equivalently Ce,m​=0.995T mJmol−1K−1, with T in kelvin, so at room temperature it is about 0.299 Jmol−1K−1. A temperature was not specified for the estimate; the linear coefficient is the general result.
At sufficiently low temperature, an ordinary nonsuperconducting metal has C≃γT+βT3: electrons dominate at the lowest temperatures, acoustic phonons produce the cubic contribution, and the lattice approaches 3R per mole as temperature exceeds its characteristic Debye scale. Anharmonic expansion can distinguish measured Cp​ from CV​. In the room-temperature regime, T/TF​≃0.0036 since TF​≃8.24×104 K, so electron excitation is strongly Pauli-limited. The free-electron estimate is only about 1.2% of the classical 3R lattice scale. Calling it negligible means small relative to the lattice contribution, not identically zero or negligible as T→0. Real band structure and interactions can alter the coefficient.

For mx¨+bx˙+kx=F0​cosωt, define ω0​=k/m​ and β=b/(2m). The parameter relating quality factor and viscous damping regimes is
Q=2βω0​​=bmω0​​​.
(26)
At weak damping it also equals 2π times stored energy divided by energy lost per cycle. The stored-energy convention and frequency matter: this cycle-based interpretation should not be extended to free motions that never complete a cycle.
For β<ω0​ the free motion is e−βt[Acosωd​t+Bsinωd​t], where ωd​=ω02​−β2​. Thus underdamping means Q>1/2. High Q gives many oscillations per amplitude-decay time, narrow resonance and strong frequency selectivity; a lightly loaded tuning fork, optical cavity or low-loss electrical resonator provides examples. The amplitude decay time is τA​=1/β=2Q/ω0​, while the slowly averaged energy decays with τE​=1/(2β)=Q/ω0​. These times differ by a factor of two.
At critical damping, β=ω0​ and Q=1/2, with x=(A+Bt)e−ω0​t. There is no ringing; a simple critically damped position indicator can settle without oscillation. For β>ω0​ and Q<1/2, there are two real negative decay rates −β±β2−ω02​​. Increasing very strong drag slows the long-time return, whose rate approaches ω02​/(2β). A strongly viscously damped mechanical indicator illustrates this regime. These classifications concern the linear homogeneous equation, not every possible nonlinear damping law.
The driven displacement amplitude is
A(ω)=(ω02​−ω2)2+4β2ω2​F0​/m​.
(27)
The displacement-resonance threshold of a viscous oscillator requires β<ω0​/2​, or Q>1/2​; it lies at ω02​−2β2​. Some underdamped systems therefore have no such maximum. Average absorbed power instead peaks at ω0​ for this viscous model. Its half-power frequencies are ω02​+β2​±β, so their separation is 2β and Q=ω0​/Δω. For broad response this ratio remains a useful parameter but not a claim of many free oscillations. The familiar displacement-linewidth version is a high-Q approximation.
A series RLC circuit has ω0​=1/LC​ and Q=ω0​L/R for the charge oscillation. Resistance dissipates energy, inductance provides the inertial analogue and capacitance the restoring analogue. Ringdown and a calibrated half-power scan independently estimate loss; parasitic resistance, loading and radiation change the measured value. A large Q increases frequency selectivity but also lengthens settling time, so it is not universally the best instrumental choice.

a OurBigBook.com
A three-phonon normal process conserves crystal wavevector without a reciprocal-lattice shift, k1​+k2​=k3​. An Umklapp process instead has
k1​+k2​=k3​+G,G=0​.
(28)
The crystal can absorb the reciprocal-lattice momentum; after reduction to the first Brillouin zone the phonon drift transporting heat is relaxed. A normal process redistributes phonons while preserving their total crystal momentum, so it is not by itself the same resistive mechanism. energy must also be conserved in either process.
At low temperature, large-wavevector phonons are rare, suppressing Umklapp scattering. Boundary and defect scattering then limit the mean free path; with a nearly constant boundary-limited path, κph​≃(Cph​/V)vℓ/3 grows as T3. As temperature increases, heat capacity rises but Umklapp scattering shortens ℓ, often producing a electrical conductivity maximum. At sufficiently high temperature a simple anharmonic model has Cph​ nearly constant and τU−1​∝T, giving κph​∝T−1. These are regime estimates, not a universal law for every material or size.

For degenerate carriers with approximately elastic scattering and a common transport relaxation time, σ=ne2τ/m∗ and κe​=(Ce​/V)vF2​τ/3. Using Ce​/V=π2nkB2​T/(2EF​) and vF2​=2EF​/m∗ gives
σTκe​​=L0​,L0​=3e2π2kB2​​=2.44×10−8 WΩK−2​.
(29)
This is the Wiedemann–Franz law. The same mobile electrons carry charge and heat, but Pauli statistics set the electronic heat capacity and hence the Lorenz number. The relation applies to electronic thermal conductivity, not automatically to total electrical conductivity including phonons. Inelastic electron-phonon scattering can relax energy and charge currents differently; nondegeneracy and strong energy dependence of scattering also limit the simple form.
A meaningful test combines a four-terminal electrical measurement with a steady thermal-gradient measurement on the same sample and temperature range. Contact heat resistance, parasitic heat leaks, phonon conduction and sample geometry must be controlled before interpreting a measured Lorenz ratio as an electronic departure from the law.

c OurBigBook.com
A carrier in a locally parabolic isotropic band obeys m∗v˙=qv×B, so
ωc​=mc​∣q∣B​,mc​=ω∣q∣Bres​​​.
(30)
Illuminate a high-mobility crystal with a transverse alternating electric field, sweep a calibrated static magnetic field at known microwave or far-infrared frequency, and detect resonant absorbed power. Cooling lengthens the relaxation time; resolving the resonance generally needs ωc​τ≫1. Varying field direction measures band anisotropy, while circular polarization distinguishes the two senses of rotation for opposite carrier charges. This is orbital motion rather than electron spin resonance.
The classic silicon/germanium measurements used single crystals near 4 K, light modulation and circularly polarized microwaves, with directional scans to infer anisotropic carrier-energy surfaces.[ref] Carrier generation, calibration, scattering linewidth and inhomogeneity must be separated from a genuine mass shift.
In a general band the cyclotron mass is an orbit property, mc​=ℏ2(∂Ak​/∂E)/(2π) for the appropriate field-normal orbit. It need not equal every component of the band-curvature mass tensor. For an ellipsoidal parabolic band with field along a principal z direction it is mx​my​​. Thus a single resonance does not determine a whole effective-mass tensor without angular information.
