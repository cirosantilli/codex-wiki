Take a p-n junction made from one semiconductor, with the p-type semiconductor on the left and the n-type semiconductor on the right. At thermal equilibrium the chemical potential μ=EF​ is horizontal throughout. Before contact the chemical potential lies nearer the valence band on the p side and nearer the conduction band on the n side; after contact the band edges bend to permit one common value.
In this orientation both the conduction band edge EC​ and the valence band edge EV​ decrease from p to n by eVbi​. For a homojunction their separation Eg​=EC​−EV​ is constant. The acceptor impurity level EA​ lies just above EV​ where acceptors are present, and the donor impurity level ED​ lies just below EC​ where donors are present. These impurity levels follow the local band bending; they do not form one additional horizontal band across the device.
The shaded depletion region has few mobile electrons or electron holes and contains fixed negative ionized acceptor impurities on the p side and positive ionized donor impurities on the n side. Thus its electric field points from n to p. Its neutral outer regions have negligible electric field, so the band edges become flat there. In the depletion approximation, charge neutrality requires NA​xp​=ND​xn​; the symmetric drawing illustrates NA​=ND​ rather than assuming that equality is universal. Diffusion and field-driven drift current cancel at equilibrium: no applied voltage does not mean zero internal electric field or zero built-in electric potential difference.

In an ordinary three-dimensional crystalline electrical insulator, heat is carried mainly by phonons. Kinetic theory gives the estimate
κ≃31​cV​vs​ℓ,
(11)
where cV​ is heat capacity per unit volume, vs​ is a typical sound speed, and ℓ is the phonon mean free path. The thermal conductivity κ has units Wm−1K−1.
At low temperature, T≪ΘD​, the Debye model gives cV​∝T3. With phonon-phonon collisions frozen out, diffuse scattering at the sample boundaries makes ℓ approximately constant, and vs​ is nearly constant. Hence
κ∝T3at low T with boundary-limited scattering.​
(12)
At high temperature, of order or above the Debye temperature, the Dulong-Petit law makes cV​ approximately constant. Anharmonic phonon scattering, especially resistive Umklapp scattering, has a collision rate approximately proportional to T. Thus ℓ∝T−1 and
κ∝T−1at high T with Umklapp-limited scattering.​
(13)
These two regimes usually give an intermediate maximum of thermal conductivity. The assumptions matter: glassy materials, strong defects or specular boundary reflection can change the low-temperature law; these are not universal laws for every electrical insulator.

A nondispersive wave has a linear dispersion relation ω=vk over the relevant range, so every Fourier transform component has the same phase velocity vp​=ω/k=v and group velocity vg​=dω/dk=v. A wave packet then translates without spreading in an ideal homogeneous lossless medium.
For wave dispersion, vp​ depends on frequency or wavenumber. Different components accumulate different phases, and in general the group velocity differs from the phase velocity; variation of group velocity across the spectrum produces spreading of a wave packet. The membrane material can be nondispersive while confinement in a membrane waveguide introduces wave dispersion along its length.

Different transverse normal modes have different cutoff frequencies and group velocities. A pulse exciting several of them reaches the output in delayed modal components, causing intermodal dispersion, pulse distortion and overlap of successive signals. Their interference also makes the transverse wave intensity pattern depend on propagation distance; irregularities can transfer energy between modes.
Mitigations are to operate between the first and second cutoff frequencies of a membrane waveguide, excite only the desired mode shape with a suitable source, and use smooth uniform boundaries or gradual transitions to reduce mode conversion. Reducing the signal bandwidth keeps its spectrum inside the single-mode interval and reduces group velocity dispersion within the selected mode. Single-mode operation removes intermodal dispersion but does not remove the dispersion derived in part (b).

For the normally incident plane wave, the incident complex amplitude and phase are uniform across the aperture. Treat the field using scalar diffraction, with the aperture represented by its prescribed amplitude transmittance h(x,y). Let R be the distance from the aperture centre to the observation point and let ℓx​,ℓy​ be the transverse direction cosines of that point.
Across an aperture of characteristic size a, the Fraunhofer diffraction assumptions are that a/R≪1, so 1/r≃1/R and the obliquity factor is nearly constant, and that the quadratic contribution to the propagation phase is negligible:
2Rka2​≪1,or, in order of magnitude,λRa2​≪1.
(27)
The remaining path-length dependence is linear:
r≃R−ℓx​x−ℓy​y.
(28)
A coherent monochromatic wave and an aperture large enough relative to wavelength for the scalar aperture model complete the usual physical assumptions. The complex amplitude becomes
ψP​=C∬h(x,y)e−ik(ℓx​x+ℓy​y)dxdy,​
(29)
where C includes the common propagation phase, distance factor and nearly constant obliquity factor. For the central part of a planar screen in the paraxial approximation, ℓx​≃X/R and ℓy​≃Y/R. Small observation angles are useful for that screen-coordinate formula; a general far-field Fourier transform can instead use the exact direction cosines. A lens can also produce this pattern in its focal plane without requiring a physically very distant screen.

The far-field complex amplitude is the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the aperture amplitude transmittance, evaluated at transverse spatial frequencies qx​=kℓx​, qy​=kℓy​. The light intensity is proportional to the squared magnitude of this transform.
Use linearity to add the transforms of simpler aperture components, preserving their relative phases. A translated component acquires a complex exponential factor. Repeated identical openings can be expressed as the convolution of one opening with a sum of Dirac delta functions; the convolution theorem then factors the field into a single-opening transform and an array factor. Multiplying aperture functions instead convolves their transforms. These operations simplify complex apertures without repeatedly evaluating the full diffraction integral. It is the complex amplitudes, rather than their wave intensities, that are added for coherent illumination.

All are below one: the ideal free-electron Fermi surface lies wholly inside the first Brillouin zone. Its nearest approach to the boundary is in three dimensions, where it lies only about 1.53% short of the nearest face. A lattice-induced band gap at a zone face therefore does not by itself make these half-filled systems insulating in the stated free-electron model. Interactions, structural changes and actual electronic band structure can alter that model prediction.

The length, area and volume of a first Brillouin zone are (2π/a)d. The corresponding occupied k-space measures are
2kF(1)​=aπ​=21​a2π​,
(55)

(56)

Hence
one Brillouin-zone measureoccupied k-space measure​=21​in all three dimensions.​
(58)
This is state counting, independent of the interval/disk/ball geometry. One Brillouin zone contains one spatial state per unit cell in a single energy band, and that band can hold two electrons per cell because of spin. One electron per cell therefore occupies half of it. The “volume enclosed” is the occupied Fermi sea, not the lower-dimensional area of the Fermi surface itself. Its half-volume does not mean half the distance to the zone boundary in two or three dimensions.

Keep the electron number fixed, so
Ne​=∫0∞​g(ϵ)pF​(ϵ)dϵ
(59)
and μ(0)=EF​. Heating smooths the Fermi-Dirac distribution across the Fermi energy, creating holes below μ and particles above it. The sign of the low-temperature adjustment of chemical potential follows from the local slope of the density of states.
In one dimension, g∝ϵ−1/2 decreases with energy. At fixed μ, smoothing removes more electrons below it than it adds above it, so μ initially rises. In three dimensions, g∝ϵ1/2 increases, so μ falls. In two dimensions the constant density of states makes the leading changes cancel, so μ stays extremely close to EF​ in the degenerate regime.
For quantitative checks, a Sommerfeld expansion gives, for kB​T≪EF​,
μ1​(T)=EF​+12π2​EF​(kB​T)2​+⋯,μ3​(T)=EF​−12π2​EF​(kB​T)2​+⋯.​
(60)
For a constant two-dimensional density of states above an unbounded parabolic band bottom, the number integral can be done exactly:
Ne​=g2​kB​Tln(1+eμ2​/(kB​T))=g2​EF​,
(61)
so
μ2​(T)=kB​Tln(eEF​/(kB​T)−1)=EF​+kB​Tln(1−e−EF​/(kB​T)).​
(62)
It actually decreases, but its low-temperature correction is exponentially small rather than quadratic. “Constant in two dimensions” is therefore a low-temperature approximation, not an exact statement for every T.
The one-dimensional rise is also only an initial trend. In the high-temperature Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution limit, the ideal d-dimensional electron gas has μ≃kB​Tln[nd​λTd​/2], where nd​=Ne​/Ld and λT​=2πℏ2/(mkB​T)​ is the thermal de Broglie wavelength. It eventually becomes negative and decreases in every dimension. The one-dimensional chemical potential thus passes through a maximum. This high-temperature statement refers to the stipulated unbounded free-electron dispersion relation, not a claim that a real finite-width crystal band remains parabolic indefinitely.

A mass M on a spring of stiffness K, subject to linear viscous damping and a sinusoidal drive, obeys the forced harmonic oscillator equation
Mx¨+2Mγx˙+Kx=F0​cosΩt,ω0​=K/M​,γ>0.
(63)
Here x is displacement, 2Mγ has units kgs−1, and F0​ is a force in newtons. A series RLC circuit is an electrical analogue, with M↔L, 2Mγ↔R, K↔1/C and x↔q.
For free motion set F0​=0 and solve the characteristic equation s2+2γs+ω02​=0. The roots s=−γ±γ2−ω02​​ distinguish three cases:
  • For underdamping, γ<ω0​, the motion is x=Ae−γtcos(ωd​t−ϕ), where ωd​=ω02​−γ2​. The amplitude decays on time 1/γ and the frequency is reduced from its undamped value.
  • At critical damping, γ=ω0​, the repeated root gives x=(A+Bt)e−ω0​t. This marks the boundary between oscillatory and nonoscillatory relaxation; it is the usual fastest settling without oscillation for a release from rest.
  • For overdamping, γ>ω0​, x=Aes+​t+Bes−​t with both roots real and negative. At large damping the slow root approaches −ω02​/(2γ), so increasing the resistance to motion can slow equilibration.
The constants are set by initial position and velocity. The mechanical energy and its rate of change in free motion are
E=21​Mx˙2+21​Kx2,dtdE​=−2Mγx˙2≤0.
(64)
For weak damping, the cycle-averaged energy decays approximately as e−2γt. The quality factor is Q=ω0​/(2γ); for Q≫1 it equals 2π times stored energy divided by the loss per cycle. Logarithmic decrement of successive free-decay peaks determines γ experimentally.
For driven motion the complete answer is the decaying free transient plus a steady response at the driving angular frequency:
xss​=X(Ω)cos(Ωt−δ),X(Ω)=(ω02​−Ω2)2+4γ2Ω2​F0​/M​,δ=atan2(2γΩ,ω02​−Ω2).​
(65)
At low frequency, X→F0​/K and the displacement is nearly in phase with the drive. At Ω=ω0​, the lag is π/2 and X=F0​/(2Mγω0​). At high frequency, X≃F0​/(MΩ2) and the lag tends to π. The displacement resonance maximum lies at
Ωr​=ω02​−2γ2​
(66)
only when γ<ω0​/2​. Thus an underdamped oscillator need not have a nonzero-frequency displacement resonance peak.
In the steady state the mean input power equals the mean dissipated power:
P=MγΩ2X2=(ω02​−Ω2)2+4γ2Ω2F02​γΩ2/M​.​
(67)
This absorption peaks exactly at Ω=ω0​, rather than at Ωr​. Its half-power points have bandwidth ΔΩ=2γ, so Q=ω0​/ΔΩ for this linear model. For weak damping, a high quality factor therefore means a narrow strong resonance and a long free-decay time. Exactly zero damping at resonant driving has no finite steady response: a secular amplitude grows with time.

To determine a crystal structure, scatter probes whose wavelength is comparable to atomic spacings, typically 0.1nm. X-ray diffraction, neutron diffraction and electron diffraction probe periodic order through coherent elastic scattering. With incident and outgoing wavevectors ki​,kf​, define Q=ki​−kf​. Elastic scattering has ∣ki​∣=∣kf​∣ and constructive interference occurs when Q=G, a reciprocal lattice vector. The equivalent Bragg's law is
2dhkl​sinθ=nλ,​
(68)
where the detector scattering angle is 2θ, not θ. Peak positions reveal plane spacings and the unit cell; systematic absent reflections constrain centring and the atomic basis.
For X-ray diffraction, a laboratory tube or a synchrotron supplies a collimated beam. In a monochromatic single-crystal experiment a goniometer rotates the specimen and an area detector records reflection positions and integrated wave intensities. A powder experiment uses many randomly oriented crystallites; the reflections form cones recorded as rings or peaks versus 2θ, permitting indexing and determination of lattice constants without selecting one crystallite. For a cubic cell, for example, dhkl​=a/h2+k2+l2​. The white-beam Laue method is especially useful for crystal orientation.
The crystal basis structure factor is
FG​=∑j​fj​(Q)eiG⋅rj​,IG​∝∣FG​∣2
(69)
after geometric and thermal corrections, including the Debye–Waller factor. For X-rays, fj​ describes scattering from the atomic electron density. Thus positions provide lattice geometry while relative wave intensities constrain atomic positions and species. Wave intensities do not directly give the phases of FG​: structure refinement or other constraints must address the phase problem.
Neutron diffraction uses a reactor or spallation source, wavelength selection and a detector bank or time-of-flight detection. Neutrons interact with nuclei through nuclear scattering lengths that vary between elements and isotopes rather than simply with atomic number. This makes them useful for light atoms, isotope contrast and distinguishing some neighbours in the periodic table; their magnetic interaction also probes magnetic order. Electron diffraction in a transmission electron microscope gives strong signals from very small thin crystallites, although strong multiple scattering often complicates quantitative wave intensity analysis. These techniques complement one another.
A phonon dispersion relation requires both momentum and energy transfer, so elastic diffraction alone is insufficient. In coherent inelastic neutron scattering, the measured transfers for phonon creation are
ℏQ=ℏ(ki​−kf​),ℏω=Ei​−Ef​,Q=G+q,ω=ωs​(q).​
(70)
Here q is the phonon wavevector reduced into a Brillouin zone, and s labels an acoustic phonon branch or optical phonon branch. Negative energy transfer corresponds to annihilating an already occupied phonon. For neutrons E=ℏ2k2/(2mn​), so wavelength and energy selection are linked. Typical thermal-neutron energies of a few to tens of millielectronvolts (1meV=10−3 electronvolts) are well matched to many lattice vibrations.
A triple-axis spectrometer uses a monochromator crystal to select ki​, the sample as the second scattering axis, and an analyser crystal to select kf​ before detection. Rotate these elements and the specimen to hold Q fixed while scanning Ei​−Ef​; inelastic peaks locate ωs​(q). Repeat at different q along symmetry directions to map the phonon dispersion relation. The dynamic structure factor governs peak weights, including the selection of phonon polarization by the scattering geometry. A time-of-flight spectrometer instead infers outgoing neutron energy from travel time over a known flight path and records many directions simultaneously.
Inelastic X-ray scattering can also resolve phonons when very high energy resolution is available. Optical Raman scattering and Brillouin scattering measure vibrations near the zone centre because photon momentum transfers are small compared with a typical reciprocal lattice spacing; they complement, rather than generally map the entire zone like, momentum-resolved neutron measurements. Ultrasound measurements give the long-wavelength sound speed, the initial slope dω/dq of an acoustic phonon branch.
