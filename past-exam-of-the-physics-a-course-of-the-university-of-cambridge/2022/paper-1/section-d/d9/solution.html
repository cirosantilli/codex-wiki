Time dependence in quantum mechanics describes how a prepared quantum state changes and thereby changes the probabilities of later measurements. For an isolated system, the Schrödinger picture assigns this evolution to a state vector ∣ψ(t)⟩, governed by the Time-dependent Schrödinger equation
iℏdtd​∣ψ(t)⟩=H^(t)∣ψ(t)⟩.
(61)
The Hamiltonian is a self-adjoint operator; it has units of energy, while ℏ has units of action, so H^/ℏ has units of inverse time. For a particle with potential energy V(r,t), this becomes
iℏ∂t​ψ(r,t)=[−2mℏ2​∇2+V(r,t)]ψ(r,t).
(62)
Given a suitable initial wavefunction and the physical boundary conditions, this first-order equation determines its subsequent propagation. Its linearity means that the evolution of a quantum superposition is the corresponding quantum superposition of evolved states.
For a real potential energy, this equation and its complex conjugate imply the local continuity equation
∂t​ρ+∇⋅j=0,ρ=∣ψ∣2,j=mℏ​Im(ψ∗∇ψ).
(63)
Here ρ is the probability density and j the probability current. Vanishing net boundary flux preserves wavefunction normalization, as required by the Born rule.
The time-evolution operator packages the solution for every initial state:
∣ψ(t)⟩=U(t,t0​)∣ψ(t0​)⟩,iℏ∂t​U(t,t0​)=H^(t)U(t,t0​),U(t0​,t0​)=I.
(64)
Because H^†=H^, differentiation gives
dtd​(U†U)=ℏi​U†H^U−ℏi​U†H^U=0.
(65)
Therefore U†U=I: this is unitary time evolution, preserving inner products and all state norms. It is reversible, with U(t,t0​)−1=U(t0​,t)=U(t,t0​)†, and obeys the composition law U(t2​,t0​)=U(t2​,t1​)U(t1​,t0​).
For a time-independent Hamiltonian, the solution is the operator exponential
U(t,t0​)=exp[−iH^(t−t0​)/ℏ].​
(66)
If the Hamiltonian varies with time, the analogous simple exponential of its integral is valid when [H^(t),H^(t′)]=0 for all relevant times. Otherwise time ordering is essential:
U(t,t0​)=Texp[−ℏi​∫t0​t​H^(t′)dt′].
(67)
The meaning is explicit in the first terms of the ordered series,
U=I−ℏi​∫t0​t​H^(t1​)dt1​−ℏ21​∫t0​t​dt1​∫t0​t1​​dt2​H^(t1​)H^(t2​)+⋯:
(68)
the later-time operator stands on the left. In the position representation, the same propagation is expressed by the quantum-mechanical propagator
K(r,t;r′,t0​)=⟨r∣U(t,t0​)∣r′⟩,ψ(r,t)=∫K(r,t;r′,t0​)ψ(r′,t0​)d3r′.
(69)
Its initial value is the Dirac delta function δ3(r−r′).
A time-independent Hamiltonian has energy eigenstates satisfying H^∣n⟩=En​∣n⟩. A single such initial state evolves as
∣ψn​(t)⟩=e−iEn​(t−t0​)/ℏ∣n⟩.
(70)
It is a stationary state: its global phase changes, but its probability density and the distributions of time-independent observables do not. Its energy is sharp. Any quantum superposition entirely within one degenerate energy eigenspace also acquires one common global phase and is stationary. Stationarity does not require zero probability current; for instance, un​(ϕ)=einϕ/2π​ on the ring has a constant density and persistent circulation when n=0.
An arbitrary normalized state can instead contain different energy eigenvalues:
∣ψ(t)⟩=∑n​cn​e−iEn​(t−t0​)/ℏ∣n⟩,∑n​∣cn​∣2=1.
(71)
A continuous spectrum requires corresponding integrals. For the discrete case, an energy measurement gives En​ with fixed probability ∣cn​∣2 (summed over degeneracies), but relative quantum phases change with time. Consequently
∣ψ(r,t)∣2=∑m,n​cm∗​cn​ϕm∗​(r)ϕn​(r)ei(Em​−En​)(t−t0​)/ℏ.
(72)
The interference terms can oscillate at angular frequencies (Em​−En​)/ℏ. For two populated energy levels, this supplies a characteristic period 2πℏ/∣Em​−En​∣ when the interference term is nonzero. Thus fixed energy populations do not imply a stationary spatial distribution.
For a normalized state and an observable A^(t), its expectation value is
⟨A⟩t​=⟨ψ(t)∣A^(t)∣ψ(t)⟩.
(73)
This is the statistical mean over repeated preparations and measurements. In an eigenbasis of energy eigenstates for a time-independent Hamiltonian and a time-independent A^,
⟨A⟩t​=∑m,n​cm∗​cn​Amn​ei(Em​−En​)(t−t0​)/ℏ.
(74)
Only matrix elements connecting different energies can generate these oscillations. If [A^,H^]=0, its expectation value is constant for every initial state, provided it has no explicit time dependence.
Differentiating the expectation value and substituting the Schrödinger equation for both the bra and ket proves the general Ehrenfest theorem:
dtd​⟨A⟩​=⟨ψ˙​∣A^∣ψ⟩+⟨∂t∂A^​⟩+⟨ψ∣A^∣ψ˙​⟩=ℏi​⟨H^A^−A^H^⟩+⟨∂t∂A^​⟩.​
(75)
Equivalently,
dtd​⟨A⟩=iℏ1​⟨[A^,H^]⟩+⟨∂t∂A^​⟩.​
(76)
These manipulations require the state to lie in the relevant operator domains. Taking A^=H^ gives d⟨H⟩/dt=⟨∂t​H^⟩, so energy is conserved under a time-independent Hamiltonian.
For H^=p^​2/(2m)+V(x^), the canonical commutation relation gives
[x^,H^]=miℏ​p^​,[p^​,H^]=−iℏV′(x^).
(77)
Hence the particularly useful position-and-momentum form of Ehrenfest theorem is
dtd​⟨x⟩=m⟨p⟩​,dtd​⟨p⟩=−⟨V′(x)⟩.​
(78)
It resembles Newton's second law, but the mean force generally differs from the force at the mean position. If a narrow wave packet samples a region where the force varies little, ⟨V′(x)⟩≃V′(⟨x⟩) gives approximate classical motion. For a quadratic potential energy this replacement is exact. In particular the quantum harmonic oscillator obeys
dt2d2​⟨x⟩=−ω2⟨x⟩,⟨x⟩t​=⟨x⟩t0​​cosωτ+mω⟨p⟩t0​​​sinωτ,τ=t−t0​.
(79)
The centroid follows the classical harmonic oscillator, although this does not specify the full evolving wavefunction or its width.
The Heisenberg picture expresses the same physics by assigning evolution to observables instead:
A^H​(t)=U†(t,t0​)A^S​(t)U(t,t0​),∣ψH​⟩=∣ψ(t0​)⟩.
(80)
The expectation value is unchanged, and differentiation yields dA^H​/dt=(i/ℏ)[H^H​,A^H​]+U†(∂t​A^S​)U. Thus state propagation, evolving expectation values, and the classical correspondence in Ehrenfest theorem are mutually consistent descriptions of the same unitary time evolution.
