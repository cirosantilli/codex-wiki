For uniform illumination, the aperture is h(x)=1 for ∣x∣<a/2 and zero otherwise. With q=2πsinθ/λ,
h(q)=∫−a/2a/2​e−iqxdx=aqa/2sin(qa/2)​,I(θ)=I(0)(αsinα​)2,α=λπasinθ​​.
(12)
Zeros occur at sinθ=jλ/a, j=0; the central maximum extends between ±λ/a. A monochromatic laser expanded into a collimated beam can illuminate the slit. A lens with the screen in its back focal plane produces the Fraunhofer pattern; alternatively use a sufficiently distant screen, z≫a2/λ. Align the slit and beam and avoid detector saturation.
For N equally spaced slits, write the aperture as a convolution
hN​=recta​∗∑j=0N−1​δ(x−jb).
(13)
The convolution theorem converts this into a single-slit transform times the finite-array transform. Therefore
IN​(q)∝a2sinc2(2qa​)[sin(qb/2)sin(Nqb/2)​]2​,sincu=usinu​.
(14)
Principal maxima are at bsinθ=mλ for normal incidence, inside the single-slit envelope. Their height scales as N2 times the envelope, and their first zeros are separated from a peak by δ(sinθ)=λ/(Nb).
The envelope-to-order-spacing ratio is b/a: increasing it admits more orders within the central envelope and changes the relative order strengths. In particular, an order is missing when ma/b is a nonzero integer. Statements about absolute angular spacing need a fixed dimension: at fixed a, increasing b reduces order spacing; at fixed b, decreasing a broadens the envelope.
For fixed incident angle, differentiating the grating equation gives an order displacement δ(sinθ)=∣m∣δλ/b. Equating this to the peak-to-first-zero width gives
δλ=∣m∣Nλ​≃∣m∣Wλb​​.
(15)
Here W≃Nb is the illuminated grating width; this is the ideal Rayleigh limit, before slit width or aberrations broaden the spectral image.
With b=(1600 mm−1)−1=0.000625 mm, the required count is N≥372.7/(2×0.2)=931.75. Thus 932 illuminated slits, or Wmin​≃0.5825 mm, suffice ideally. Checking diffraction-order feasibility, normal incidence cannot produce this second order because 2λ/b=1.193>1; an oblique spectrograph geometry is required. A feasible reflection-Littrow example has sinθ=mλ/(2b)=0.5963, or θ≃36.6∘.

For time dependence e−iωt, a scalar Fresnel-Kirchhoff aperture formula for a point-source amplitude A is
U(P)=iλA​∫A​K(Q)s(Q)r(Q)eik[s(Q)+r(Q)]​dSQ​,k=λ2π​.
(16)
Here s=∣S−Q∣, r=∣P−Q∣, and K is the obliquity factor, approximately the mean of the incident and outgoing direction cosines measured with the forward aperture normal. The factors describe incident spherical spreading, outgoing spreading and accumulated phase. This is the scalar Kirchhoff aperture approximation; it does not supply an exact electromagnetic edge boundary solution.
In the Fresnel regime, expand path lengths to quadratic order in aperture coordinates while replacing slowly varying amplitude and obliquity factors by their axial values. In the Fraunhofer regime the quadratic aperture phase is negligible, leaving the aperture Fourier transform. For characteristic aperture size D, the latter requires D2/(λR)≪1 in the relevant finite-source geometry; a lens can supply the Fourier-plane configuration.
For the one-dimensional Cornu construction, put S,O,P on the unobstructed straight ray, with the aperture plane perpendicular at O. An element is displaced transversely by x, with ∣x∣≪a,b, where a=SO and b=OP. A long straight edge or slit makes the other transverse direction separable. Then
a2+x2​+b2+x2​=a+b+2x2​(a1​+b1​)+O(x4),R1​=a1​+b1​​.
(17)
For knife-edge diffraction at the fence, the edge is h=0.5 m above the direct ray, with a=5 m, b=45 m, λ≃0.100 m, and R=4.5 m. The unblocked aperture extends upward from h, so its Cornu chord runs from uh​=h2/(λR)​=1.054 to infinity. Using C(∞)=S(∞)=1/2 and normalizing by the full unobstructed chord gives
T=Ifree​Ifence​​=2[1/2−C(uh​)]2+[1/2−S(uh​)]2​≃0.038​.
(18)
The approximate chord coordinates are C=0.775, S=0.492. This is the standard single-knife-edge Fresnel normalization.[ref]
For geometric spreading in a moving-receiver diffraction measurement, set b=d while retaining a=5 and h=0.5:
u(d)=hλ2​(51​+d1​)​,Ireceived​(d)∝(5+d)2T[u(d)]​.
(19)
Normalized transmission decreases toward the fence. Absolute received intensity initially increases, because the free-space spherical-wave intensity rises as source–receiver distance decreases. In this model it reaches a maximum near d≃2.3 m, then falls. The formal paraxial small-d behavior is I∝d/(5+d)3; extrapolation to d≲h is outside the small-angle approximation. The sketch distinguishes the absolute signal from its ratio to the unobstructed signal at each position.

C10 OurBigBook.com
The Pauli exclusion principle confines thermally excited conduction electrons to an energy interval of order kB​T around the Fermi surface. Their excess energy is therefore proportional to T2, giving an electronic heat capacity γT. More precisely, γ=(π2/3)kB2​gmol​(ϵF​). In a three-dimensional Debye model, acoustic phonons have density of states proportional to ω2; their thermal energy scales as T4, giving
C=γT+βT3,β=5θD3​12π4R​​
(20)
per mole of atoms. This assumes a normal metal without a lower-temperature superconducting or magnetic anomaly.
For θD​≪T≪TF​, the lattice approaches the Dulong-Petit value, so CV​≃3R+γT, usually dominated by 3R. Each classical vibrational mode contributes kB​ through kinetic and potential equipartition. This is a high-temperature solid-state regime, not a claim that the solid persists to arbitrarily high temperature; anharmonicity and CP​−CV​ can matter.
The body-centred cubic cell has two atoms, so nat​=2/a3. Counting three acoustic branches gives kD3​=6π2nat​. By the Debye-temperature definition,
ωD​=ℏkB​θD​​=1.18×1013 s−1,vD​=ℏ(12π2/a3)1/3kB​θD​​≃1.25×103 ms−1​.
(21)
This is an effective Debye sound speed; separate longitudinal and transverse speeds are not determined. For isotropic branches, 3/vD3​=1/vL3​+2/vT3​.

The first Brillouin zone is ∣kx​∣,∣ky​∣≤π/a. For one atom per primitive cell and two spin states, a monovalent free-electron gas has
n2D​=a21​=2πkF2​​,kF​a=2π​​.
(22)
Its circular Fermi surface lies inside the zone and encloses half its area: a partially filled band is conducting. Here odd filling obstructs a spin-degenerate band insulator: a monovalent band insulator is impossible in this spin-degenerate one-atom independent-electron model. It would require changed premises, such as unit-cell enlargement or physics beyond the free-electron picture; no insulating free-electron contour should be invented.

For divalent atoms, kF​a=4π​. The circle has the same area as the first Brillouin zone but crosses its faces and leaves its corners unfilled. Thus the free-electron case remains conducting: filling capacity alone does not remove band overlap. A nearly free-electron conductor has reconstructed electron and hole pockets; a divalent band insulator instead has one completely filled band separated from all higher bands, with no Fermi surface in the gap.

For a plane sound wave, acoustic impedance is Z=p/u=ρc, with pressure amplitude p and particle-velocity amplitude u. Continuity of pressure and normal velocity at normal incidence on a lossless interface gives
rp​=Z2​+Z1​Z2​−Z1​​,R=rp2​,T=(Z1​+Z2​)24Z1​Z2​​​.
(26)
Matching Z1​=Z2​ eliminates reflection. A quarter-wave intermediate layer with Zm​=Z1​Z2​​ transforms the load to the source impedance at its design frequency; its bandwidth is limited. For strings, the analogous mechanical impedance is transverse force divided by velocity, Z=Tv−1=Tμ​; continuity of displacement and transverse force gives the corresponding matching condition. These amplitudes use different force/velocity conventions from acoustic pressure, so reflection signs must be stated consistently.

A Michelson interferometer divides light at a beam splitter, sends it to two mirrors, and recombines the returned beams. A compensating plate can equalize glass traversal. For approximately axial rays the optical path difference is Δ=2(n1​L1​−n2​L2​), plus fixed instrument phase, and
I=I1​+I2​+2I1​I2​​cos(2πΔ/λ0​+ϕ0​)
(28)
for coherent, mode-matched fields. Moving one mirror by δL in air changes the round-trip path by 2δL, so one fringe corresponds to λ0​/2 mirror motion. It measures displacement, wavelength and refractive-index changes; scanning the delay enables Fourier-transform spectroscopy. Mirror alignment, polarization overlap, vibration and source coherence control visibility. A finite coherence length limits useful arm mismatch.

D13 OurBigBook.com
Donor doping supplies conduction electrons to n-type semiconductor; acceptor doping supplies holes to p-type semiconductor. In the nondegenerate, fully ionized limit, majority densities are approximately nn​=ND​ and pp​=NA​, while minority densities follow np=ni2​. On contact, majority carriers diffuse across the junction, exposing positive donor ions on the n side and negative acceptor ions on the p side. The depletion-region electric field points from n to p and opposes further diffusion. At thermal equilibrium the Fermi level is uniform and drift balances diffusion, with no net current.
For an abrupt junction with uniform doping and permittivity εs​, charge neutrality gives ND​xn​=NA​xp​. Poisson's equation then gives
Vbi​=qkB​T​logni2​NA​ND​​,W=xn​+xp​=q2εs​​(NA​1​+ND​1​)(Vbi​−V)​​.
(29)
Positive applied V means forward bias: it lowers the barrier and narrows the depletion region. Most depletion width lies in the more lightly doped side. The formula assumes the depletion approximation and fails once its operating assumptions break down.
forward bias increases minority densities at the depletion edges by the factor eqV/kB​T. In the neutral material, the steady diffusion–recombination equation gives exponentially decaying excess minorities, with lengths Ln​=Dn​τn​​ and Lp​=Dp​τp​​. Taking the carrier gradients at the junction yields
I=IS​(eqV/kB​T−1),IS​=qAj​ni2​(Ln​NA​Dn​​+Lp​ND​Dp​​)​.
(30)
This ideal diode law assumes low injection, nondegenerate statistics, negligible depletion-region recombination and series resistance, and sufficiently long neutral regions. Its exponential comes from the bias-dependent injection barrier; there is no universal abrupt “turn-on” voltage. reverse bias suppresses injection, giving an approximate −IS​ current until additional leakage or breakdown matters.[ref]
Rectification uses the asymmetric current-voltage characteristic; controlled reverse breakdown enables voltage references, with current limiting. Bias-dependent depletion capacitance enables varactors. photodiodes and solar cells collect optically generated carriers through the junction field and diffusion toward it; efficient light-emitting diodes use radiative electron–hole recombination, preferably in a direct-gap material. Series resistance, high injection, trap-assisted recombination, heating and finite carrier storage explain departures from the ideal steady-state law.
