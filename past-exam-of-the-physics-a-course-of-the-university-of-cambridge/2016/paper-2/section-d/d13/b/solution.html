In an electrical insulator, lattice vibrations usually carry most heat. A small temperature gradient produces an imbalance between phonons moving in opposite directions. In an isotropic kinetic approximation,
κph​≃31​cV​vs​ℓtr​=31​cV​vs2​τtr​,​
(62)
where cV​ is heat capacity per unit volume, vs​ an appropriate acoustic speed and ℓtr​ the transport mean free path. The factor one third is the angular average of the squared direction cosine. With several branches and frequency-dependent scattering, replace this estimate by the corresponding weighted mode integral. A short lifetime matters for conductivity only insofar as it relaxes heat current.
For a clean three-dimensional crystal at T≪ΘD​, the Debye heat capacity varies as T3. If diffuse boundary scattering fixes ℓtr​ near a specimen dimension, then κph​∝T3. The size dependence is a test of that boundary-limited regime. Specular surfaces, defects and small specimens can change the simple law; at ballistic scales a local conductivity description itself needs care.
As temperature rises, more phonons are excited, but phonon-phonon scattering shortens their transport path. Normal processes conserve total crystal momentum, whereas an Umklapp process satisfies q1​+q2​−q3​=G=0 with reciprocal vector G and can relax directed heat flow. Low-temperature Umklapp scattering is strongly suppressed; its increasing importance often produces a conductivity maximum followed by a decline. Isotope and impurity scattering lower or broaden that maximum.
At sufficiently high temperature, cV​ approaches its classical constant while a typical anharmonic Umklapp rate grows approximately as T. Under that further approximation,
ℓtr​∝T−1,κph​∝T−1.​
(63)
Thus a clean crystalline insulator commonly shows a low-temperature rise, a peak, then a high-temperature fall. This is not a universal curve for every insulator: glasses, strong disorder, multiple scattering mechanisms, radiative heat transfer and structural transitions can give different behavior.
A steady-state bar experiment supplies a concrete test. Inject known heating power, measure the small temperature difference between two separated sensors and infer κ=PL/(AΔT) after correcting for heat leaks. Keep the gradient small enough that a single representative temperature is meaningful, calibrate sensor contact effects and subtract radiative/background losses. Repeat with different widths or isotope/defect content to distinguish boundary limitation from intrinsic scattering. A power-dependent result may reflect a changing temperature profile rather than a new microscopic law.
