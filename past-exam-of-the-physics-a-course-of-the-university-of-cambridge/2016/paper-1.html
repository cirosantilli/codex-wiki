C10 OurBigBook.com
identical particles have the same intrinsic properties, including mass, charge and spin. Their labels are bookkeeping devices rather than measurable identities. When wave functions overlap, quantum mechanics supplies no observable trajectory that permanently identifies “particle 1” independently of “particle 2”. A many-particle description must therefore treat exchange consistently. The Hamiltonian and physical observables are invariant under relabelling identical particles.
For two particles, the exchange operator obeys P^122​=I^. The usual three-dimensional symmetrization postulate selects states symmetric under exchange for bosons and antisymmetric for fermions. In relativistic quantum theory, the Spin-statistics theorem associates integer spin with bosons and half-integer spin with fermions under its locality and positivity assumptions; the connection is not derived merely from P^122​=I^ in elementary nonrelativistic mechanics. For orthonormal one-particle states u and v, the two-particle spatial combinations are
Ψ±​(1,2)=2​u(1)v(2)±v(1)u(2)​.
(27)
The sign concerns the complete state, including spin and other internal variables, not necessarily the spatial factor alone.
The antisymmetric state vanishes if both fermions occupy the same one-particle state: this is the Pauli exclusion principle. For N fermions in orthonormal spin orbitals χj​, the normalized Slater determinant Ψ=(N!)−1/2det[χj​(i)] supplies the required exchange sign. Bosonic occupation has no corresponding upper limit, and symmetrized products describe many bosons. Fermionic occupation is zero or one per complete one-particle state; two electrons can still occupy the same spatial atomic orbital with opposite spin.
Spin and spatial symmetry are linked. A two-electron spin singlet state is antisymmetric, so its spatial factor must be symmetric. A symmetric triplet state instead requires an antisymmetric spatial factor. Exchange then changes pair correlations and the energies available when interactions depend on particle separation, even without introducing a new classical “exchange force”. It contributes to atomic shell structure, bonding and magnetic behaviour.
In an occupation-number description, bosonic creation and annihilation operators satisfy [a^i​,a^j†​]=δij​, whereas fermionic operators satisfy {c^i​,c^j†​}=δij​ and anticommute among themselves. The latter imply (c^i†​)2=0. This formalism implements the same exchange constraints without assigning permanent particle labels.
For noninteracting particles in thermal equilibrium, the mean occupation of a state of energy ϵ is
nˉB​=e(ϵ−μ)/(kB​T)−11​,nˉF​=e(ϵ−μ)/(kB​T)+11​.
(28)
Fermi-Dirac statistics produces a filled Fermi sea at zero temperature and degeneracy pressure; Bose-Einstein statistics permits macroscopic Ground state occupation when the density of available states and thermodynamic conditions allow condensation. The different statistics therefore have measurable collective consequences, not just different notation.[ref]
When phase-space occupation is dilute, both distributions reduce to the Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics limit. Likewise, well-separated localized wave packets have negligible overlap and small exchange corrections, so effectively labelled classical trajectories can become a useful approximation. In two spatial dimensions more general exchange statistics are possible; the standard boson/fermion account here concerns ordinary three-dimensional particles and their total states.

For the nonrelativistic Coulomb Hamiltonian with reduced electron-proton mass μ, separation in Spherical coordinate system gives ψnℓm​(r,θ,ϕ)=Rnℓ​(r)Yℓm​(θ,ϕ). The quantum numbers obey n≥1, 0≤ℓ≤n−1 and −ℓ≤m≤ℓ. These are joint eigenstates of energy, L2 and Lz​, with the latter two eigenvalues ℓ(ℓ+1)ℏ2 and mℏ.
The Coulomb bound-state energies and characteristic length are
En​=−2(4πϵ0​)2ℏ2n2μe4​,aμ​=μe24πϵ0​ℏ2​.​
(29)
For example, ψ100​=(πaμ3​)−1/2e−r/aμ​ is spherically symmetric and has no radial node. In general the radial part contains an exponential times a Generalized Laguerre polynomial, with n−ℓ−1 radial nodes. The spherical harmonic specifies angular structure and azimuthal phase; real linear combinations of degenerate m states give familiar directional orbitals.
The leading Coulomb energy depends on n alone, giving n2 spatial states for each principal shell, or 2n2 including electron spin. Fine structure, Lamb shift and Hyperfine structure lift parts of this ideal degeneracy. An atomic orbital is a probability amplitude, not a classical planetary path.[ref]

b OurBigBook.com
Quantum tunnelling is supported by transport and decay through barriers that classical particles of the same energy cannot traverse. Three complementary examples are:
  • In alpha decay, an alpha cluster escapes the attractive nuclear region through a Coulomb barrier. The barrier penetration factor depends exponentially on a forbidden-region action, helping explain the strong dependence of lifetimes on emitted alpha energy. This is evidence from systematic decay rates, rather than direct imaging of an alpha inside the barrier.
  • A scanning tunnelling microscope measures current across a narrow vacuum gap between a conducting tip and sample. At small bias an approximate dependence is I∝e−2κs with κ≃2mΦ​/ℏ. The strong gap-distance sensitivity enables atomic-scale imaging and local spectroscopy. Electronic density of states and barrier height also affect the current, so constant-current contrast is not an unqualified geometric atom map.[ref]
  • A heavily doped Tunnel diode has a narrow p-n barrier. Bias changes the overlap of occupied and empty states across it; interband tunnelling produces a characteristic peak and Negative differential resistance interval in its current-voltage curve. The measured voltage dependence agrees with the changing band overlap, providing a solid-state transport example.[ref]
Together these test barrier penetration in nuclear decay, vacuum-gap transport and semiconductor transport. Their detailed currents depend on state supply and matching as well as the barrier transmission probability.

The ideal voltage-feedback Operational amplifier has zero input currents and, while stable negative feedback holds it in its linear operating range, equal input voltages: i+​=i−​=0 and v+​=v−​. The first rule follows from infinite input impedance; the second is the large-open-loop-gain limit of vo​=A(v+​−v−​). Equal input electric potential is a virtual short, not an electrically conducting connection.
For an inverting amplifier with the noninverting input grounded, v−​=0 and node-current balance gives vi​/Ri​=−vo​/Rf​, hence vo​=−Rf​vi​/Ri​. For a noninverting amplifier, the feedback divider gives v−​=vo​Rg​/(Rf​+Rg​)=vi​, hence vo​=(1+Rf​/Rg​)vi​.
One should check output swing, input common-mode range, slew rate, Gain-bandwidth product and stability before using these limits in a real circuit. Saturation, positive feedback or operation beyond bandwidth invalidates the equal-voltage rule; finite input bias currents and offset voltage can also matter.[ref]

Thermal agitation in a resistor produces Johnson-Nyquist noise, even without an applied mean current. In the classical low-frequency regime, its one-sided open-circuit voltage spectrum is SV​=4kB​TR and its equivalent short-circuit current spectrum is SI​=4kB​T/R. In an Effective noise bandwidth B,
vrms​=4kB​TRB​,irms​=4kB​TB/R​.​
(33)
For a matched load, available thermal noise power is kB​TB. A resistor and load at the same temperature exchange noise power without a net heat flow.
Cooling reduces the rms amplitude as T​ if Electrical resistance and bandwidth remain fixed. A cryogenic bath or refrigerator, thermal anchoring and controlled heat input can maintain a steady low temperature. Radiation shields, an insulating vacuum, thermally anchored leads and limited measurement dissipation reduce heat leaks and self-heating. Temperature sensing and stable feedback control reduce slow temperature-induced drift; keeping temperature constant does not remove microscopic thermal fluctuations.
Bandwidth restriction, suitable impedance and a low-noise amplifier complement cooling. Warm leads, contacts and the amplifier can otherwise dominate the observed noise. At fixed mean current, shot noise is not generally reduced merely by cooling, and low-frequency excess noise need not follow the Johnson law. At frequencies with hf≳kB​T, quantum noise expressions replace the classical approximation.[ref]

Uniform signal sampling at rate fs​ multiplies a continuous signal by a time-domain Dirac comb, giving frequency-domain replicas spaced by fs​:
Xs​(f)=fs​∑k=−∞∞​X(f−kfs​).
(34)
If a baseband signal is restricted to ∣f∣≤B and fs​>2B, these replicas can be separated by an ideal reconstruction filter. Frequencies differing by integer multiples of fs​ have the same sampled phase progression; for real signals they fold into the interval from zero to fs​/2. This ambiguity is aliasing.
For example, 0.8fs​ and 0.2fs​ cosines produce identical sample values. Apply an analogue Anti-aliasing filter before digitization and choose enough sample-rate margin for its finite transition band. A digital filter applied afterward cannot distinguish components already folded onto one another. Out-of-band noise also aliases unless filtered. The sample rate sets temporal/spectral coverage; quantization resolution sets amplitude precision and is a separate limitation. Bandpass Signal sampling has additional conditions beyond the simple baseband rule.[ref]
