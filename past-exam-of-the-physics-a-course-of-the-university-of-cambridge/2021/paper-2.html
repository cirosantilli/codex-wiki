For the deep-water gravity wave, the phase velocity and group velocity follow from its dispersion relation:
vp​=kω​=kg​​,vg​=dkdω​=21​kg​​=2vp​​.
(1)

(2)

A bubble is a thin water layer surrounded by air. At a locally flat patch, let its thickness be d, its refractive index be n, and the internal angle of refraction be θt​. The two principal reflected light contributions are the reflection at the first surface and the contribution that enters the layer, reflects at its second surface and leaves again. The first reflection, from lower to higher refractive index, introduces a π phase shift; the second does not. Their optical path difference is 2ndcosθt​, so their phase difference is, modulo 2π,
Δϕ=λ0​4πndcosθt​​+π,
(3)
where λ0​ is the vacuum wavelength. Constructive interference in reflected light therefore occurs approximately when
2ndcosθt​=(m+21​)λ0​,m=0,1,2,….​
(4)
Destructive interference occurs at 2ndcosθt​=mλ0​. In the weak-reflection approximation, the reflected light intensity at each wavelength is proportional to sin2(2πndcosθt​/λ0​). This is a smooth spectral filter, with broad maxima and minima: it does not select three pure wavelengths corresponding to display primaries. Ordinary illumination contains many visible wavelengths, and the eye combines their remaining contributions through color vision. The result is a mixture of colours, often pastel or desaturated.
The layer thickness varies over the bubble because of drainage and flow; the bubble's curvature also changes the local angle of refraction and the viewed optical path difference. The spectral filter therefore changes continuously across the surface and in time. Very thin regions become dark because the two reflected contributions nearly cancel across the visible optical spectrum. Thin-film interference explains both the changing colours and this dark limit.
The ray sketch shows a local patch and is not to scale. The plotted curves use a chosen constant n=1.33 at normal incidence and illustrative thicknesses; they show the spectral filter rather than calibrated perceived colours.

Model the room's decaying acoustic waves as a weakly damped resonator. The quality factor from ringdown is related to stored energy E by
Q=ω−dE/dtE​,E(t)=E(0)e−ωt/Q,A(t)=A(0)e−ωt/(2Q),
(5)
where A is the pressure wave amplitude and ω=2πf. The measured frequency f=440Hz gives ω≃2.76×103s−1. If the stated 5s is interpreted as an approximate wave amplitude exponential relaxation time,
Q≃2ωτA​​=π(440Hz)(5s)≃6.9×103,​
(6)
an estimate of order 104. Taking 5s as an energy exponential relaxation time instead gives Q≃1.4×104.
There is a real convention ambiguity: “heard to die away” gives neither an initial sound level nor an audibility threshold, so it does not determine either exponential relaxation time. More generally, if the energy falls by a factor E(0)/E(td​) in td​,
Q=ln[E(0)/E(td​)]2πftd​​.

Let N=L/a be the integer number of primitive unit cells. A Bloch state can be written as ψk​(x)=eikxuk​(x), with uk​(x+a)=uk​(x). The periodic boundary condition ψk​(x+L)=ψk​(x) therefore requires
eikL=1,kj​=L2πj​,j∈Z.
(10)
The spacing of allowed wavevectors is Δk=2π/L. The first Brillouin zone has width 2π/a, so
Nk​=2π/L2π/a​=aL​=N.​
(11)
For an exact endpoint convention take a half-open Brillouin zone, such as [−π/a,π/a). Its two endpoints differ by a reciprocal lattice vector and must not both be counted as distinct values of crystal momentum. There are exactly N inequivalent integers j modulo N, irrespective of whether N is even or odd. This is state counting in a Bloch band: one allowed wavevector per primitive unit cell per energy band. A separate twofold spin multiplicity gives 2N electronic states per energy band, without doubling the number of wavevectors.

The expression is a scalar diffraction theory integral for a monochromatic wave. The complex quantity ψP​ represents a field complex amplitude, with physical field obtained by taking the real part after multiplication by e−iωt. The light intensity is proportional to ∣ψP​∣2. The coordinates x,y label transverse positions in the aperture plane, Σ is its transmitting region, and h(x,y) is the incident field multiplied by the aperture's complex transmission. The wavenumber is k=2π/λ in the propagation medium, and R is the axial propagation distance if h already includes the incident field.
To derive the Fresnel diffraction, start from Huygens-Fresnel principle contributions with propagation factor eiks/s. With the coordinate origin at the projection of P into the aperture plane,
s=R2+x2+y2​=R+2Rx2+y2​−8R3(x2+y2)2​+⋯.
(29)
The paraxial approximation requires ρ/R≪1, where ρ2=x2+y2. Replace the slowly varying 1/s and the obliquity factor by their axial values, but retain the quadratic variation of wave phase. Neglect of the next wave phase term requires kρ4/(8R3)≪1 over the relevant aperture. Thus, apart from a common constant,
ψP​≃iλReikR​∬Σ​h(x,y)eik(x2+y2)/(2R)dxdy.
(30)
The quadratic wave phase may be of order one or much larger; setting it to zero would require the stronger conditions for Fraunhofer diffraction. The scalar treatment also neglects the detailed vector field at aperture edges and assumes dimensions large enough compared with wavelength for this approximation to be useful.
For a finite-distance axial point source at distance p and an observer at distance q, the incident field contributes its own quadratic wave phase. If h instead denotes only the aperture transmission, the combined curvature is described by an effective distance

(31)
These are two equivalent conventions for distributing the incident wave phase between h and the exponential. Both distances must be included in the later calculation.

The crystal has periodic potential V(x+a)=V(x). By Bloch's theorem, an energy eigenstate may be chosen as
ψk​(x)=eikxuk​(x),uk​(x+a)=uk​(x).
(42)
Expand the periodic factor in a Fourier series:
uk​(x)=∑n∈Z​Ck,n​einG1​x,G1​=a2π​.
(43)
Multiplication by eikx gives the stated plane-wave expansion. Here x is position, a is the primitive unit cell length, G1​ is the primitive reciprocal lattice wavenumber, n runs over every integer, and Ck,n​ are complex numbers specifying the component wave amplitudes. The wavevector k labels the Bloch state modulo G1​; ℏk is its crystal momentum, rather than generally its measured free-space momentum.
The component ei(k+nG1​)x is a plane wave of wavevector k+nG1​. All these components acquire the same phase factor eika under x↦x+a, since einG1​a=1. Their mixing therefore respects the crystal's translation symmetry. The coefficients and energy eigenvalue follow from the Schrödinger equation; the expression is not a claim that the coefficients are equal or that only finitely many are nonzero. The energy band index is suppressed in the notation, so different energy bands at a common k generally have different coefficient sets.

In a metal, a partially filled energy band or overlapping energy bands supplies electrons with nearby unoccupied states. At thermal equilibrium, oppositely directed group velocities are equally populated and the net electric current vanishes. An applied electric field distorts the occupation distribution, producing a small mean drift velocity. A filled energy band alone has no such low-field intraband response because its velocity contributions cancel over the entire Brillouin zone.
In the Drude model, let n be the mobile electron number density, let e>0 be the elementary charge, and let τ be the momentum relaxation time. The mean motion obeys
mdtdvd​​=−eE−τmvd​​.
(55)
The steady state is vd​=−eτE/m. The current density is opposite to the electron drift velocity:
J=−nevd​=σE,σ=mne2τ​,ρel​=ne2τm​.​
(56)
This gives microscopic Ohm's law, with electrical conductivity σ in Sm−1 and electrical resistivity ρel​ in Ωm. For a uniform wire of length ℓ and cross-sectional area A, its electrical resistance is R=ρel​ℓ/A. The dissipated power per volume is J⋅E=σE2, the Joule heating supplied by the external source.
The free electron model with Fermi-Dirac statistics improves the microscopic picture. The Pauli exclusion principle fills states up to the Fermi energy at low temperature. For a spherical, parabolic energy band with two spin states,
kF​=(3π2n)1/3,EF​=2mℏ2kF2​​,vF​=mℏkF​​.
(57)
Only states near the Fermi surface change their occupation in a weak-field response; the large equilibrium Fermi velocity is not the small drift velocity. The same ne2τ/m formula results for this isotropic parabolic model when τ is evaluated for transport near the Fermi surface. In a crystal, the energy band group velocity, effective mass and possibly direction-dependent scattering replace the simplest bare-particle picture. The mean free path is approximately ℓmfp​=vF​τ.
A perfectly periodic static lattice is already included in Bloch states and does not by itself give a finite electrical resistivity. Momentum relaxation comes from defects, impurities, boundaries, and electron-phonon scattering. Independent scattering mechanisms approximately add their rates, giving Matthiessen's rule,
τ1​≃τimp​1​+τph​1​+⋯,ρel​(T)≃ρ0​+ρph​(T).
(58)
The approximately temperature-independent ρ0​ is the residual resistivity. In ordinary simple metals, the phonon contribution is approximately proportional to temperature at high temperature; a three-dimensional acoustic-phonon transport model gives a T5 contribution well below the relevant Debye temperature. Other mechanisms can dominate in particular materials or regimes, so neither power law is universal.
The classical Drude model incorrectly treats the equilibrium carrier statistics. Fermi-Dirac statistics explains the small electronic heat capacity, while electrons carrying both charge and heat give the approximate Wiedemann-Franz law κe​/(σT)=π2kB2​/(3e2) for degenerate carriers with appropriate nearly elastic scattering. Both the simple electrical conductivity formula and this thermal relation depend on the assumptions about energy bands and scattering, rather than being exact for all metals.

The Debye model approximates a three-dimensional crystal as an elastic continuum supporting quantized normal modes, or phonons. At long wavelengths, the acoustic phonon branches have approximately linear dispersion relations ωs​(k)=vs​k: one longitudinal branch and two transverse branches. Replace their complicated actual spectra by these linear forms, then introduce a cutoff chosen to preserve the total 3N vibrational degrees of freedom for N atoms. This is an approximation to the spectrum, not a claim that a real crystal has no non-acoustic branches.
For periodic boundary conditions in volume V, each allowed wavevector occupies (2π)3/V in the space of wavevectors. Counting a spherical shell for each branch gives the density of states per unit angular frequency
g(ω)=2π2Vω2​(vl3​1​+vt3​2​).
(59)
An effective speed of sound vD​ can be defined by 3/vD3​=1/vl3​+2/vt3​. In the single-speed Debye model, choose ωD​=vD​kD​ so that
∫0ωD​​g(ω)dω=3N.
(60)
Thus the Debye wavevector, Debye frequency and Debye temperature satisfy
kD​=(V6π2N​)1/3,ωD​=vD​kD​,ΘD​=kB​ℏωD​​,g(ω)=ωD3​9Nω2​(0<ω<ωD​).​
(61)
Here g has units of seconds when angular frequency is measured in s−1; its integral is the dimensionless number of normal modes. The spherical cutoff replaces the real Brillouin zone by an equal-count continuum construction.
Each phonon mode is a quantum harmonic oscillator. Since phonon number is not conserved at thermal equilibrium, the chemical potential is zero and its Bose-Einstein distribution gives mean thermal energy ℏω/[eℏω/(kB​T)−1]. The zero-point energy is independent of temperature in the harmonic, fixed-volume approximation and does not contribute to the heat capacity at constant volume. Excluding that constant term,

U≃53π4​NkB​ΘD3​T4​,CV​≃512π4​NkB​(ΘD​T​)3.​

This T3 law follows from the three-dimensional ω2 density of states and the availability only of low-frequency modes at low temperature. It is a central success of the Debye model.
For T≫ΘD​, the Bose-Einstein distribution reduces to the classical equipartition theorem: every mode contributes thermal energy kB​T. Consequently

the Dulong-Petit law, or 3R per mole of atoms. The simple model omits detailed phonon dispersion relation, distinct optical phonon branches, and anharmonic effects such as thermal expansion. In a metal, the low-temperature total heat capacity additionally contains a term approximately proportional to T from electrons, so the lattice T3 term should not be mistaken for the whole measurement.
