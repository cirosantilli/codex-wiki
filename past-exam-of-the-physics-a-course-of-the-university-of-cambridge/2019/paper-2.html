A Michelson interferometer splits a collimated beam at a partially reflecting beam splitter. Two mutually perpendicular arms reflect from mirrors and recombine at the same beam splitter; the superposed returning beams produce interference fringes at the detector. Translate one mirror along its arm while holding the other fixed.
If that mirror moves a measured distance Δx, the round-trip optical path difference changes by 2Δx in air, approximated as vacuum. Every change of one wavelength moves one complete bright-dark-bright fringe cycle past a fixed detector point. Count N such cycles to obtain
Δϕ=λ2π​(2Δx)=2πN,λ=N2Δx​.​
(6)
Use a spectral line selected with a suitable optical filter, and count many interference fringes to reduce the fractional uncertainty of the distance and count. Keep the two arms within the source's optical coherence length and maintain overlap and alignment. If the vacuum wavelength is required for an arm filled with air of known refractive index n, the corrected relation is λ0​=2nΔx/N at stable n.

pp​≃NA​,np​≃ni2​/NA​,nn​≃ND​,pn​≃ni2​/ND​,

A high-finesse Fabry–Pérot etalon obtains very narrow transmission peaks by summing many coherent round trips, allowing high resolving power with a compact optical path. It can transmit a substantial fraction of resonant light when coating absorption is small, and works well for precise relative line separations and narrow-line measurements. A diffraction grating may require many illuminated grooves to achieve the same resolving power.
The etalon's free spectral range is limited, and repeated orders cause ambiguity across a broad optical spectrum. It commonly needs scanning and an order-selecting optical filter or another dispersive element. Mirror alignment, flatness and illumination angle strongly affect peak positions and widths, and absorption lowers throughput. A diffraction grating instead separates a broad range of wavelengths spatially for simultaneous detection, although grating orders can also overlap. The preferred instrument depends on whether narrow-band precision or broad spectral coverage is the priority.

In Fresnel diffraction, the propagation wave phase varies quadratically across the aperture and that curvature must be retained. For a transverse scale w at propagation distance L, its size is of order πw2/(λL). In Fraunhofer diffraction, that quadratic term is negligible, approximately w2/(λL)≪1, while the linear phase encoding the observation angle remains. A lens can also form the far-field angular pattern in its focal plane.
For a normally incident plane wave and aperture transmission h(x,y), the far-field complex amplitude is its Fourier transform:
ψ(qx​,qy​)∝∬h(x,y)e−i(qx​x+qy​y)dxdy,qx​=λ2π​sinθx​.
(30)
The detected light intensity is proportional to ∣ψ∣2. For identical parallel slits, write the transmission as a sum of shifted copies of one slit. The Fourier shift theorem gives one single-slit transform multiplied by the sum of positional phase factors:
ψ(q)∝asinc(qa/2)∑j​e−iqxj​,sincu=usinu​.
(31)
The single-slit diffraction envelope sets the broad scale, while coherent interference between slit centres produces the finer array pattern. One sums complex amplitudes before squaring, rather than adding independent slit intensities.

a OurBigBook.com
A waveguide confines the transverse structure of a wave while permitting propagation along a chosen route. It can deliver energy to a distant receiver without the same geometric spreading as an unguided beam, protect a signal from unwanted coupling, and select transverse normal modes. It also makes propagation paths and coupling to devices more controllable. Confinement does not eliminate wave absorption or scattering loss, and it can introduce wave dispersion and cutoff frequencies of a membrane waveguide or corresponding cutoffs for other wave types.
Two examples are an optical fiber, which guides light by its core-and-cladding refractive index structure, and a hollow electromagnetic waveguide used to carry microwaves between a source and an antenna. A duct for acoustic waves is a further example. Each uses different material and boundary conditions, but the common idea is controlled transverse confinement.

A Bravais lattice is the set of positions generated by integer combinations of independent primitive translation vectors, R=n1​a1​+n2​a2​+n3​a3​. Every lattice point has the same translational environment. A crystal may additionally have a basis of several atoms attached to each lattice point; those atoms are not automatically separate points of its Bravais lattice.
A unit cell is a region whose translated copies fill the crystal without gaps or overlaps. It can be chosen for convenient symmetry, and a conventional cell may contain several lattice points. A primitive unit cell is a smallest such repeating cell, containing exactly one lattice point when boundary sharing is counted. Its shape is not unique, but its volume Ω=∣a1​⋅(a2​×a3​)∣ is fixed for a given lattice. For a monatomic Bravais crystal, each primitive cell contains one atom.

Use temperature-dependent X-ray diffraction, with the crystal in a controlled heating stage and a detector measuring diffracted directions. The quantum free electron model treats conduction electrons as independent particles moving in an approximately constant potential, with parabolic dispersion relation E=ℏ2k2/(2m). Fermi-Dirac statistics successfully explains important features such as a Fermi energy and a small electronic heat capacity, but a constant potential gives no band gaps and cannot by itself explain why different band fillings produce metals, semiconductors and band insulators. It also misses lattice-dependent effective masses, detailed directional responses and the topology of real Fermi surfaces. Scattering and finite electrical resistivity require additional physics.
The nearly-free electron model introduces a weak periodic lattice potential. Bloch's theorem replaces individual free plane waves by Bloch states, and Fourier components of the potential mix plane waves whose wavevectors differ by a reciprocal lattice vector. Near degeneracy at a Brillouin zone boundary, this coupling opens a band gap; for a two-wave crossing its size is 2∣VG​∣. Away from the crossing, energies remain close to free-particle values. Band curvature produces band effective masses, and band filling determines whether the Fermi energy lies inside an allowed band or a gap.
The improvement is the inclusion of lattice periodicity, rather than a switch from classical to quantum statistics: the quantum free electron model already uses Fermi-Dirac statistics. The strong delta barriers in the later numerical example are treated exactly by the Kronig-Penney model, rather than by assuming that their strength is perturbatively small.

A wave propagates an organized disturbance through space. What oscillates depends on the medium: displacement in a string or solid, pressure and density in a fluid, electric fields and magnetic fields in an electromagnetic wave, or a quantum wavefunction. The propagation law follows from the restoring mechanism, inertia and relevant boundary conditions. A common linear equation is utt​=c2∇2u, whose plane waves obey ω=c∣k∣. The resulting phase velocity and group velocity coincide, so a narrow wave packet can travel without dispersive spreading. More generally the material supplies a dispersion relation ω(k); the envelope velocity is ∇k​ω, while constant phase surfaces move at the corresponding phase velocity.
Mechanical waves make the balance of restoring force and inertia particularly visible. A flexible stretched string has c=T/μ​: greater tension stiffens the string, whereas greater linear mass density slows its transverse motion. Small-amplitude acoustic waves in a uniform fluid are longitudinal, with cs​=Ks​/ρ​, where Ks​ is the adiabatic bulk modulus. For an ideal gas, cs​=γp/ρ​. Pressure changes in rapid sound oscillations are approximately adiabatic because little heat crosses a wavelength in one period. Fluids do not support ordinary bulk shear waves, whereas an elastic solid supports both longitudinal and transverse waves. In an isotropic solid their speeds are cL​=(K+4G/3)/ρ​ and cT​=G/ρ​, where G is the shear modulus.
A material can be dispersive even when its equilibrium properties are uniform. In a liquid with a free surface, gravity and surface tension act as restoring mechanisms. A simple inviscid capillary-gravity wave has
ω2=(gk+ργs​​k3)tanh(kh),
(57)
where h is depth and γs​ is surface tension. Long shallow gravity waves have approximately constant speed gh​; deep-water gravity waves have ω=gk​ and group velocity half their phase velocity. Short capillary waves have a different dispersion dominated by surface tension. Bending stiffness similarly changes short-wavelength propagation in a string or plate, giving a contribution proportional to k4 in ω2. Different restoring mechanisms can therefore control different wavelength ranges of the same medium.
Electromagnetic waves propagate in vacuum without material displacement, because Maxwell equations couple changing electric and magnetic fields. They are transverse and travel at c=1/ϵ0​μ0​​. In a simple transparent isotropic material, the phase speed is 1/ϵμ​=c/n, where n is the refractive index. Real material polarization depends on frequency. A Lorentz oscillator model for bound charges explains why permittivity and refractive index become strongly frequency dependent near material resonances, accompanied by absorption. In a birefringent medium, different field polarizations have different propagation constants, permitting wave plates and mode-dependent delays. A electrical conductor additionally supports dissipative currents; its complex wavenumber gives attenuation and, for a good conductor, a skin depth approximately 2/(μσω)​. Energy removed from the wave becomes heat rather than disappearing.
At an interface, frequency is conserved for stationary media, and tangential wave phase must match. These conditions give Snell's law for refraction and constrain reflected and transmitted directions. Amplitudes follow from continuity of the appropriate fields or mechanical forces. On strings the relevant mechanical impedance is Tμ​; for sound it is related to ρcs​, and for electromagnetic waves to μ/ϵ​. A mismatch gives wave reflection, and a lossless boundary conserves the total incident, reflected and transmitted power. Multiple reflections can reinforce or cancel, as in a Fabry–Pérot etalon. Total internal reflection and other boundary conditions confine waves in waveguides. Transverse modes then become discrete, and even an otherwise nondispersive material develops longitudinal cutoffs and wave dispersion. A membrane strip, for example, obeys ωn2​=cm2​[k2+(nπ/a)2].
Propagation also involves transport and loss. The Poynting vector gives electromagnetic energy flux; mechanical energy transport is force or pressure times velocity, averaged over a cycle. Wave absorption, viscosity, electrical resistance and imperfect boundaries dissipate energy. Scattering redistributes energy into other directions and modes, while geometric spreading reduces local intensity without necessarily dissipating anything. These mechanisms should be distinguished when interpreting a decreasing measured amplitude. In strongly absorbing or rapidly varying dispersive regimes, the derivative called group velocity is not automatically the speed of a sharp signal front; pulse reshaping and the medium's causal response must be considered.
Finally, quantum matter waves illustrate how the same propagation mathematics can describe a different physical quantity. For a free nonrelativistic particle, ω=ℏk2/(2m), so the group velocity ℏk/m equals its particle velocity and differs from the phase speed ℏk/(2m). In a periodic crystal, Bloch's theorem replaces the free dispersion by energy bands. Their slope controls wave-packet velocity, vg​=ℏ−1dE/dk, and their curvature gives band effective mass. Thus mechanical confinement, material response and microscopic periodicity all reshape propagation through their allowed modes and dispersion, while the physical interpretation of the wave and its transported quantities remains essential.

The Hall effect is a transverse electric response to a magnetic force on moving charge carriers. Take conventional current density J=Jx​x and magnetic field B=Bz​z in a conducting slab. For one carrier type of number density n and signed electric charge q, the drift velocity is vd​=J/(nq). (58)

RH​=Jx​Bz​Ey​​=nq1​.​
(59)

For width w along y and thickness t along z, I=Jx​wt. Define the signed Hall voltage by VH​=Ey​w, namely the electric potential at the lower-y edge minus that at the higher-y edge. Then

Semiconductor doping introduces controlled impurities to alter equilibrium carrier populations and electrical conductivity. In an intrinsic semiconductor, thermally excited electrons in the conduction band and electron holes in the valence band have equal densities n=p=ni​. A shallow donor impurity, such as phosphorus in silicon, supplies an electron from an impurity level just below the conduction-band edge. Once ionized it is positively charged, and the material is n type. An acceptor impurity, such as boron in silicon, accepts a valence electron into a level just above the valence-band edge, leaving a mobile hole and a negatively charged ionized impurity; the material is p type.
With dopant densities ND​,NA​, charge neutrality requires
p+ND+​=n+NA−​.
(62)
For fully ionized, nondegenerate dopants at equilibrium, the semiconductor mass-action law gives np=ni2​. In a substantially n-type sample, ND​−NA​≫ni​, so
n≃ND​−NA​,p≃ND​−NA​ni2​​.​
(63)
The corresponding p-type result is p≃NA​−ND​ and n≃ni2​/(NA​−ND​). The majority population grows while the minority population decreases; adding donors does not simply add equal numbers of free electrons and holes.
The Fermi energy shifts toward the conduction band for n-type material and toward the valence band for p-type material. In the nondegenerate approximation,
n=NC​e−(EC​−EF​)/(kB​T),p=NV​e−(EF​−EV​)/(kB​T),
(64)
where NC​,NV​ are effective band-edge state densities. At very low temperature, dopants may not be ionized and carrier freeze-out occurs. At intermediate temperatures, ionized dopants dominate the carrier density; at sufficiently high temperature, intrinsic excitation can dominate. Heavy doping can produce degenerate carrier statistics, invalidating these Boltzmann expressions.
The electrical conductivity is σ=e(nμe​+pμh​)​. Doping usually raises the majority carrier density, but ionized impurity scattering can lower mobility, so conductivity need not scale indefinitely with dopant concentration. Joining p-type and n-type regions creates a p-n junction, with a depletion region and built-in electric field established by carrier diffusion and compensating drift. The fixed ionized dopants, not an absence of all charge, account for the depleted region's net charge density.

In a metal, mobile electrons transport heat as well as electric charge. A temperature gradient creates an imbalance between carriers arriving from warmer and colder regions. The resulting heat current obeys Fourier's law jQ​=−κ∇T for an isotropic material, where thermal conductivity κ has units Wm−1K−1. For a bar of area A carrying steady heat flow P, P=−κAdT/dx when parasitic losses are negligible.
A kinetic estimate for the electronic contribution is
κe​≃31​Ce​vF​ℓκ​=31​Ce​vF2​τκ​,
(65)
where Ce​ is the electronic heat capacity per volume, vF​ the Fermi velocity, and ℓκ​=vF​τκ​ the heat-transport mean free path. Fermi-Dirac statistics and the Pauli exclusion principle restrict thermal rearrangements to states near the Fermi surface, rather than allowing every electron to contribute a classical heat capacity. A degenerate parabolic band gives
Ce​=2EF​π2nkB2​T​,κe​=3mπ2nkB2​Tτκ​​.​
(66)
Using the Drude model form σ=ne2τσ​/m for electrical transport, the relation between thermal and electrical relaxation times in metals becomes
σTκe​​=3π2​(ekB​​)2τσ​τκ​​.
(67)
For scattering that relaxes heat and charge currents similarly, τκ​≃τσ​ and the Wiedemann-Franz law follows:
σTκe​​≃L0​,L0​=3e2π2kB2​​≃2.44×10−8WΩK−2.​
(68)
Thus good electrical conductors are usually good thermal conductors. The correct quantum Lorenz number differs from a naive classical electron-gas estimate.
Scattering from impurities, boundaries and phonons limits the heat-transport path. In an impurity-dominated low-temperature regime with approximately constant relaxation time, κe​ is approximately proportional to T. Where phonon-limited electrical resistivity is proportional to T and the Wiedemann-Franz approximation is applicable, κe​ can be roughly temperature independent. Inelastic processes can relax heat and charge currents differently, so the Lorenz ratio is not universal at all temperatures. Lattice phonons add a separate positive thermal contribution; the measured total is κ=κe​+κph​, and the electronic Wiedemann-Franz expression should not be applied blindly to that total.
