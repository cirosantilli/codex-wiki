In a metal, mobile electrons transport heat as well as electric charge. A temperature gradient creates an imbalance between carriers arriving from warmer and colder regions. The resulting heat current obeys Fourier's law jQ​=−κ∇T for an isotropic material, where thermal conductivity κ has units Wm−1K−1. For a bar of area A carrying steady heat flow P, P=−κAdT/dx when parasitic losses are negligible.
A kinetic estimate for the electronic contribution is
κe​≃31​Ce​vF​ℓκ​=31​Ce​vF2​τκ​,
(65)
where Ce​ is the electronic heat capacity per volume, vF​ the Fermi velocity, and ℓκ​=vF​τκ​ the heat-transport mean free path. Fermi-Dirac statistics and the Pauli exclusion principle restrict thermal rearrangements to states near the Fermi surface, rather than allowing every electron to contribute a classical heat capacity. A degenerate parabolic band gives
Ce​=2EF​π2nkB2​T​,κe​=3mπ2nkB2​Tτκ​​.​
(66)
Using the Drude model form σ=ne2τσ​/m for electrical transport, the relation between thermal and electrical relaxation times in metals becomes
σTκe​​=3π2​(ekB​​)2τσ​τκ​​.
(67)
For scattering that relaxes heat and charge currents similarly, τκ​≃τσ​ and the Wiedemann-Franz law follows:
σTκe​​≃L0​,L0​=3e2π2kB2​​≃2.44×10−8WΩK−2.​
(68)
Thus good electrical conductors are usually good thermal conductors. The correct quantum Lorenz number differs from a naive classical electron-gas estimate.
Scattering from impurities, boundaries and phonons limits the heat-transport path. In an impurity-dominated low-temperature regime with approximately constant relaxation time, κe​ is approximately proportional to T. Where phonon-limited electrical resistivity is proportional to T and the Wiedemann-Franz approximation is applicable, κe​ can be roughly temperature independent. Inelastic processes can relax heat and charge currents differently, so the Lorenz ratio is not universal at all temperatures. Lattice phonons add a separate positive thermal contribution; the measured total is κ=κe​+κph​, and the electronic Wiedemann-Franz expression should not be applied blindly to that total.
