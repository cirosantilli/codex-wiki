The Hall effect is a transverse electric response to a magnetic force on moving charge carriers. Take conventional current density J=Jx​x and magnetic field B=Bz​z in a conducting slab. For one carrier type of number density n and signed electric charge q, the drift velocity is vd​=J/(nq). Carriers initially deflect sideways until a Hall electric field balances the magnetic Lorentz force:
q(EH​+vd​×B)=0,Ey​=nqJx​Bz​​.
(58)
The Hall coefficient is therefore
RH​=Jx​Bz​Ey​​=nq1​.​
(59)
For electrons it is −1/(ne), whereas a simple hole-dominated conductor gives +1/(pe). The sign reveals the effective carrier sign; the magnitude estimates their density in the one-carrier approximation.
For width w along y and thickness t along z, I=Jx​wt. Define the signed Hall voltage by VH​=Ey​w, namely the electric potential at the lower-y edge minus that at the higher-y edge. Then
VH​=nqtIBz​​,n=et∣VH​∣∣IBz​∣​(∣q∣=e).​
(60)
This explicit contact convention fixes the voltage sign. A Hall bar with longitudinal and transverse contacts measures both electrical resistivity and the Hall response. Reverse the field and take the part of the transverse voltage odd in Bz​ to suppress longitudinal-voltage pickup from misaligned contacts; reversing current provides another useful check.
A Hall measurement is useful for carrier identification, semiconductor doping measurements and magnetic-field sensing. Combining a one-carrier RH​ with electrical conductivity gives a mobility magnitude μ=σ∣RH​∣. However, mixed electron and hole conduction is not described by a single density: in weak fields,
RH​=e(pμh​+nμe​)2pμh2​−nμe2​​.
(61)
The response weights mobilities differently from ordinary conductivity. Complicated energy bands, anisotropic Fermi surfaces and multiple bands can likewise change the simple interpretation in metals; a positive Hall coefficient does not mean that the material contains positively charged fundamental electrons.
