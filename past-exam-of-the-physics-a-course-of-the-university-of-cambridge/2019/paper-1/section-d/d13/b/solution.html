Temperature changes affect measurements through several routes. Thermal expansion changes lengths and alignments; temperature-dependent resistance and refractive index change electrical and optical calibrations; temperature differences between dissimilar junctions generate Seebeck effect voltages. These slow environmental changes are distinct from unavoidable equilibrium thermal fluctuations and Johnson-Nyquist noise. A useful strategy identifies the coupling to the measured signal before choosing a remedy.
Reduce environmental coupling by enclosing the apparatus against drafts, using thermal insulation and radiation shields, isolating heat sources, and minimizing dissipated power. A vacuum enclosure suppresses convection but leaves thermal conduction and thermal radiation paths to control. A large heat capacity reduces rapid temperature excursions. In a simple thermal model with heat capacity CT​ and thermal conductance GT​ to a reservoir, the relaxation time is CT​/GT​, so adequate settling time is necessary after changing the apparatus. A temperature sensor close to the sensitive element and stable negative feedback heating or cooling can regulate its temperature; poorly placed sensors or excessive feedback gain can leave gradients or cause oscillations.
Electrical thermal noise has the classical low-frequency one-sided voltage power spectral density
SV​(f)=4kB​TR,Vrms​=4kB​TRB​​
(76)
for a resistor measured over a flat bandwidth B without significant loading. More generally replace B by the effective noise bandwidth. Lower temperature or narrower bandwidth reduces the noise, and resistance choices must account for source loading and the amplifier's own voltage and current noise. A lock-in amplifier uses modulation and narrowband detection to reject much low-frequency drift and out-of-band noise, while thermal noise inside the accepted band remains. Temperature stabilization does not remove equilibrium fluctuations.
Use a matched reference or differential bridge to reject common environmental changes. Keep junctions isothermal and choose matched materials to reduce thermoelectric offsets. For a resistance measurement, reversing a small test current separates a stable offset from the wanted voltage:
V(+I)=IR+Vth​,V(−I)=−IR+Vth​,R=2IV(+I)−V(−I)​.​
(77)
Reversal must be fast enough that the offset stays approximately fixed, with enough settling time and sufficiently little self-heating. Recording the sample temperature permits a measured calibration slope dS/dT to correct a signal to a reference temperature; the correction's calibration and thermometer uncertainties must be propagated. Independent white noise fluctuations decrease under averaging, whereas correlated temperature drift may not decrease as the inverse square root of the number of readings. Temperature records and repeated reference measurements help distinguish the two.
