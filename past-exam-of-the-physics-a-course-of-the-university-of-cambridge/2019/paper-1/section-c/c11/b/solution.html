In the Schrödinger picture, a time-independent Hamiltonian gives ∣ψS​(t)⟩=U(t)∣ψ(0)⟩, where U=e−iHt/ℏ. The Heisenberg picture transfers this evolution to each observable:
AH​(t)=U†(t)AS​(t)U(t),∣ψH​⟩=∣ψ(0)⟩.

Both pictures give the same probabilities and expectation values. Differentiating yields the Heisenberg equation of motion
dtdAH​​=ℏi​[HH​,AH​]+U†∂t∂AS​​U.​

The last term is an explicit change of the measured operator. If it is absent and [H,A]=0, the observable is conserved.
The corresponding classical equation is dA/dt={A,H}PB​+∂A/∂t. The correspondence principle relates the Poisson bracket to [A,H]/(iℏ), with suitable operator ordering and a classical limit. For H=p2/(2m)+V(x), the canonical commutation relation gives
x˙H​=mpH​​,p˙​H​=−V′(xH​).
(60)
Taking means gives Ehrenfest theorem. Its second equation contains −⟨V′(x)⟩, which need not equal −V′(⟨x⟩). A narrow wave packet in a slowly varying force field can follow the classical equations approximately; a quadratic potential makes the equality exact for the means.
For a quantum harmonic oscillator, direct solution gives xH​(t)=xH​(0)cosωt+pH​(0)sinωt/(mω) and pH​(t)=pH​(0)cosωt−mωxH​(0)sinωt. These have the classical form as operator identities. A coherent state supplies a localized center moving on the classical orbit, whereas an energy eigenstate has stationary density and ⟨x⟩=0, even at large excitation. Thus large quantum numbers alone do not create a localized classical trajectory. Classical behavior also concerns state preparation, relative fluctuations and the resolution of the measurements.
