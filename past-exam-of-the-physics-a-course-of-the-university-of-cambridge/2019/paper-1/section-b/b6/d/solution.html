A Stern-Gerlach experiment uses an oven and slits to produce a collimated atomic beam, conventionally of neutral silver atoms, and sends it through an inhomogeneous magnetic field onto a position-sensitive detector. The unpaired outer electron supplies the effective spin one-half magnetic dipole moment; neutral atoms avoid the ordinary Lorentz deflection of a charged beam. For an analysing direction z, the spatial gradient produces a force approximately Fz​=μz​∂z​Bz​. The resulting separated beam positions measure the allowed projections of angular momentum. A spin one-half beam gives two branches, rather than the continuous distribution expected from classical dipoles with arbitrary orientations. A uniform magnetic field can rotate a spin but does not itself provide this spatial separation.
To measure the rotation, first use a z-directed Stern-Gerlach experiment and an aperture to transmit only the +z branch. Pass this prepared spin eigenstate through a region of approximately uniform field in the x direction, then use a second z analyser to separate and count the two populations. For a field region of length ℓ and beam speed v, the precession angle is θ=λBℓ/v, and the predicted fractions are
P+​=cos2(θ/2),P−​=sin2(θ/2).​
(18)
Varying B or the transit time gives complementary oscillations, demonstrating coherent Larmor precession. Velocity selection and a sufficiently uniform rotation field keep different particles from accumulating different phases and washing out the contrast. Detection should compare the branch fractions after allowing for losses and analyser efficiency.
