A Stern-Gerlach experiment uses an oven and slits to produce a collimated atomic beam, conventionally of neutral silver atoms, and sends it through an inhomogeneous magnetic field onto a position-sensitive detector. The unpaired outer electron supplies the effective spin one-half magnetic dipole moment; neutral atoms avoid the ordinary Lorentz deflection of a charged beam. For an analysing direction z, the spatial gradient produces a force approximately Fz​=μz​∂z​Bz​. The resulting separated beam positions measure the allowed projections of angular momentum. A spin one-half beam gives two branches, rather than the continuous distribution expected from classical dipoles with arbitrary orientations. A uniform magnetic field can rotate a spin but does not itself provide this spatial separation.
To measure the rotation, first use a z-directed Stern-Gerlach experiment and an aperture to transmit only the +z branch. Pass this prepared spin eigenstate through a region of approximately uniform field in the x direction, then use a second z analyser to separate and count the two populations. For a field region of length ℓ and beam speed v, the precession angle is θ=λBℓ/v, and the predicted fractions are
P+​=cos2(θ/2),P−​=sin2(θ/2).​
(18)
Varying B or the transit time gives complementary oscillations, demonstrating coherent Larmor precession. Velocity selection and a sufficiently uniform rotation field keep different particles from accumulating different phases and washing out the contrast. Detection should compare the branch fractions after allowing for losses and analyser efficiency.

In the sudden approximation, the electronic wavefunction does not have time to change during the increase from Zi​=1 to Zf​=2. A localized quantum particle is described by a wave packet: a coherent quantum superposition of momentum components, rather than a single delocalized plane wave. The Born rule assigns the position probability density ∣ψ(x,t)∣2. A useful normalized initial Gaussian wave packet is
ψ(x,0)=(2πσ02​)−1/4exp[−4σ02​(x−x0​)2​+ℏip0​(x−x0​)​].
(50)
Its mean position and momentum are x0​ and p0​, and its standard deviations satisfy σx​(0)=σ0​, σp​=ℏ/(2σ0​). This unchirped initial state saturates the Heisenberg uncertainty relation.
For a free particle, the momentum-space Schrödinger equation multiplies each amplitude by e−ip2t/(2mℏ). The inverse Fourier transform is a Gaussian integral, giving the free Gaussian wave packet evolution
ψ(x,t)=1+iη​(2πσ02​)−1/4​exp[−4σ02​(1+iη)(x−x0​−p0​t/m)2​+ℏip0​(x−x0​)​−2mℏip02​t​],η=2mσ02​ℏt​.
(51)
One way to obtain this expression explicitly is to write the initial momentum amplitude as proportional to exp[−σ02​(p−p0​)2/ℏ2−ipx0​/ℏ], multiply by the free evolution phase and complete the square in p. Taking the modulus squared removes the position-dependent phase and leaves
∣ψ(x,t)∣2=2π​σx​(t)1​exp[−2σx​(t)2(x−xc​(t))2​],xc​(t)=x0​+mp0​t​,σx​(t)=σ0​1+(τt​)2​,τ=ℏ2mσ02​​.​
(52)
The expectation value follows uniform classical motion while the density spreads. A narrower initial packet needs a wider momentum distribution and therefore spreads more rapidly. At long times its width grows linearly with ∣t∣; its variance grows quadratically, unlike the linear variance growth of ordinary diffusion. The probability continuity equation preserves total probability despite the decreasing peak height. The mean momentum and the mean energy (p02​+σp2​)/(2m) remain constant, since the momentum populations do not change.
The same result has a wave interpretation. For a free nonrelativistic particle ω(k)=ℏk2/(2m), so the group velocity is dω/dk=ℏk/m, equal to p0​/m at the packet center. The curvature d2ω/dk2=ℏ/m makes different momentum components travel with different group velocities, producing dispersion. The phase velocity is ω/k=ℏk/(2m) for this energy convention and does not describe the motion of the envelope. The evolving quadratic spatial phase is essential to the spreading; a density snapshot alone does not specify the full quantum state.
A freely spreading Gaussian wave packet remains a pure Gaussian state but develops position-momentum correlations. Its symmetrized covariance is Cxp​=⟨{x−⟨x⟩,p−⟨p⟩}⟩/2=tσp2​/m. Consequently
σx​σp​=2ℏ​1+η2​,σx2​σp2​−Cxp2​=4ℏ2​.​
(53)
Equality in the Robertson-Schrödinger uncertainty relation survives at every time, whereas the simpler product σx​σp​ equals ℏ/2 only initially. A chirped Gaussian wave packet can already have Cxp​(0)=0. More generally, x(t)=x(0)+p(0)t/m gives σx​(t)2=σx​(0)2+2tCxp​(0)/m+t2σp​(0)2/m2. A sufficiently negative initial covariance permits initial contraction before subsequent spreading.
A potential energy changes this behavior. A quadratic potential preserves the Gaussian form because the evolution of position and momentum is linear. For a quantum harmonic oscillator, the Gaussian covariance in a harmonic potential is
σx​(t)2=σx​(0)2cos2ωt+m2ω2σp​(0)2​sin2ωt+mω2Cxp​(0)​sinωtcosωt.
(54)
A coherent state has σx2​=ℏ/(2mω), σp2​=mℏω/2 and zero centered covariance. Its width is constant, and its center follows xc​(t)=x0​cosωt+p0​sinωt/(mω). In oscillator notation its coherent state label rotates as α(t)=α(0)e−iωt, apart from the common ground-energy phase of the state vector. A squeezed coherent state generally has a width that breathes at 2ω. Thus being Gaussian does not imply either free spreading or constant width; the Hamiltonian operator and initial covariance determine the result.
For a general nonquadratic potential, different parts of the packet experience different nonlinear forces, and an initially Gaussian profile need not remain Gaussian. Ehrenfest theorem gives the exact relations d⟨x⟩/dt=⟨p⟩/m and d⟨p⟩/dt=−⟨V′(x)⟩. Replacing the last mean by −V′(⟨x⟩) is exact for a quadratic potential and approximate for a packet narrow compared with the scale on which the force varies. This connects packet motion to the correspondence principle without treating a quantum state as a definite classical path. Scattering can split a packet into reflected and transmitted components, while confinement can produce oscillations and interference between discrete energy eigenstates. Their relative phases evolve even when their populations remain constant. Localization, spreading and coherent motion all follow from the same linear unitary time evolution.

An observable is represented by a self-adjoint operator. For an ideal projective measurement, let Pa​ be its spectral projector for outcome a. The Born rule gives p(a)=⟨ψ∣Pa​∣ψ⟩, or p(a)=tr(ρPa​) for a density matrix ρ. Conditional on obtaining a, the Lüders rule gives
∣ψ⟩⟼p(a)​Pa​∣ψ⟩​,ρ⟼p(a)Pa​ρPa​​.​
(61)
For a degenerate outcome this projects onto the whole associated eigenspace, rather than arbitrarily selecting a vector within it. An immediate repeated ideal measurement returns the same outcome, provided there is no intervening evolution or disturbance.
Commuting finite-dimensional observables admit a common orthonormal eigenbasis; their projectors commute and ideal joint measurements are compatible. Noncommuting observables generally have no complete common eigenbasis, and measurement order can affect the subsequent statistics. Particular common eigenvectors can nevertheless exist, so a nonzero commutator does not mean that every state has a nonzero uncertainty in both quantities.
For a quantitative constraint, apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to (A−⟨A⟩)∣ψ⟩ and (B−⟨B⟩)∣ψ⟩. The imaginary part of their inner product is ⟨[A,B]⟩/(2i), giving the Robertson uncertainty principle
ΔAΔB≥21​∣⟨[A,B]⟩∣.​
(62)
The canonical commutation relation [x,p]=iℏ gives ΔxΔp≥ℏ/2. Retaining the real part of the same inner product gives the stronger Robertson-Schrödinger uncertainty relation, including a covariance term. These are constraints on the preparation of a quantum state, not simply statements about a poorly calibrated instrument.
A sequential spin one-half example makes the change in statistics explicit. Prepare ∣+z⟩. An immediate Sz​ measurement is certainly positive. If an Sx​ measurement is inserted first, its outcomes each have probability 1/2 and leave states ∣±x⟩. Each then yields either Sz​ result with probability 1/2, so the final positive fraction is 1/2. The change reflects projection onto incompatible spin eigenstates, even with ideal measuring apparatus. The theory of measurement in quantum mechanics allows more general state-update rules, but the operator description and the Born rule still constrain their probabilities.

An ideal operational amplifier has infinite differential open-loop gain, infinite input impedance, zero output impedance, unlimited bandwidth and slew rate, and zero input offset voltage, input bias current and intrinsic noise. The idealized model has no finite output-swing or output-current limit.
The usual circuit rules are
i+​=i−​=0,v+​=v−​ under stable, unsaturated negative feedback.​
(63)
The first follows from infinite input impedance. The second follows because a finite output requires v+​−v−​=vo​/A→0 as the open-loop gain tends to infinity. It is a virtual equality of potentials, not a conducting connection between the inputs. It cannot be imposed for open-loop operation or after an amplifier reaches its supply rails.

A real operational amplifier has large but finite, frequency-dependent open-loop gain; the gain falls and its phase changes at higher frequencies. Finite bandwidth and slew rate constrain small-signal and large-signal responses respectively. There are also finite input impedance and output impedance, nonzero input bias currents and input offset voltage, and internally generated noise. Offset and bias can drift with temperature.
The output is limited by the supply voltages and output-current capability, and the inputs have a permitted common-mode range. Rejection of common-mode and supply changes is finite. Consequently v+​−v−​ need not vanish exactly, and neither the virtual-ground approximation nor linear gain formulas apply after saturation. For the calculation below the stipulated infinite input impedance removes the input loading currents, while finite gain and optionally finite output resistance are retained.

A chi-squared test compares squared deviations with the size expected from the assumed random errors. For independent observations with a normal distribution yi​ with known standard deviations σi​ and predictions μi​(θ), use
Q=∑i=1N​σi2​[yi​−μi​(θ)]2​.
(74)
For a correct fixed model, the standardized independent residuals are unit-variance normal distributions, so Q has a chi-squared distribution with ν=N. Fitting p independent linear parameters removes p residual directions, giving ν=N−p. This distribution is exact for a full-rank linear model with known errors with a normal distribution; it is an approximation for many nonlinear fits. With known correlated errors with a multivariate normal distribution, replace the sum by Q=rTΣ−1r, where Σ is the covariance matrix and its rank and fitted constraints determine the available statistical degrees of freedom.
Under the null hypothesis, EQ=ν and VarQ=2ν. The reduced statistic Q/ν should therefore be near one, with standard deviation 2/ν​, rather than equal to one exactly. An upper-tail p-value is
p=Pr(χν2​≥Qobs​).​
(75)
Compare it with a significance threshold chosen in advance. A small value indicates tension with the model together with its error assumptions; it can arise from a wrong mean model, underestimated uncertainties or neglected correlations. An unusually small statistic can suggest overestimated uncertainties, correlations or overfitting. A p-value is not the probability that the model is true, and failure to reject is not proof of the model.
For counts in categories, the Pearson form is Q=∑j=1k​(Oj​−Ej​)2/Ej​. Its chi-squared distribution is an asymptotic approximation requiring adequate expected counts. For a fixed-total multinomial sample there is one normalization constraint, so the usual statistical degrees of freedom are k−1−p after fitting p other parameters. Independent Poisson bins instead start with k statistical degrees of freedom before parameters are fitted. Sparse counts require an appropriate count likelihood or a calibrated exact or simulation-based test; plugging tiny expected counts into the asymptotic formula is unreliable. The error model, binning, fitted parameters and chosen test must therefore be reported with the result.

Temperature changes affect measurements through several routes. Thermal expansion changes lengths and alignments; temperature-dependent resistance and refractive index change electrical and optical calibrations; temperature differences between dissimilar junctions generate Seebeck effect voltages. These slow environmental changes are distinct from unavoidable equilibrium thermal fluctuations and Johnson-Nyquist noise. A useful strategy identifies the coupling to the measured signal before choosing a remedy.
Reduce environmental coupling by enclosing the apparatus against drafts, using thermal insulation and radiation shields, isolating heat sources, and minimizing dissipated power. A vacuum enclosure suppresses convection but leaves thermal conduction and thermal radiation paths to control. A large heat capacity reduces rapid temperature excursions. In a simple thermal model with heat capacity CT​ and thermal conductance GT​ to a reservoir, the relaxation time is CT​/GT​, so adequate settling time is necessary after changing the apparatus. A temperature sensor close to the sensitive element and stable negative feedback heating or cooling can regulate its temperature; poorly placed sensors or excessive feedback gain can leave gradients or cause oscillations.
Electrical thermal noise has the classical low-frequency one-sided voltage power spectral density
SV​(f)=4kB​TR,Vrms​=4kB​TRB​​
(76)
for a resistor measured over a flat bandwidth B without significant loading. More generally replace B by the effective noise bandwidth. Lower temperature or narrower bandwidth reduces the noise, and resistance choices must account for source loading and the amplifier's own voltage and current noise. A lock-in amplifier uses modulation and narrowband detection to reject much low-frequency drift and out-of-band noise, while thermal noise inside the accepted band remains. Temperature stabilization does not remove equilibrium fluctuations.
Use a matched reference or differential bridge to reject common environmental changes. Keep junctions isothermal and choose matched materials to reduce thermoelectric offsets. For a resistance measurement, reversing a small test current separates a stable offset from the wanted voltage:
V(+I)=IR+Vth​,V(−I)=−IR+Vth​,R=2IV(+I)−V(−I)​.​
(77)
Reversal must be fast enough that the offset stays approximately fixed, with enough settling time and sufficiently little self-heating. Recording the sample temperature permits a measured calibration slope dS/dT to correct a signal to a reference temperature; the correction's calibration and thermometer uncertainties must be propagated. Independent white noise fluctuations decrease under averaging, whereas correlated temperature drift may not decrease as the inverse square root of the number of readings. Temperature records and repeated reference measurements help distinguish the two.

Digital acquisition converts a continuous voltage signal into timed numerical samples. A practical chain is a suitably buffered and amplified sensor signal, an analogue anti-aliasing filter, a sampling or sample-and-hold stage, an analog-to-digital converter, and a clocked data-storage system. Choose the gain so that the interesting variation uses the available input range without clipping. Finite input impedance can load a sensor, so an appropriate buffer or operational amplifier may be needed.
Sampling makes time discrete. If the conditioned signal is confined below frequency fmax​, choose a sampling rate fs​>2fmax​, allowing a transition band for the physical filter. The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem permits ideal reconstruction of a suitably bandlimited function from exact samples. Without filtering, components separated by integer multiples of fs​ have identical samples; in particular real sinusoids at f and fs​−f can be indistinguishable. This aliasing cannot generally be undone by later digital filtering. Oversampling can ease filter design, but does not replace the requirement to suppress unwanted high-frequency content before sampling.
Amplitude quantization is separate from sampling. A uniform b-bit converter covering voltage span Vspan​ has step q≃Vspan​/2b. Rounding an in-range input gives the uniform-quantization error bound ∣ϵ∣≤q/2. If the rounding errors can additionally be modeled as uniform and uncorrelated, their standard deviation is q/12​. This statistical estimate is not a guarantee for a constant input or a coherent periodic signal. More bits increase resolution, but accuracy also depends on voltage-reference calibration, offset, gain error, nonlinearity and electronic noise. Out-of-range signals clip and invalidate the rounding bound.
Clock quality matters too. Timing jitter δt gives a voltage error approximately (dV/dt)δt; for a sinusoid its scale grows with 2πfVp​σt​. A clock calibration error biases measured frequencies and time intervals. Simultaneous multichannel signals need known relative timing, and the sampling and analogue settling times must be appropriate to the signal bandwidth.
For nc​ channels the raw recording rate is approximately nc​fs​b bits per second, before overhead, so storage and sustained transfer speed must prevent missing data. Store units, channel identities, timestamps, sampling rate, input ranges and calibration information along with the readings. Preserve the original samples when applying digital filters or other processing. Averaging can improve precision for independent fluctuating errors, but cannot restore aliased frequencies, clipped peaks or a fixed rounding bias. Digital recording makes reproducible analysis and transmission possible when its sampling, amplitude and timing limitations are all controlled.
