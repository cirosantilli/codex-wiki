B9 OurBigBook.com
Exchange constraint. Exchange of two identical particles cannot change physical predictions. For the usual three-dimensional bosonic or fermionic state space, the exchange operator obeys P122​=1, so its allowed eigenvalues are ±1. bosons use the symmetric overall wavefunction, P12​Ψ=+Ψ; fermions use the antisymmetric overall wavefunction, P12​Ψ=−Ψ. Exchange includes the spatial coordinates and all internal labels. The identity P122​=1 alone does not prove which particle species has which statistics; that assignment is the Spin-statistics theorem.
Factored fermion states. If Ψ=ψspace​χspin​ and both factors have definite exchange symmetry, their exchange signs must multiply to −1. Thus a symmetric spatial factor requires an antisymmetric spin factor, and an antisymmetric spatial factor requires a symmetric spin factor. For two spin-one-half particles, the former spin factor is the singlet (∣↑↓⟩−∣↓↑⟩)/2​; the latter factors are the three triplet states. A more general entangled spin-space state need not factor, but must still be antisymmetric overall.
hydrogen spin isomers. The two protons are identical spin-one-half fermions. parahydrogen has total nuclear spin In​=0, the antisymmetric singlet; orthohydrogen has In​=1, the symmetric triplet. Here In​ is a spin quantum number, distinct from the moment of inertia used below.
Rotational restriction. In the electronic and vibrational ground state, these two factors are symmetric under nuclear exchange. Exchanging the protons reverses the internuclear axis, (θ,ϕ)↦(π−θ,ϕ+π). The spherical harmonic rotational state contributes the exchange sign (−1)ℓ. Overall antisymmetry therefore requires
(−1)ℓ(−1)=−1for <parahydrogen>,(−1)ℓ(+1)=−1for <orthohydrogen>.
(29)
Hence parahydrogen has even ℓ; orthohydrogen has odd ℓ. The ℓ here labels molecular rotation, rather than the angular momentum obtained by also coupling the nuclear spins.
Conversion heat. The ortho-para conversion heat follows from the linear rigid rotor approximation, Eℓ​=ℏ2ℓ(ℓ+1)/(2I) with I=μr2≃mp​r2/2. Thus conversion of one molecule from ℓ=1 to ℓ=0 releases
ΔE=Iℏ2​=mp​r22ℏ2​≃2.43×10−21 J.
(30)
Neglecting the small electron contribution to the molecular mass, a sample of mass M contains M/(2mp​) molecules. Only the initial orthohydrogen fraction releases this energy, giving
Q=43​2mp​M​mp​r22ℏ2​=4mp2​r23Mℏ2​=5.44×105 J(M=1kg)​.
(31)
The estimate uses r=74 pm, mp​≃1.67262×10−27 kg and ℏ≃1.05457×10−34 J s. Handbook-level rounding and the rigid rotor approximation justify about three significant figures, not a high-precision thermochemical prediction.[ref]
Storage consequence. The internal conversion is a delayed heat source even in a well insulated container, causing warming or additional evaporation of the liquid. Insulation reduces external heat transfer but does not remove this source. Converting to predominantly parahydrogen during liquefaction, while removing the released heat, reduces the subsequent storage heat load. No evaporation fraction can be calculated without a latent heat and a model for how the heat is removed.

A density operator describes both a quantum state prepared in one pure state and a statistical preparation whose members occupy different pure states. If normalized vectors ∣ψi​⟩ occur with probabilities pi​, define
ρ=∑i​pi​∣ψi​⟩⟨ψi​∣,pi​≥0,∑i​pi​=1.
(32)
The resulting operator is Hermitian, positive semidefinite and has trace one. Positivity follows because ⟨v∣ρ∣v⟩=∑i​pi​∣⟨ψi​∣v⟩∣2≥0. Conversely, the spectral theorem gives such a representation of any finite-dimensional operator with these properties, using its eigenvectors and eigenvalues. The matrix representing ρ depends on the chosen basis; the operator and its predictions do not.
One operator gives every measurement probability. For a projective measurement with orthogonal projections Πa​ and outcomes a,
P(a)=Tr(ρΠa​),⟨A⟩=Tr(ρA),(ΔA)2=Tr(ρA2)−⟨A⟩2.
(33)
For example, the first formula follows by averaging ⟨ψi​∣Πa​∣ψi​⟩ over the preparation. The trace expression is basis independent. A general measurement replaces Πa​ by positive measurement effects that sum to the identity, without changing the probability formula. Thus ρ specifies the statistics accessible to measurements on that system.
The distinction between pure and mixed states is especially transparent in the eigenvalues. A pure state has ρ=∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣, ρ2=ρ, and Trρ2=1. A mixed state has at least two positive eigenvalues and Trρ2<1. For a qubit,
ρ=21​(I+r⋅σ),∣r∣≤1,Trρ2=21​(1+∣r∣2).
(34)
pure states lie on the Bloch sphere; mixed states lie inside the Bloch ball. The maximally mixed qubit ρ=I/2 can be prepared by choosing equally between the two z spin eigenstates, or equally between the two x spin eigenstates. These ensembles are different preparation procedures but give the same density operator. No measurement on the resulting qubit alone can identify which ensemble was used.
quantum coherence must be distinguished from merely having two populated levels. In the z basis, the state ∣+x⟩ has
ρ+x​=21​(11​11​),ρmix​=21​(10​01​).
(35)
Both give equal probabilities for the two z outcomes. A measurement along x gives the positive result with probability one for the first state and one half for the second. The off-diagonal entries encode the relative phase information responsible for this difference. Whether a matrix entry is off diagonal is basis dependent; purity is not.
For an isolated system, each component evolves with the same unitary time-evolution operator. Therefore ρ(t)=U(t)ρ(0)U†(t) and
iℏρ˙​=[H,ρ]​.
(36)
Unitary evolution preserves the eigenvalues and purity of ρ. A density operator commuting with a time-independent Hamiltonian operator is stationary. A thermal equilibrium example is the canonical ensemble ρβ​=e−βH/Z, where Z=Tr(e−βH) and β=1/(kB​T). expectation values then reproduce quantum statistical mechanics without assigning the system a definite energy eigenstate on every run.
A mixed state can also arise without classical randomness in the joint preparation. For a bipartite quantum system, the reduced density operator ρA​=TrB​ρAB​ gives every measurement probability accessible to subsystem A. For the Bell state (∣00⟩+∣11⟩)/2​, the joint state is pure, whereas ρA​=I/2. Its local mixedness reflects entanglement with the inaccessible system; the partial trace discards correlations that matter for joint measurements. More generally, interaction with an environment can suppress off-diagonal entries in a selected basis, producing decoherence while the combined system still evolves unitarily.
Finally, state evolution conditional on a measurement outcome differs from isolated evolution. An ideal projective measurement yielding a updates the state by the Lüders rule ρ↦Πa​ρΠa​/P(a). Discarding the outcome gives ∑a​Πa​ρΠa​, which generally removes coherence between different eigenspaces. The Von Neumann entropy −Tr(ρlnρ) quantifies mixedness: it vanishes for pure states and reaches lnd for I/d. Multiplying by kB​ gives thermodynamic entropy units. These uses make the density operator a common language for preparation uncertainty, measurement, thermal states and entangled subsystems, while retaining their distinct physical origins.

With two coherent paths, the probability amplitude at a screen is ψ1​(x)+eiϕψ2​(x). The Born rule gives
p(x)=∣ψ1​∣2+∣ψ2​∣2+2Re(eiϕψ1∗​ψ2​).
(37)
The final term shifts when the relative phase changes, producing interference fringes. For slit separation d in the far field, the phase difference is approximately 2πdsinθ/λ; constructive interference occurs at dsinθ=nλ. Finite slit widths add a diffraction envelope, so an ideal equally bright, infinitely narrow slit model does not predict the full observed envelope.
single-electron interference pattern buildup starts with isolated localized detector events at sufficiently low source intensity. Repeated events accumulate the interference probability distribution. Thus detection is discrete while propagation is described by coherent probability amplitudes. The effect cannot be explained by interference between different particles if the apparatus is occupied by at most one particle at a time. A motivating realization used a field-emission electron source, an electron biprism to recombine two paths, electron lenses and a position-sensitive electron counter; it recorded individual arrivals building up the fringes. This was a biprism realization of two-path interference, not a plate with two literal slits.[ref]
The crucial comparison is between both coherent paths open and a preparation that supplies which-path information. If the paths become correlated with detector states ∣D1​⟩,∣D2​⟩, the joint state is ψ1​∣D1​⟩+eiϕψ2​∣D2​⟩. Ignoring that detector multiplies the interference term by ⟨D1​∣D2​⟩. Orthogonal detector states remove the term completely, while identical detector states preserve it. This is a consequence of entanglement and the partial trace; a human need not read the detector.
In a real test, check the single-path distributions, detector background, source coherence and stability of the relative phase. Classical phase drift can wash out fringes without supplying which-path information. Conversely, localized detections alone do not establish coherent two-path propagation; the controlled phase dependence of their accumulated distribution provides the discriminating evidence.

For a nonrelativistic spinless particle in a scalar potential, the Time-dependent Schrödinger equation is
iℏ∂t∂ψ​=[−2mℏ2​∇2+V(r,t)]ψ​.
(41)
An initial wavefunction together with boundary conditions specifies the evolution. The Hamiltonian operator must be self-adjoint on the appropriate domain for closed-system evolution to be unitary. The equation is linear, so a superposition of solutions is another solution. Its complex wavefunction predicts probability amplitudes; the Born rule separately supplies the connection to measurement probabilities.
For a time-independent potential, separation ψ(r,t)=ϕ(r)e−iEt/ℏ gives the time-independent Schrödinger equation Hϕ=Eϕ. Normalizability and boundary conditions select the allowed energies of bound states; scattering states instead use incoming and outgoing boundary conditions and generalized normalization. A general state is a superposition of energy eigenstates, with a spectral integral as well if there is a continuous spectrum. Different energies accumulate different phases, allowing time-dependent position densities and oscillatory expectation values even though a single energy eigenstate has a stationary density.
For a real scalar potential, combining the equation with its complex conjugate yields probability conservation:
∂t​∣ψ∣2+∇⋅J=0,J=mℏ​Im(ψ∗∇ψ).
(42)
With no current escaping the boundary, ∫∣ψ∣2d3r is constant. Matching conditions at a finite potential step follow from the differential equation and enforce the flux balance used in B6.
The Schrödinger equation is a dynamical postulate of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, not a consequence of classical mechanics alone. The substitutions E↦iℏ∂t​ and p↦−iℏ∇ motivate its free-particle form from the dispersion relation, but do not derive the measurement postulates. The scalar equation omits intrinsic spin and relativistic particle creation. Spinful or relativistic problems require the corresponding extended theory. Nor does its deterministic unitary evolution, by itself, prescribe conditioning on a particular observed measurement outcome.

A systematic error is a reproducible displacement caused by the apparatus, procedure or measurement model. Repeating the same experiment can determine its mean very precisely while retaining that displacement. Reducing random measurement error therefore does not establish accuracy.
Start with a measurement model listing the response, zero offset, calibration scale and relevant environmental corrections. Compare the instrument with traceable standards at several points across its working range; a single zero check cannot reveal a gain error or nonlinearity. Measure blanks and backgrounds using the same acquisition chain as the signal. Repeat calibration before and after the run to detect drift, and retain the uncertainty of every applied correction.
systematic-error cancellation by reversal separates a wanted effect from an offset with a different symmetry. For example, a resistance measurement with thermoelectric offset V0​ gives V+​=RI+V0​ and V−​=−RI+V0​. The difference gives
R=2IV+​−V−​​​,
(49)
cancelling a stable offset, while (V+​+V−​)/2 diagnoses it. Alternate the current direction quickly enough that the offset is approximately unchanged. This does not cancel a current-scale calibration error or resistance changes from Joule heating; vary the current magnitude and monitor temperature to test those effects. Control measurements must target the actual proposed bias.
Randomizing the order of settings prevents slow drift from consistently imitating a physical trend. Interleaving a reference measurement helps track that drift. Reversing geometry, exchanging matched detectors and changing alignment can expose effects tied to one part of the apparatus. Repeating the measurement with an independent method is particularly valuable when its dominant biases differ; agreement between two instruments sharing the same calibration does not test a common calibration error.
Apply justified corrections, propagate their uncertainties and include correlations when the same calibration enters several observations. A systematic component shared by all observations does not shrink as 1/N​. A measurement uncertainty budget should distinguish the physical origin of each effect from the method used to evaluate its uncertainty: statistical Type A and other Type B evaluations do not simply mean random and systematic, respectively.[ref]

Bayes' theorem updates a hypothesis using observed data. For mutually exclusive exhaustive hypotheses Hj​ and data D of nonzero probability,
P(Hj​∣D)=∑k​P(D∣Hk​)P(Hk​)P(D∣Hj​)P(Hj​)​​.
(50)
The prior P(Hj​) describes information before these data; the likelihood P(D∣Hj​) describes the sampling model; the denominator is the evidence; and the posterior is the updated probability. likelihood and posterior are different conditional quantities. For a continuous parameter, sums become integrals and the posterior is a probability density.
Consider a counter registering independent events at unknown constant rate λ, observed for known exposure T with negligible background and dead time. The observed count has a Poisson distribution:
P(n∣λ,T)=n!(λT)ne−λT​.
(51)
Take a proper gamma prior in shape-rate convention,
π(λ)=Γ(α)βα​λα−1e−βλ,α,β>0.
(52)
Multiplying by the likelihood adds the count to the shape and the exposure to the rate. Normalization therefore gives
λ∣n,T∼Gamma(α+n,β+T)​,E(λ∣n,T)=β+Tα+n​,Var(λ∣n,T)=(β+T)2α+n​.
(53)
Here β has exposure units. This is Poisson-gamma conjugacy. Successive independent counting runs update the same posterior by adding their counts and exposures, so the result agrees with analysing all the data together.
A credible interval contains a specified posterior probability for λ, conditional on the chosen prior and model. For low counts, the posterior can be asymmetric and sensitive to the prior, and a symmetric Gaussian error bar can be misleading. For large n and an exposure dominating prior information, the mean approaches n/T and its standard deviation approaches n​/T. Background, missed events or time-dependent rates require a richer likelihood; Bayes' theorem cannot compensate for an incorrect physical counting model. The prior must be specified independently of the same data being used in the likelihood, unless its estimation is explicitly included in a larger model.

shot noise arises from fluctuations in discrete transfer events. For equal charges q arriving independently with a constant Poisson rate λ, the charge collected during τ is Q=qN, with
E(N)=Var(N)=λτ,I=qλ,Var(Q/τ)=τqI​.
(54)
Thus the relative counting uncertainty is 1/λτ​. Longer integration improves the precision of the mean current even when the microscopic arrivals remain irregular.
In the frequency range where independent arrivals can be treated as instantaneous pulses, the one-sided current power spectral density is
SI(1)​(f)=2qIA2Hz−1​.
(55)
For an ideal unit-gain rectangular bandwidth B, this gives irms​=2qIB​. boxcar averaging of Poisson shot noise over duration τ has effective noise bandwidth Bn​=1/(2τ), reproducing the counting result irms​=qI/τ​. The two-sided spectral convention instead assigns qI to each sign of frequency. Stating that convention prevents a factor-of-two error.
A photodiode illuminated at a stable rate provides a useful measurement: subtract the mean current, amplify its fluctuations through a calibrated linear filter, and compare the variance with the mean photocurrent. Independent detected photoelectrons predict a variance proportional to mean current. Measure the dark and amplifier backgrounds separately, check the filter's effective noise bandwidth and vary illumination to distinguish this term from classical intensity noise, whose variance can scale quadratically with mean current. The shot noise model concerns counting statistics, whereas thermal electrical noise can exist in an unbiased resistor. Walter Schottky's motivating analysis treated amplifier limits and independent electron transfers in thermionic discharge tubes.[ref]
The Poisson result is a model, not a universal consequence of charge quantization. Correlated arrivals, detector dead time, avalanche multiplication or a non-Poisson light source can change the fluctuations. Filtering reduces integrated noise at the cost of temporal resolution. Increasing the detected event rate reduces relative shot noise but raises its absolute variance; balanced photodetection cancels shared classical fluctuations, not the independent shot noise of two uncorrelated detectors.
