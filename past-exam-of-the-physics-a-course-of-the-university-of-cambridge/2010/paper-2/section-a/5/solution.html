A small constant input can repeatedly produce the same analogue-to-digital converter code, so averaging those codes cannot reveal its position within a conversion interval. dither makes adjacent codes toggle; their relative frequencies then encode changes smaller than one conversion step. With adequate oversampling, low-pass filtering and averaging reduce the added, sufficiently uncorrelated noise while retaining the slow signal.
For independent sample errors, the averaged standard deviation scales as N−1/2; ideally four times as many samples gain about one effective bit. For example a constant input 0.2Δ rounded to levels separated by Δ would always return zero. Adding uniform dither on [−Δ/2,Δ/2] gives output Δ with probability 0.2 and zero otherwise: the expected average is 0.2Δ, with error 0.4Δ/N​. The benefit concerns the filtered estimate, not a more accurate individual conversion. Excessive dither wastes range, and averaging does not correct calibration bias, correlated drift or arbitrary converter nonlinearity.[ref]
