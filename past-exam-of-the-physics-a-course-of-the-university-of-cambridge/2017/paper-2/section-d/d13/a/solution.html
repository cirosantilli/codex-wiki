The Debye model treats a solid's low-frequency lattice vibrations as acoustic phonons in an elastic continuum. There are three polarizations per wavevector, one longitudinal and two transverse, with approximately linear dispersion relations. A cutoff is chosen to preserve the total 3N vibrational modes of N atoms. The phonon number itself is not fixed; mode occupations follow the Bose-Einstein distribution with zero chemical potential.
For one polarization of speed vs​, counting wavevector states in a spherical shell gives gs​(ω)=Vω2/(2π2vs3​). Define an average Debye speed by 3/vD3​=1/vL3​+2/vT3​. The total density of states can then be written
g(ω)=ωD3​9Nω2​,0≤ω≤ωD​,∫0ωD​​g(ω)dω=3N.
(69)
The Debye wavevector is kD​=(6π2N/V)1/3, the Debye frequency is ωD​=vD​kD​, and the Debye temperature is ΘD​=ℏωD​/kB​.
Omitting the temperature-independent zero-point energy, the vibrational energy and resulting Debye heat capacity are
U=∫0ωD​​eℏω/(kB​T)−1g(ω)ℏω​dω,CV​=9NkB​(ΘD​T​)3∫0ΘD​/T​(ex−1)2x4ex​dx​.
(70)
At low temperature the upper limit can be extended to infinity. The integral is 4π4/15, giving
CV​≃512π4​NkB​(ΘD​T​)3​,T≪ΘD​.
(71)
Only the long-wavelength acoustic modes are appreciably populated, explaining the Debye T-cubed test. At high temperature every mode contributes kB​ by equipartition theorem, so CV​→3NkB​​, the Dulong-Petit law.
The model succeeds especially at low temperature, where long-wavelength elasticity is reliable. It replaces real anisotropic branches by a simple continuum and cannot reproduce detailed optical phonon branches or every intermediate-temperature feature. Metals also have an electronic heat capacity approximately proportional to T; defects, magnetic excitations or a phase transition can add other terms. Experiments often measure heat capacity at constant pressure, which need not equal the harmonic model's CV​ exactly.
