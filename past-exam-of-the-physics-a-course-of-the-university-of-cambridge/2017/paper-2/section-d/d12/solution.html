Circular apertures and circular obstructions illustrate both the usefulness and the limits of geometric shadow arguments. In scalar diffraction theory, each point of a transmitted wavefront contributes a complex secondary field. Their coherent sum, described by the Huygens-Fresnel principle, determines the intensity. Circular symmetry simplifies the integral, but the answer depends on whether propagation is in the Fraunhofer diffraction or Fresnel diffraction regime.
For aperture radius a and axial propagation distance z, the Fresnel number NF​=a2/(λz) measures the importance of quadratic phase across the aperture. The far-field condition is NF​≪1; a lens can produce the same angular spectrum in its back focal plane. Fresnel propagation retains that quadratic phase. For an axial point source a distance s before the object and an observer a distance r after it, the phase uses the effective distance zeff​=sr/(s+r), because both incident and outgoing spherical paths contribute curvature. Scalar, paraxial propagation and an opening large compared with wavelength are assumed throughout.

Larger apertures narrow the angular pattern; a lens of focal length f maps the first dark ring to radius 1.22λf/D in its focal plane.
In the Fresnel regime, the axial intensity oscillates as aperture size or distance changes. Fresnel half-period zones have boundaries at path excesses mλ/2 and approximately opposite phases in successive zones. For a plane wave, their radii obey rm2​=mλz. Complementary holes and opaque disks are related at the field level. Babinet's principle states Ua​+Ud​=U0​ for complementary aperture and disk transmissions under the same illumination. In the Fraunhofer region away from the direct transmitted beam, the background field is absent and the diffracted fields are opposite in sign. Their scattered intensity profiles therefore have the same Airy angular dependence. Exactly in the forward direction the unobstructed field must be retained; subtracting intensities is incorrect. Finite incident beams also give a finite-width direct beam rather than an ideal angular delta function.

The ideal circular disk has an Arago spot at the centre of its geometric shadow. Circular edge contributions reach the axial point with the same phase. This does not mean the whole shadow is illuminated: off-axis cancellation produces the surrounding shadow and rings, and the axial spot becomes narrower for larger obstruction or shorter propagation distance. An aperture's axial intensity oscillates, while its complementary disk's axial intensity stays constant in this ideal paraxial model.
A practical observation uses a compact coherent source, an accurately circular hole or disk, and an aligned screen or camera. Varying the propagation distance or aperture radius reveals the transition between regimes. A point source, or spatial filtering, prevents different source positions from washing out the fine structure. Finite source size, incomplete coherence, edge roughness, misalignment, vector effects for wavelength-sized objects and lens aberrations reduce the ideal contrast. These experimental limitations explain why a geometrically unexpected axial bright point requires a suitable setup even though its scalar derivation is simple.
