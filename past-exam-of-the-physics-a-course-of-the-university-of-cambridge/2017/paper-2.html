Wavepacket derivation of group velocity
Words: 97
Solution OurBigBook.com
Superpose two nearby harmonic waves. The identity
cos(k1​x−ω1​t)+cos(k2​x−ω2​t)=2cos(2Δkx−Δωt​)cos(kˉx−ωˉt)
(23)
separates a rapid carrier from a slow beat envelope. A constant point of the envelope moves at Δω/Δk, which tends to
vg​=dkdω​​
(24)
as the components approach one another. Equivalently, expand a narrow wave packet spectrum around k0​: ω(k)=ω0​+vg​(k−k0​)+⋯. Factoring the carrier out of its Fourier transform representation leaves an envelope depending on x−vg​t. This interpretation requires a narrow spectrum and little attenuation or distortion; higher derivatives of the dispersion relation can change the envelope shape. The carrier phase velocity is vp​=ω/k and need not equal vg​.

Transit and pulse evolution
Words: 169
Solution OurBigBook.com
Taking L=1.00cm as the distance travelled in the medium, the narrowband group delay is
tg​=L/vg​=8.76×10−11s=87.6ps​.
(30)
If 1cm denotes a slab thickness measured normal to the interface instead, the central ray travels L/cos17.80∘, giving about 92.1ps. The coordinate-mixed velocity above would give 89.3ps for a 1cm path; it should not replace the consistent calculation silently.
The pulse envelope travels at the local group velocity, while individual carrier crests move at the phase velocity. In general, variation of group velocity across the pulse spectrum produces different delays, temporal broadening and chirp. Absorption can reshape the spectrum and envelope as well. The refraction data also show wavelength-dependent angles, so a finite-width incident beam acquires spatial separation of its spectral components. The supplied first derivative determines the central delay but does not specify a pulse duration, absorption spectrum or reliable second derivative of the dispersion relation; it therefore does not determine a numerical broadening, or its sign. If propagation phase is effectively linear across a sufficiently narrow spectrum, the leading approximation is a delayed pulse with unchanged shape.

Convolution and a finite slit array
Words: 157
Solution OurBigBook.com
Use Fourier transform convention f​(q)=∫f(x)e−iqxdx. The convolution theorem gives
f∗g​=f​g​​,fg​=2π1​f​∗g​​.
(31)
A finite array of very narrow slits at x=jD, j=0,…,N−1, has transmission proportional to ∑j​δ(x−jD). It can be constructed by multiplying an infinite Dirac comb by a finite window. The infinite comb transforms to a reciprocal comb; the finite window convolves each reciprocal peak with a narrow sinc-shaped amplitude. Thus increasing the illuminated length narrows each Fraunhofer diffraction peak.
The exact finite sum makes the argument quantitative:
t(q)∝∑j=0N−1​e−iqjD=e−iq(N−1)D/2sin(qD/2)sin(NqD/2)​,q=λ2π​sinθ.
(32)
Its normalized intensity is [sin(NqD/2)/(Nsin(qD/2))]2. Principal maxima occur at qD=2πm; their first zeros are displaced by ∣Δ(qD)∣=2π/N. Replacing the ideal narrow slit by a finite slit function convolves the array in real space, multiplying this field by the single-slit Fourier transform. Field amplitudes are transformed and combined before their squared modulus is taken.

Its internal round-trip geometric length is 2d/cosθ, but the external path to a common outgoing wavefront must also be included. The lateral separation is 2dtanθ, whose projection along the external ray is 2dtanθsinθi​. The net optical path difference is therefore
Δℓ=cosθ2nd​−2dtanθsinθi​=cosθ2nd(1−sin2θ)​=2ndcosθ,
(40)

(41)

Nonuniform oil film in monochromatic light
Words: 139
Solution OurBigBook.com
At normal incidence, the propagation phase is 4πnoil​d(x,y)/λ. Different film thicknesses therefore produce bright and dark contours of equal thickness, with adjacent same-type fringes differing by Δd=λ/(2noil​)​. Under monochromatic illumination these are intensity variations of the incident colour, rather than a rainbow.
For the usual case noil​>nwater​>1, reflection at air-oil reverses phase while reflection at oil-water does not. The additional relative π makes reflected minima occur at 2noil​d=mλ and maxima at 2noil​d=(m+1/2)λ. If the oil index were below the water index, the phase-reversal pattern would change and these conditions would interchange. The thinnest regions need not be completely dark because the two reflected amplitudes need not match; at vanishing thickness the residual reflection approaches that of the air-water boundary. Surface motion changes the thickness contours and moves the fringes.

Physical meaning of a phonon
Words: 55
Solution OurBigBook.com
A phonon is one quantum of a crystal's vibrational normal modes. In the harmonic approximation, each mode is a quantum harmonic oscillator with energy levels (n+1/2)ℏω. A phonon carries excitation energy ℏω and crystal momentum ℏq modulo a reciprocal lattice vector. It is a collective lattice excitation, not one atom travelling along the chain. Phonons obey Bose-Einstein statistics; their number need not be conserved in thermal equilibrium.

Acoustic and optical branches
Words: 96
Solution OurBigBook.com
The minus solution is the acoustic phonon branch. As q→0, ω−​ tends to zero and U=V: adjacent atoms move together in a long-wavelength compression. The plus solution is the optical phonon branch, with nonzero zone-centre frequency and mA​U+mB​V=0: the two sublattices move against one another with fixed cell centre of mass. For oppositely charged ions, this relative displacement can couple to an electric field, motivating the optical name.
At the zone boundary the frequencies are 2α/mheavy​​ and 2α/mlight​​. Different masses produce a gap; equal masses make the two branches meet, recovering a folded monatomic dispersion.

Semiconductor bands and efficient light emission
Words: 182
Solution OurBigBook.com
A semiconductor has a filled valence band and an empty conduction band at zero temperature in the intrinsic idealisation, separated by a modest band gap. Thermal excitation or deliberately introduced impurities can create mobile electrons and electron holes. Its conductivity is consequently very sensitive to temperature and doping. A metal has available electronic states at the occupied energy boundary, through a partly filled band or overlapping bands, and need not thermally excite carriers across a gap.
For a direct band gap, the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum occur at the same crystal momentum. An electron can recombine with a hole near these extrema and emit a photon while conserving crystal momentum, since optical photon momentum is small on the lattice scale. For an indirect band gap, the extrema lie at different crystal momenta. A band-edge radiative transition then ordinarily needs a phonon as well, making it less probable and allowing nonradiative processes to compete more effectively.
gallium arsenide has a direct fundamental gap, while silicon has an indirect one. This makes efficient band-edge emission and hence a light-emitting diode easier to obtain from gallium arsenide. It does not mean silicon has no optical transitions or that every direct-gap sample emits efficiently: defects, surfaces and carrier confinement also matter.
Doping and the equilibrium junction sketch
Words: 172
Solution OurBigBook.com
Semiconductor doping introduces impurities with levels close to the band edges. A donor impurity can release an electron to the conduction band, leaving a fixed positive ion; an n-type semiconductor has electrons as majority carriers. An acceptor impurity can take an electron from the valence band, leaving a mobile hole and fixed negative ion; a p-type semiconductor has holes as majority carriers. The opposite species remains as a minority carrier population, and a neutral bulk sample balances the ionised dopants with mobile carriers.
At a p-n junction, concentration gradients initially drive electrons and holes across the join. Their removal near the join exposes negative acceptor ions on the p side and positive donor ions on the n side, forming a depletion region. Its electric field opposes further majority-carrier diffusion. Equilibrium has a flat Fermi level, no net current and spatially bent band edges. With p on the left and n on the right, the electron band edges fall towards the n side; the difference is the equilibrium barrier in electron energy. A flat Fermi level does not mean flat conduction and valence edges.

Circular apertures and circular obstructions illustrate both the usefulness and the limits of geometric shadow arguments. In scalar diffraction theory, each point of a transmitted wavefront contributes a complex secondary field. Their coherent sum, described by the Huygens-Fresnel principle, determines the intensity. Circular symmetry simplifies the integral, but the answer depends on whether propagation is in the Fraunhofer diffraction or Fresnel diffraction regime.
For aperture radius a and axial propagation distance z, the Fresnel number NF​=a2/(λz) measures the importance of quadratic phase across the aperture. The far-field condition is NF​≪1; a lens can produce the same angular spectrum in its back focal plane. Fresnel propagation retains that quadratic phase. For an axial point source a distance s before the object and an observer a distance r after it, the phase uses the effective distance zeff​=sr/(s+r), because both incident and outgoing spherical paths contribute curvature. Scalar, paraxial propagation and an opening large compared with wavelength are assumed throughout.
A circular hole in the far field gives an Airy pattern. For uniform plane illumination the field is the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the aperture. In polar coordinates, let Q=ksinθ; the angular integral gives the Bessel function J0​:

Larger apertures narrow the angular pattern; a lens of focal length f maps the first dark ring to radius 1.22λf/D in its focal plane.

A circular opening can therefore expose alternating constructive and cancelling zone contributions. Complementary holes and opaque disks are related at the field level. Babinet's principle states Ua​+Ud​=U0​ for complementary aperture and disk transmissions under the same illumination. In the Fraunhofer region away from the direct transmitted beam, the background field is absent and the diffracted fields are opposite in sign. Finite incident beams also give a finite-width direct beam rather than an ideal angular delta function.

This does not mean the whole shadow is illuminated: off-axis cancellation produces the surrounding shadow and rings, and the axial spot becomes narrower for larger obstruction or shorter propagation distance. An aperture's axial intensity oscillates, while its complementary disk's axial intensity stays constant in this ideal paraxial model.
A practical observation uses a compact coherent source, an accurately circular hole or disk, and an aligned screen or camera. Varying the propagation distance or aperture radius reveals the transition between regimes. A point source, or spatial filtering, prevents different source positions from washing out the fine structure. Finite source size, incomplete coherence, edge roughness, misalignment, vector effects for wavelength-sized objects and lens aberrations reduce the ideal contrast. The Debye model treats a solid's low-frequency lattice vibrations as acoustic phonons in an elastic continuum. There are three polarizations per wavevector, one longitudinal and two transverse, with approximately linear dispersion relations. A cutoff is chosen to preserve the total 3N vibrational modes of N atoms. The phonon number itself is not fixed; mode occupations follow the Bose-Einstein distribution with zero chemical potential.
For one polarization of speed vs​, counting wavevector states in a spherical shell gives gs​(ω)=Vω2/(2π2vs3​). Define an average Debye speed by 3/vD3​=1/vL3​+2/vT3​. The total density of states can then be written
g(ω)=ωD3​9Nω2​,0≤ω≤ωD​,∫0ωD​​g(ω)dω=3N.
(69)
The Debye wavevector is kD​=(6π2N/V)1/3, the Debye frequency is ωD​=vD​kD​, and the Debye temperature is ΘD​=ℏωD​/kB​.
Omitting the temperature-independent zero-point energy, the vibrational energy and resulting Debye heat capacity are

(70)
At low temperature the upper limit can be extended to infinity. The integral is 4π4/15, giving
CV​≃512π4​NkB​(ΘD​T​)3​,T≪ΘD​.
(71)
Only the long-wavelength acoustic modes are appreciably populated, explaining the Debye T-cubed test. At high temperature every mode contributes kB​ by equipartition theorem, so CV​→3NkB​​, the Dulong-Petit law.
The model succeeds especially at low temperature, where long-wavelength elasticity is reliable. It replaces real anisotropic branches by a simple continuum and cannot reproduce detailed optical phonon branches or every intermediate-temperature feature. Metals also have an electronic heat capacity approximately proportional to T; defects, magnetic excitations or a phase transition can add other terms. Experiments often measure heat capacity at constant pressure, which need not equal the harmonic model's CV​ exactly.

In the scalar relaxation-time approximation, a conduction electron in an electric field reaches vd​=−eτE/m∗. Thus
μe​=m∗eτ​​,σ=neμe​=m∗ne2τ​​.
(74)
For a crystal with different directional curvatures, conductivity is a tensor and involves the occupied states and their scattering. Contributions from electrons and holes add: σ=e(nμe​+pμh​) in an isotropic two-carrier description. A filled band contributes no ordinary longitudinal current because opposite group velocities cancel; a partially filled band or holes permit a current response.
Effective mass therefore connects measured electrical transport to electronic band structure, but carrier density and momentum relaxation time matter as much as curvature. Ionised impurities and phonons alter τ. A smaller mass does not guarantee a higher conductivity if carrier population or scattering changes. Nor are all reported masses identical: density-of-states, cyclotron and conductivity masses weight anisotropic bands differently. The simple semiclassical formula assumes an isolated band, slowly varying weak fields and negligible interband transitions; near degeneracies or strong fields a single constant scalar mass is inadequate.

For a Bravais lattice with primitive translation vectors a1​,a2​,a3​, define the reciprocal lattice by vectors G satisfying eiG⋅R=1 for every direct-lattice translation R. A convenient basis is
b1​=2πa1​⋅(a2​×a3​)a2​×a3​​​
(75)
with cyclic formulas for b2​,b3​. Then ai​⋅bj​=2πδij​ and G=hb1​+kb2​+lb3​ for integers h,k,l. The reciprocal primitive volume is (2π)3/Vcell​.
These vectors are the spatial Fourier series frequencies of a periodic lattice potential or density. Elastic coherent crystal diffraction occurs when the scattering wavevector transfer equals a reciprocal lattice vector, with intensities additionally controlled by the basis crystal lattice structure factor. This is the reciprocal-space version of Bragg's law. A lattice of points and its basis should not be confused: a basis can make allowed reciprocal-lattice diffraction intensities vanish.
The first Brillouin zone is the Wigner-Seitz cell around the reciprocal origin: it consists of points closer to G=0 than to any other reciprocal point. Its faces lie on the perpendicular bisectors
k⋅G=∣G∣2/2​.
(76)
To see the condition, equate ∣k∣2 and ∣k−G∣2. These are also free-electron Bragg-degeneracy planes, where a periodic potential can open a band gap. Higher zones classify regions beyond successive Bragg planes; the reduced-zone description folds them into the first zone while retaining a band label.
In one dimension with spacing a, reciprocal points occur at 2πj/a, and the first zone is [−π/a,π/a]. A square lattice of spacing a has reciprocal basis 2πx^/a,2πy^​/a, and a square first zone with edges at kx​,ky​=±π/a.
The reciprocal of a face-centered cubic lattice is a body-centered cubic lattice and vice versa, with the scale fixed by the primitive-vector definition. In a Bloch state, wavevectors differing by G describe equivalent crystal momenta modulo the lattice periodicity. A crystal with N primitive cells has N allowed wavevectors in one Brillouin zone, hence N states in each band per spin. This state count links reciprocal geometry to band filling, metallic conduction and band insulating behaviour. Reciprocal space also labels phonons and makes conservation of crystal momentum modulo ℏG transparent.
