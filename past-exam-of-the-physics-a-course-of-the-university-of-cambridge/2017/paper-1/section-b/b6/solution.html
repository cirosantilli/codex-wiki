Equation and boundary conditions. The delta potential has strength V0​ with units of energy times length, because the Dirac delta function has units of inverse length. The time-independent Schrödinger equation is
−2mℏ2​ψ′′(x)−V0​δ(x)ψ(x)=Eψ(x).
(14)
The wavefunction is continuous at the origin. A finite jump in ψ would produce a derivative of a Dirac delta function in ψ′′, with no corresponding term in this potential. A jump in ψ′ does produce a delta contribution and is allowed. Integrate through a symmetric interval:
−2mℏ2​[ψ′(ϵ)−ψ′(−ϵ)]−V0​ψ(0)=E∫−ϵϵ​ψ(x)dx.
(15)
For continuous finite ψ, the right-hand side vanishes as ϵ→0. Consequently
ψ′(0+)−ψ′(0−)=−ℏ22mV0​​ψ(0).​
(16)
This derivative jump is the boundary condition associated with the attractive delta potential.
Unique bound state. For E<0, write κ=−2mE​/ℏ>0. Away from the origin, ψ′′=κ2ψ. Normalizability excludes exponentials growing at either infinity, leaving
ψ(x)={Aeκx,Be−κx,​x<0,x>0.​
(17)
Continuity gives A=B=C. The derivative jump then becomes −2κC=−2mV0​C/ℏ2. For a nonzero state,
κ=ℏ2mV0​​,E=−2mℏ2κ2​=−2ℏ2mV02​​.​
(18)
There is exactly one positive value of κ, so exactly one negative-energy bound state. An odd solution would have ψ(0)=0; the allowed decaying exponentials would then both vanish identically. Normalize the even solution using
1=2∣C∣2∫0∞​e−2κxdx=κ∣C∣2​.
(19)
Choosing the overall phase real and positive gives the bound state in a delta potential:
ψ0​(x)=κ​e−κ∣x∣,κ=ℏ2mV0​​.​
(20)
It peaks at the origin, has a downward cusp there, and decays exponentially on both sides. Its value, but not its slope, is continuous.
Scattering amplitudes and fluxes. For E>0, put k=2mE​/ℏ and retain κ=mV0​/ℏ2 as a strength parameter. A unit-amplitude wave incident from the left has
ψL​=eikx+re−ikx,ψR​=teikx.
(21)
Continuity gives t=1+r. The jump condition gives
ikt−ik(1−r)=−2κt,ikr=−κ(1+r).
(22)
Solving,
r=−κ+ikκ​,t=κ+ikik​.
(23)
The probability current of a plane wave of amplitude A is ℏk∣A∣2/m in its propagation direction. Since the asymptotic wavenumber is the same on both sides, reflection probability and transmission probability are just ∣r∣2 and ∣t∣2:
R=k2+κ2κ2​=2E+mV02​/ℏ2mV02​/ℏ2​,T=k2+κ2k2​=2E+mV02​/ℏ22E​.​
(24)
They satisfy R+T=1, expressing conserved probability current in this real potential.
The right-hand plot uses η=κ/k=V0​m/(2E)​/ℏ, proportional to V0​ at fixed incident energy. Then R=η2/(1+η2) rises monotonically from zero to one, while T=1/(1+η2) falls from one to zero; they are equal at η=1. Despite the absence of a classical forbidden barrier, an abrupt attractive interaction reflects a quantum wave. Increasing attraction strengthens the derivative mismatch and can approach complete reflection at fixed positive energy. At fixed V0​, increasing incident energy instead makes the well increasingly transparent. The negative-energy bound state and the positive-energy scattering states are different stationary solutions, not an absorptive loss channel.
