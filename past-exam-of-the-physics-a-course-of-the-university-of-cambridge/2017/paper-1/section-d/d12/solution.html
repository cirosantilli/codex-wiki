Ideal spectrum. Write Pi​(f)=C/(f2+fc2​), with C>0. The overdamped harmonic-trap power spectrum is a Lorentzian line shape:
Pi​≃fc2​C​(f≪fc​),Pi​≃f2C​(f≫fc​).
(75)
Thus its log-log plot is horizontal at low frequency and approaches slope −2 at high frequency, changing around fc​. The left panel below compares two characteristic frequencies with the same illustrative C; a common numerator is explicitly assumed for this sketch. Their high-frequency tails coincide and their low-frequency plateaux differ as fc−2​.
The right panel plots all nine original data points, including the very-low-frequency excess and the highest-frequency suppression. It also shows the ideal curve inferred only from the requested intermediate range.
Graphical corner estimate. Between roughly 1 and 100Hz the measured power spectral density is approximately the plateau P0​=10−6V2Hz−1. At 104 and 105Hz,
f2Pm​(f)≃1V2Hz,
(76)
so the descending asymptote is C/f2 with C≃1V2Hz. The graphical corner frequency of a Lorentzian spectrum is where these asymptotes intersect:
fc​=P0​C​​≃103Hz.​
(77)
Independently, the measured value Pm​(1000Hz)=5×10−7V2Hz−1=P0​/2 is exactly the half-plateau condition Pi​(fc​)=P0​/2. Both graphical readings agree. The inferred ideal curve is approximately
Pi​(f)=1+(f/1000Hz)210−6V2Hz−1​.
(78)
Low-frequency excess and recording duration. The two lowest-frequency measurements increase approximately as 1/f, whereas the ideal trapped oscillator tends to a constant plateau. This resembles flicker noise or slow apparatus drift superposed on the motion. The transimpedance amplifier, detector or environment could contribute; the spectrum alone does not identify the source uniquely. It would be incorrect to attribute that whole excess to the ideal Brownian oscillator.
A finite record of duration T has ordinary Fourier frequency resolution of order 1/T. Resolving the lowest tabulated frequency 0.01Hz therefore requires at least
Tmin​≃0.01Hz1​=100s.​
(79)
This is a formal lower bound corresponding to about one cycle; a reliable noise-spectrum estimate normally needs a substantially longer record and averaging.
Sampling. The largest reported frequency is fmax​=106Hz. The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem therefore gives the Nyquist lower bound
fs​≥2fmax​=2.0×106sampless−1.​
(80)
In practice choose a rate above 2MSs−1, with an analogue anti-aliasing filter suppressing unmeasured content above half the sampling rate. Exact equality cannot recover an arbitrary pure sinusoid precisely at the boundary for every phase; it is the limiting lower-bound answer rather than a comfortable practical sampling design.
High-frequency departure and amplifier design. Extrapolating the fitted f−2 tail to 106Hz predicts approximately 10−12V2Hz−1, while the measured value is 10−15V2Hz−1. The observed drop from 105 to 106Hz has effective logarithmic slope −5, much steeper than the ideal −2.
A detector or amplifier bandwidth limit, or intentional filtering before digitization, can explain such suppression. For a linear filter the spectral density transformation under a linear filter is Pout​=∣H∣2Pin​, so the last point corresponds approximately to an additional relative voltage gain 10−3​=0.0316, if attributed entirely to the readout transfer function. One additional point does not determine a unique filter order or identify the circuit responsible.
Rolling off an amplifier's gain beyond the useful signal band reduces integrated high-frequency noise, suppresses interference, and helps prevent aliasing in the analogue-to-digital converter. Limiting high-frequency loop gain can also improve feedback stability and suppress unwanted oscillation. The cutoff must retain the frequencies needed for the measurement; excessive filtering would distort the desired fluctuation spectrum itself.
