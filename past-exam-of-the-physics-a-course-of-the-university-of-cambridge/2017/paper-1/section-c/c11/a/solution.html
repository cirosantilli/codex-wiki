The Bohr model combines classical circular motion in the Coulomb potential with explicitly quantum postulates. A one-electron atom of nuclear charge +Ze has electron–nucleus reduced mass μ. With C=Ze2/(4πε0​), the relative circular orbit obeys
rμv2​=r2C​,L=μvr.
(61)
Bohr postulates permitted nonradiating stationary orbits with L=nℏ, for n=1,2,…. These assumptions give
rn​=μCn2ℏ2​=μZe24πε0​ℏ2​n2.​
(62)
For hydrogen with the nucleus taken infinitely heavy, the n=1 radius is the Bohr radius, a0​≃5.29×10−11m. The finite reduced mass gives the small nuclear-motion correction, and increasing Z contracts the one-electron orbit.
The circular-motion equation gives T=C/(2r) while the potential energy is −C/r, so the total energy is −C/(2r). Substituting the quantized radius yields
En​=−2ℏ2n2μC2​=−2(4πε0​)2ℏ2n2μZ2e4​.​
(63)
For the heavy-nucleus hydrogen approximation, En​≃−13.6eV/n2. The ionization threshold is zero energy, so removing the ground-state electron requires about 13.6eV.
The model further postulates that radiation is emitted or absorbed only in transitions between stationary orbits, with photon energy hν=∣Ei​−Ef​∣. Emission from ni​>nf​ gives
λ1​=Rμ​Z2(nf2​1​−ni2​1​),Rμ​=8ε02​h3cμe4​.
(64)
Thus it reproduces the principal hydrogen spectral series, their limiting wavelengths, and the gross hydrogenic energy levels. For example, n=3→2 in hydrogen has ΔE≃1.89eV and λ≃656nm, a visible line. Absorption reverses the transition when the photon matches the energy gap.
The achievement is that quantized energies explain discrete spectra, but the model's stationary orbits are postulates rather than consequences of classical electrodynamics: an accelerating classical charge would radiate. It does not provide a general calculation of line intensities or transition selection rules and fails as a quantitative theory of many-electron atoms. Spin, fine structure and the full Zeeman effect require further physics. Modern quantum mechanics replaces definite electron orbits by wavefunctions and quantum probabilities. In particular, the true hydrogen ground state has ℓ=0, so its orbital angular momentum vanishes; the model's rule L=nℏ should not be identified with the modern orbital eigenvalue rule L2=ℓ(ℓ+1)ℏ2. Bohr's energy quantization remains a successful hydrogenic approximation, while its literal orbital picture does not.
