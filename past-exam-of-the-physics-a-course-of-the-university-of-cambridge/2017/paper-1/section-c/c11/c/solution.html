A Hermitian operator satisfies the adjoint symmetry condition
⟨ϕ∣Aψ⟩=⟨Aϕ∣ψ⟩
(69)
for vectors in its specified domain. For a finite matrix this means A=A†. For an unbounded differential operator, boundary conditions and domains matter: symmetry on a chosen domain is not alone sufficient for self-adjointness. Physical observables require the corresponding self-adjoint operator and its spectral measure. This distinction prevents purely formal integration by parts from being mistaken for a complete operator definition.
Real eigenvalues. If A∣a⟩=a∣a⟩ with nonzero norm, Hermiticity implies
a⟨a∣a⟩=⟨a∣A∣a⟩=⟨a∣A∣a⟩∗=a∗⟨a∣a⟩,
(70)
so a=a∗. Thus eigenvalues representing measurement outcomes are real.
Orthogonality. For eigenvectors ∣a⟩,∣b⟩,
a⟨b∣a⟩=⟨b∣A∣a⟩=⟨Ab∣a⟩=b⟨b∣a⟩.
(71)
Hence (a−b)⟨b∣a⟩=0, and distinct eigenvalues have orthogonal eigenvectors. Within a degenerate eigenspace, Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization can choose an orthonormal basis. In finite dimensions the spectral theorem gives a complete orthonormal eigenbasis and
A=a∑​aPa​,I=a∑​Pa​,​
(72)
where Pa​ projects onto each eigenspace. In infinite dimensions a continuous spectrum may require a spectral integral rather than a normalizable eigenvector for every possible outcome; a position operator on the full line is a familiar example.
Measurements and probabilities. In the discrete case the Born rule assigns probability ⟨ψ∣Pa​∣ψ⟩ to the outcome a. For a nondegenerate eigenbasis, ∣ψ⟩=∑a​ca​∣a⟩ gives probabilities ∣ca​∣2, with
⟨A⟩=∑a​a∣ca​∣2∈R,(ΔA)2=⟨(A−⟨A⟩)2⟩≥0.
(73)
The variance vanishes precisely when the state lies in an eigenspace with a single sharp outcome. A repeatable ideal projective measurement prepares the corresponding eigenspace; superpositions of different eigenvalues do not have a preassigned sharp value of that observable in this description.
Compatibility and dynamics. Commuting Hermitian matrices admit simultaneous diagonalization, permitting a common set of sharp labels. For example L2,Lz​ commute, whereas Lx​,Ly​ do not. In the differential-operator setting the corresponding spectral compatibility requires appropriate domain conditions. Noncommuting observables obey the Robertson uncertainty principle
ΔAΔB≥21​∣⟨[A,B]⟩∣.​
(74)
The canonical commutation relation then gives ΔxΔp≥ℏ/2.
The Hamiltonian operator represents energy. A self-adjoint H generates unitary time evolution, U(t)=e−iHt/ℏ, preserving norm and total probability; its eigenvalues determine stationary-state phases and transition energies. Hermiticity supplies real outcomes and orthogonal eigenspaces, while self-adjointness and the spectral theorem make these measurement and evolution rules mathematically complete.
