The original diagram places hard walls at 0 and 3L, and the central plateau on L<x<2L is exactly at the indicated ground-state energy. This equality determines the middle of the requested sketch. In the time-independent Schrödinger equation,
ψ′′=ℏ22m​(V−E)ψ,
(1)
the central interval has V=E, hence ψ′′=0. The ground state can be chosen real and positive inside the walls by the nodeless theorem for a one-dimensional ground state. Reflection symmetry makes it even about x=3L/2, so its linear central segment must be constant.
The hard-wall boundary conditions are ψ(0)=ψ(3L)=0. On both outer intervals V=0<E, so the positive wavefunction has negative curvature and is sinusoidal. Wavefunction matching at a finite potential step requires both ψ and ψ′ to remain continuous at L,2L; the derivative therefore vanishes where each sinusoidal flank meets the flat middle. A normalized representative is
ψ0​(x)=2L​1​⎩⎨⎧​sin(2Lπx​),1,sin(2Lπ(3L−x)​),0,​0<x<L,L≤x≤2L,2L<x<3L,x≤0 or x≥3L.​​
(2)
Each side contributes L/2 and the plateau contributes L to the squared norm before multiplying by 1/(2L). The corresponding consistency condition is E=π2ℏ2/(8mL2); a numerical energy is not required for the sketch.
Thus the ground state at the top of a central rectangular barrier has no internal nodes, continuous value and slope, and a flat middle, rather than an exponential dip through a classically forbidden region. Multiplication by an overall constant phase changes none of these physical features.

Crucially, material and student exhibit complete confounding: each student measured only one sample. An experimenter-dependent systematic error could mimic the difference. Operators, quantization and rotations. Angular momentum is the generator of spatial rotations. In quantum mechanics, its components are Hermitian operators on their appropriate domains and obey
[Ji​,Jj​]=iℏϵijk​Jk​,[J2,Ji​]=0.
(55)
These angular momentum commutation relations prevent all three components from having simultaneous sharp values. For example, the Robertson uncertainty principle gives ΔJx​ΔJy​≥ℏ∣⟨Jz​⟩∣/2. One conventionally diagonalizes J2 and a selected component Jz​:
J2∣j,m⟩=ℏ2j(j+1)∣j,m⟩,Jz​∣j,m⟩=ℏm∣j,m⟩.​
(56)
The allowed labels are j=0,21​,1,23​,… and m=−j,−j+1,…,j. An angular-momentum multiplet has 2j+1 states. The magnitude ℏj(j+1)​ differs from the largest possible component ℏj; the remaining components fluctuate even in a state with maximal m.
The angular momentum lowering operator and its raising counterpart, J±​=Jx​±iJy​, satisfy
J±​∣j,m⟩=ℏj(j+1)−m(m±1)​∣j,m±1⟩.
(57)
The ladder terminates at m=±j. A rotation through angle θ about unit vector n acts through the unitary operator exp[−iθn⋅J/ℏ]. Thus quantized components and the continuous rotation of a state are compatible.
Orbital angular momentum. The orbital contribution is associated with motion in space:
L=r×p​=−iℏr×∇.
(58)
For a spinless spatial wavefunction, the simultaneous angular eigenfunctions are the spherical harmonics Yℓm​(θ,ϕ), with ℓ=0,1,2,… and m=−ℓ,…,ℓ. Single-valued ordinary scalar spatial wavefunctions give integer orbital labels. In a central potential energy, [H,L2]=[H,Lz​]=0, so stationary states may be labelled by these angular quantum numbers. The angular kinetic contribution appears in the radial equation as ℓ(ℓ+1)ℏ2/(2mr2), the centrifugal potential. Noncentral interactions need not conserve these orbital labels.
Intrinsic spin and addition. Spin angular momentum is an intrinsic internal degree of freedom with the same commutation algebra. It is not the angular momentum of a small classical charged sphere literally rotating about its centre. For an electron, s=1/2 and ms​=±1/2, so Si​=(ℏ/2)σi​ in terms of the Pauli matrices. Its squared spin is 3ℏ2/4. Other particles can have integer or half-integer spin, including spin zero.
For one particle with orbital and spin degrees of freedom, J=L+S; the two component sets commute with each other. Addition of angular momentum permits j=∣ℓ−s∣,∣ℓ−s∣+1,…,ℓ+s. The coupled states ∣j,mj​⟩ are linear combinations of the uncoupled ∣ℓ,mℓ​⟩∣s,ms​⟩ with mj​=mℓ​+ms​, determined by Clebsch-Gordan coefficients. A rotationally invariant spin-orbit interaction can conserve J2,Jz​ while mixing the separate component labels.
Angular momentum also carries a magnetic moment. For an electron's orbital motion and intrinsic spin,
μL​=−2me​e​L,μS​=−gs​2me​e​S,gs​≃2.
(59)
The minus signs reflect negative electric charge. With the Bohr magneton μB​=eℏ/(2me​), a spin projection ms​ has μz​=−gs​μB​ms​. A magnetic field can therefore distinguish angular-momentum states through their interaction energy −μ⋅B.
Stern–Gerlach measurement. In the Stern-Gerlach experiment, atoms emerge from a heated source, pass through collimating apertures, traverse a strongly inhomogeneous magnetic field, and strike a position-sensitive screen. The beam is neutral, so its separation is interpreted through the atomic magnetic moment rather than the ordinary charged-particle Lorentz force. Near a chosen quantization direction, the interaction gives
U=−μz​Bz​,Fz​≃μz​∂z∂Bz​​.
(60)
A uniform field can exert a torque but would not separate the beam by this translational force; the gradient is essential. Different allowed magnetic-moment components become correlated with different trajectories. A classical ensemble with continuously varying orientations would yield a continuous spread, whereas discrete quantum components yield separated channels.
In the modern electronic description of ground-state silver, filled shells cancel their electronic angular momenta and the unpaired 5s electron has L=0, S=1/2, J=1/2. The effective electronic measurement therefore has two components, approximately μz​=±μB​, rather than an orbital ℓ=1/2, which is not allowed. For positive field gradient the electronic mJ​=+1/2 component has negative μz​ and deflects downward in this coordinate convention. Nuclear spin and hyperfine structure are neglected in this simplified two-channel account. Historically the original splitting preceded the electron-spin interpretation, so the modern explanation should not be confused with the original investigators' interpretation.
More generally a prepared spin state a∣+z⟩+b∣−z⟩ gives the two outcomes with probabilities ∣a∣2,∣b∣2. Selecting one channel prepares a state for further measurements. An ideal second analyser along the same axis then gives a definite result, whereas one along a perpendicular axis gives two equally likely outcomes for a spin-one-half eigenstate. This connects the apparatus directly to incompatible spin components and the Born rule. Angular momentum is quantized through its operator algebra; the Stern–Gerlach gradient turns that discrete internal information into spatially distinguishable outcomes.

The Bohr model combines classical circular motion in the Coulomb potential with explicitly quantum postulates. A one-electron atom of nuclear charge +Ze has electron–nucleus reduced mass μ. With C=Ze2/(4πε0​), the relative circular orbit obeys
rμv2​=r2C​,L=μvr.
(61)
Bohr postulates permitted nonradiating stationary orbits with L=nℏ, for n=1,2,…. These assumptions give
rn​=μCn2ℏ2​=μZe24πε0​ℏ2​n2.​
(62)
For hydrogen with the nucleus taken infinitely heavy, the n=1 radius is the Bohr radius, a0​≃5.29×10−11m. The finite reduced mass gives the small nuclear-motion correction, and increasing Z contracts the one-electron orbit.
The circular-motion equation gives T=C/(2r) while the potential energy is −C/r, so the total energy is −C/(2r). Substituting the quantized radius yields
En​=−2ℏ2n2μC2​=−2(4πε0​)2ℏ2n2μZ2e4​.​
(63)
For the heavy-nucleus hydrogen approximation, En​≃−13.6eV/n2. The ionization threshold is zero energy, so removing the ground-state electron requires about 13.6eV.
The model further postulates that radiation is emitted or absorbed only in transitions between stationary orbits, with photon energy hν=∣Ei​−Ef​∣. Emission from ni​>nf​ gives
λ1​=Rμ​Z2(nf2​1​−ni2​1​),Rμ​=8ε02​h3cμe4​.
(64)
Thus it reproduces the principal hydrogen spectral series, their limiting wavelengths, and the gross hydrogenic energy levels. For example, n=3→2 in hydrogen has ΔE≃1.89eV and λ≃656nm, a visible line. Absorption reverses the transition when the photon matches the energy gap.
The achievement is that quantized energies explain discrete spectra, but the model's stationary orbits are postulates rather than consequences of classical electrodynamics: an accelerating classical charge would radiate. It does not provide a general calculation of line intensities or transition selection rules and fails as a quantitative theory of many-electron atoms. Spin, fine structure and the full Zeeman effect require further physics. Modern quantum mechanics replaces definite electron orbits by wavefunctions and quantum probabilities. In particular, the true hydrogen ground state has ℓ=0, so its orbital angular momentum vanishes; the model's rule L=nℏ should not be identified with the modern orbital eigenvalue rule L2=ℓ(ℓ+1)ℏ2. Bohr's energy quantization remains a successful hydrogenic approximation, while its literal orbital picture does not.

Ehrenfest theorem relates the dynamics of quantum expectation values to operator commutators. For a normalized state obeying iℏ∣ψ˙​⟩=H∣ψ⟩ and a possibly time-dependent observable A, differentiate ⟨A⟩=⟨ψ∣A∣ψ⟩. Using the adjoint Schrödinger equation for the bra gives
dtd​⟨A⟩​=ℏi​⟨HA⟩−ℏi​⟨AH⟩+⟨∂t∂A​⟩=iℏ1​⟨[A,H]⟩+⟨∂t∂A​⟩.​
(65)
Thus
dtd​⟨A⟩=iℏ1​⟨[A,H]⟩+⟨∂t​A⟩.​
(66)
The identity assumes a common domain on which the required products and derivatives are defined.
For H=p2/(2m)+V(x), the canonical commutation relation gives [x,H]=iℏp/m and [p,H]=−iℏV′(x). Therefore
dtd​⟨x⟩=m⟨p⟩​,dtd​⟨p⟩=−⟨V′(x)⟩.​
(67)
These resemble Hamilton's equations and Newton's second law, but generally ⟨V′(x)⟩=V′(⟨x⟩). If the state is localized about x=⟨x⟩, a local expansion gives
⟨V′(x)⟩=V′(x)+21​V(3)(x)⟨(x−x)2⟩+⋯.
(68)
The classical trajectory equation for the mean is accurate when the force changes little across the wave packet. For a quadratic harmonic potential, V′ is linear and this closure is exact for every state with the relevant finite moments: md2⟨x⟩/dt2=−mω2⟨x⟩. This exact classical evolution of the centroid does not eliminate its quantum position uncertainty or make every state a point particle.
The correspondence principle is broader: quantum predictions recover classical ones in an appropriate limit, such as large action compared with ℏ and observations that cannot resolve individual quantum levels. It is not the assertion that every large quantum number automatically makes every possible state classical. Delocalized superpositions and interference can remain significant unless the physical preparation and measurement justify a classical description.
A spectral example is useful. For a quantum harmonic oscillator, neighboring levels differ by ℏω, giving transition frequency ω/(2π), equal to its classical oscillation frequency. A coherent state makes the connection more concrete: its mean position and momentum oscillate classically while the finite-width Gaussian profile retains its quantum uncertainty. For hydrogenic Bohr levels En​=−A/n2, transitions between nearby large n have ΔE/ℏ≃2A/(ℏn3), which equals the classical circular orbital angular frequency in that model. Ehrenfest's theorem supplies an exact expectation-value bridge; a classical trajectory follows only when the force can also be evaluated at the mean position.

A Hermitian operator satisfies the adjoint symmetry condition
⟨ϕ∣Aψ⟩=⟨Aϕ∣ψ⟩
(69)
for vectors in its specified domain. For a finite matrix this means A=A†. For an unbounded differential operator, boundary conditions and domains matter: symmetry on a chosen domain is not alone sufficient for self-adjointness. Physical observables require the corresponding self-adjoint operator and its spectral measure. This distinction prevents purely formal integration by parts from being mistaken for a complete operator definition.
Real eigenvalues. If A∣a⟩=a∣a⟩ with nonzero norm, Hermiticity implies
a⟨a∣a⟩=⟨a∣A∣a⟩=⟨a∣A∣a⟩∗=a∗⟨a∣a⟩,
(70)
so a=a∗. Thus eigenvalues representing measurement outcomes are real.
Orthogonality. For eigenvectors ∣a⟩,∣b⟩,
a⟨b∣a⟩=⟨b∣A∣a⟩=⟨Ab∣a⟩=b⟨b∣a⟩.
(71)
Hence (a−b)⟨b∣a⟩=0, and distinct eigenvalues have orthogonal eigenvectors. Within a degenerate eigenspace, Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization can choose an orthonormal basis. In finite dimensions the spectral theorem gives a complete orthonormal eigenbasis and
A=a∑​aPa​,I=a∑​Pa​,​
(72)
where Pa​ projects onto each eigenspace. In infinite dimensions a continuous spectrum may require a spectral integral rather than a normalizable eigenvector for every possible outcome; a position operator on the full line is a familiar example.
Measurements and probabilities. In the discrete case the Born rule assigns probability ⟨ψ∣Pa​∣ψ⟩ to the outcome a. For a nondegenerate eigenbasis, ∣ψ⟩=∑a​ca​∣a⟩ gives probabilities ∣ca​∣2, with
⟨A⟩=∑a​a∣ca​∣2∈R,(ΔA)2=⟨(A−⟨A⟩)2⟩≥0.
(73)
The variance vanishes precisely when the state lies in an eigenspace with a single sharp outcome. A repeatable ideal projective measurement prepares the corresponding eigenspace; superpositions of different eigenvalues do not have a preassigned sharp value of that observable in this description.
Compatibility and dynamics. Commuting Hermitian matrices admit simultaneous diagonalization, permitting a common set of sharp labels. For example L2,Lz​ commute, whereas Lx​,Ly​ do not. In the differential-operator setting the corresponding spectral compatibility requires appropriate domain conditions. Noncommuting observables obey the Robertson uncertainty principle
ΔAΔB≥21​∣⟨[A,B]⟩∣.​
(74)
The canonical commutation relation then gives ΔxΔp≥ℏ/2.
The Hamiltonian operator represents energy. A self-adjoint H generates unitary time evolution, U(t)=e−iHt/ℏ, preserving norm and total probability; its eigenvalues determine stationary-state phases and transition energies. Hermiticity supplies real outcomes and orthogonal eigenspaces, while self-adjointness and the spectral theorem make these measurement and evolution rules mathematically complete.

Electrical noise reduction starts by identifying what is unwanted in the measured signal and where it enters. Increasing gain amplifies both signal and input noise; it does not by itself improve the input signal-to-noise ratio. Separate intrinsic component fluctuations, amplifier noise and external pickup before choosing a remedy.
Physical sources. A resistor at temperature T has classical one-sided open-circuit thermal electrical noise density SV​=4kB​TR per hertz. In a flat measurement bandwidth B, its rms voltage is 4kB​TRB​. Lowering an appropriate resistance, reducing temperature or narrowing the useful bandwidth can reduce this noise, with loading and signal-gain tradeoffs considered. These are power spectral densities; an amplitude spectral density is their square root.
Discrete independent charge transfers give shot noise, with one-sided current density SI​=2eI in the simple Poisson process model. Reducing leakage or unwanted bias current can help, but reducing a photocurrent may also reduce the desired signal. Charge correlations can change the simple shot-noise law. At low frequency, flicker noise and drift often dominate and are not described by a white spectrum.
Amplifier and impedance choice. An operational amplifier has equivalent input voltage and current noise. In a simplified uncorrelated model, a resistive source contributes the input-referred voltage density
SV,eq​≃en2​+4kB​TRs​+in2​∣Zs​∣2.
(81)
Here en​,in​ are the voltage and current amplitude spectral densities and Zs​ is the source electrical impedance; feedback components add further terms in a full circuit. Low current noise matters particularly for a high-impedance source, while voltage noise can dominate for a low-impedance source. Minimize needless source impedance, leakage and long sensitive connections without sacrificing the required transduction gain or bandwidth. Bias-current offsets and thermal drift also require calibration; they are not removed simply by quoting a random-noise density.
Pickup and bandwidth. Shield sensitive circuits against electric-field coupling, minimize loop area to reduce changing magnetic-flux pickup, and use twisted signal/return pairs or suitable coaxial connections. Differential readout rejects common-mode interference when impedances and amplifier responses are adequately matched. Plan the signal returns to avoid unwanted circulating ground currents, and decouple supply rails locally so supply fluctuations do not enter the signal path. Mechanical vibration, cable motion and temperature changes may be converted into electrical noise as well.
A low-pass filter removes out-of-band noise before digitization; an anti-aliasing filter is needed because digital filtering cannot undo already aliased content. Avoid a narrower passband than the physical signal requires. For a known periodic signal, modulation and a lock-in amplifier select a narrow frequency interval, often moving the measurement above a strong flicker noise region. A notch can reject a narrow interference line if that does not remove essential signal information.
Independent noise contributions add in variance, not directly in rms amplitude. Averaging N independent equal-variance readings reduces the standard error by N−1/2, but slow correlated drift and systematic errors need separate controls. Good noise reduction preserves the information-bearing signal while reducing unwanted coupling, intrinsic fluctuations or the admitted noise bandwidth.

In this digital-sampling context, Nyquist's theorem means the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem. A continuous signal with no Fourier content outside ∣f∣≤B can be reconstructed from uniformly spaced samples taken sufficiently fast. The familiar practical condition is
fs​>2B,Ts​=fs​1​,fN​=2fs​​.​
(82)
Here fN​ is the Nyquist frequency, while 2B is the Nyquist rate required by that signal bandwidth. A finite-energy bandlimited formulation allows equality; a rule intended to include arbitrary pure sinusoids at the boundary must be strict, because sin(πfs​t) vanishes at every sampling time t=n/fs​.
The mechanism is visible in the Fourier transform. Sampling by a time comb creates shifted copies of the original spectrum, separated by fs​:
xs​(t)=∑n​x(nTs​)δ(t−nTs​),Xs​(f)=fs​∑k​X(f−kfs​).
(83)
If those copies do not overlap, an ideal reconstruction filter selects one copy and recovers the original waveform. Equivalently, ideal interpolation uses the normalized sinc function:
x(t)=n=−∞∑∞​x(nTs​)sinc(Ts​t−nTs​​),sincu=πusinπu​.​
(84)
Each interpolation function is one at its own sample and zero at all other sample times. Exact reconstruction presumes the bandwidth condition, an ideal sampling clock and the full sample sequence.
If the spectrum extends beyond half the sampling rate, different continuous frequencies become indistinguishable: this is aliasing. A sinusoid at frequency f is indistinguishable on the sampling grid from frequencies differing by integer multiples of fs​, with the usual sign reflection for real signals. For example, 10kHz sampled at 8kSs−1 aliases to 2kHz; a later digital filter cannot tell which signal generated those samples.
Use an analogue anti-aliasing filter before the analogue-to-digital converter and choose a sampling rate leaving room for its finite transition band. Faster sampling can relax that analogue filtering problem, but simply increasing the number of samples in a fixed-duration record does not improve the independent Fourier-bin spacing beyond approximately 1/T. A long recording is needed for low-frequency resolution, while a small sampling interval is needed for high-frequency coverage. Finite records also introduce windowing and spectral leakage.
Digitization adds amplitude quantization as well as time sampling. An ideal N-bit converter spanning voltage range Vspan​ has step Δ=Vspan​/2N. When quantization error may be modeled as independent and uniform across one step, its rms size is Δ/12​; small coherent signals need not obey that white-noise approximation. Sampling rate controls temporal ambiguity, recording duration controls frequency resolution, and converter resolution controls amplitude discretization.

A Gaussian distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ has probability density
p(x)=2π​σ1​exp[−2σ2(x−μ)2​].​
(85)
Its symmetry, finite variance and simple behavior under linear combinations make it useful for repeated measurements. Roughly 68.3%, 95.4% and 99.7% of observations lie within one, two and three true standard deviations of the mean. These are coverage probabilities for a specified distribution, not guarantees that every experimental error is normal.
Why it appears. The central limit theorem explains why many sums of small independent finite-variance contributions approach a Gaussian law after centering and scaling, provided no single contribution dominates. Detector noise, small mechanical disturbances and multiple electronic fluctuations can combine approximately this way. The theorem does not remove a common calibration bias, and it does not justify assuming normal tails for rare large disturbances or arbitrarily correlated errors. A large-mean Poisson distribution also approaches a Gaussian with variance equal to its mean, making normal approximations useful for high-count photon or particle data; low counts require the discrete model.
A classical particle at thermal equilibrium in a one-dimensional harmonic potential has a Gaussian position distribution directly from the Boltzmann distribution:
p(x)∝e−kx2/(2kB​T),⟨x2⟩=kkB​T​.
(86)
Thus a Gaussian law can arise from the energy dependence of equilibrium probability, rather than only from an informal appeal to many independent errors.
Precision of repeated measurements. For N independent observations with the same mean and variance, the sample mean has variance σ2/N. For Gaussian observations this mean is exactly Gaussian:
σx​=N​σ​.​
(87)
Consequently quadrupling the number of independent readings halves the random standard error. When the true variance is unknown, a small-sample confidence interval for a normal mean uses a Student t-distribution with N−1 statistical degrees of freedom; replacing that by a normal quantile can overstate precision. Shared systematic errors and correlations do not decrease by the same averaging rule.
Combining results and fitting models. Independent Gaussian estimates xi​ of one quantity, with known variances σi2​, combine through the inverse-variance weighted mean
μ​=∑i​1/σi2​∑i​xi​/σi2​​,Var(μ​)=∑i​1/σi2​1​.
(88)
For a model yi​=gi​(θ)+ϵi​ with independent zero-mean Gaussian noise, maximizing the likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the weighted least-squares statistic
χ2(θ)=∑i​σi2​[yi​−gi​(θ)]2​.
(89)
For a correct linear model with known independent Gaussian errors and p fitted parameters of full rank, the minimized statistic has a chi-squared distribution with N−p degrees of freedom. Nonlinear fits only inherit that familiar approximation under additional regularity and adequate-data assumptions. Known correlated Gaussian errors require the full inverse covariance matrix in the quadratic form.
These properties support confidence intervals, consistency checks, calibration and error propagation. They also give practical diagnostics: inspect residuals for trends, changing spread, correlations or outliers before trusting a Gaussian fit. Gaussian models connect physical fluctuations to quantitative uncertainty, but their assumptions must be checked rather than inferred from the shape of a convenient bell curve.
