The Sturm oscillation theorem gives three nodes of a wavefunction for the fourth bound state. reflection symmetry and nondegeneracy of one-dimensional bound states make it odd: one node of a wavefunction is at the centre, and the other two lie at opposite positions. In each flat allowed region the wavefunction is a sinusoid, with k=2m(E−V)​/ℏ; its wavelength is shortest in the central, deepest region. Outside the well it decays exponentially, with opposite signs on the two sides. Both Ψ and Ψ′ remain continuous at every finite step, as required by wavefunction matching at a finite potential step; only the curvature jumps. In particular, the wavefunction need not vanish at a step.
This original wavefunction matching at a finite potential step illustration uses freely chosen well dimensions and energies. It shows the required parity, node count, wavelength ordering and decaying tails; the source drawing supplies no numerical scale from which to fix exact node positions.

C10 OurBigBook.com
identical particles have the same intrinsic properties. Exchanging their bookkeeping labels cannot distinguish a physical preparation or change a measurable prediction. This is stronger than merely failing to track classical trajectories. For ordinary bosons and fermions, the many-particle wavefunction has respectively bosonic exchange symmetry or fermionic exchange symmetry under every particle exchange operator, acting on spatial and spin coordinates together. The Spin-statistics theorem connects integer spin to bosons and half-integer spin to fermions; this connection uses relativistic quantum-field assumptions, rather than following from the nonrelativistic Schrödinger equation alone.
For one-particle states u,v satisfying orthonormality, the two alternatives form a quantum superposition:
Ψ±​(1,2)=2​u(1)v(2)±v(1)u(2)​.
(22)
Its probability density contains an exchange interference term, absent from an incoherent mixture of labelled alternatives. The antisymmetric expression vanishes when both one-particle states are identical: one complete one-particle state can contain at most one fermion. This Pauli exclusion principle explains electron shell filling, the electronic Fermi sea and degeneracy pressure. Opposite spins are different one-particle states, so two electrons may share an atomic orbital. Spatially separated wave packets with negligible overlap can often be treated as distinguishable for local measurements; the underlying exchange symmetry remains.
In thermal equilibrium, occupation of a level of energy ϵ obeys
nˉB​=e(ϵ−μ)/(kB​T)−11​,nˉF​=e(ϵ−μ)/(kB​T)+11​.
(23)
bosons can accumulate macroscopically in one state, giving Bose-Einstein condensation; fermions instead fill successive states up to the Fermi energy at zero temperature. When occupation is dilute, both expressions reduce to the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. The identical-particle classical partition function contains 1/N!, avoiding spurious entropy from permutations and the Gibbs paradox. Exchange effects become conspicuous when the thermal de Broglie wavelength is comparable to the interparticle spacing.
An operational test is the Hong-Ou-Mandel effect. Down-converted photon pairs enter separate ports of a balanced beam splitter; indistinguishable alternatives for one photon per output cancel. Scanning the relative arrival delay restores coincidences away from overlap. Matching polarization, spectral filtering and spatial alignment controls distinguishability; measuring delayed accidental coincidences controls background. The observed dip tests two-photon interference, and its finite depth must not automatically be interpreted as a failure of bosonic symmetry. The original apparatus varied beam-splitter position and subtracted a separately measured accidental background.[ref]

For observables A,B, nonzero [A,B] obstructs a complete common eigenbasis. In a finite-dimensional system, commuting Hermitian operators can be simultaneously diagonalized; quantum degeneracy may require choosing an appropriate basis inside the degenerate eigenspace. Noncommuting observables can still share particular eigenvectors, so incompatibility is not a claim that every individual state must have nonzero spreads in both quantities.
The Robertson uncertainty relation follows by applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to (A−⟨A⟩)∣ψ⟩ and (B−⟨B⟩)∣ψ⟩:
ΔAΔB≥21​∣⟨[A,B]⟩∣​.
(24)
In particular [x,px​]=iℏ gives ΔxΔpx​≥ℏ/2. These are preparation spreads, not necessarily measurement errors. A zero commutator expectation can make this bound zero even when the linear operators do not commute.
Measurement order can matter: preparing ∣+z⟩, measuring Sx​, then measuring Sz​ gives both z outcomes with probability 1/2 when the intermediate result is ignored. A direct z measurement would give +ℏ/2 certainly. Finally the Heisenberg equation of motion dA/dt=(i/ℏ)[H,A]+∂A/∂t relates commutation to conservation: an observable with no explicit time dependence and [H,A]=0 is conserved. For unbounded self-adjoint operators these statements require the appropriate common domains; a formal commutator calculation alone need not justify simultaneous spectral measurements.

b OurBigBook.com
In a classically forbidden region, the Schrödinger equation has evanescent solutions rather than forcing the wavefunction to vanish. Matching at both sides of a finite barrier permits nonzero transmission. For a broad barrier the WKB approximation gives
T≃Cexp[−2∫x1​x2​​ℏ2m(V(x)−E)​​dx],
(25)
where C is a matching prefactor. Transmission is exponentially sensitive to barrier width and height. A single decaying exponential function carries no probability current; the matched scattering solution includes the combination needed for transmitted current. energy is conserved throughout.
In alpha decay, a quasibound alpha particle penetrates the nuclear Coulomb barrier. Multiplying the penetration probability by an assault frequency estimates the decay rate; the strong dependence on alpha energy explains the wide range of lifetimes. In a scanning tunnelling microscope, a voltage drives electron quantum tunnelling between a conducting tip and sample. Approximately I∝Ve−2κd, so constant-current feedback resolves changes in gap width, with electronic density of states also affecting the image. Piezoelectric motion, vibration isolation and stable voltage are necessary; a bright feature is not automatically a taller atom. Atomic-scale imaging was demonstrated with a vacuum-tunnelling microscope in the original surface study.[ref] In a Josephson junction, Cooper pair quantum tunnelling through a weak link gives I=Ic​sinφ, allowing supercurrent without an applied voltage and phase-sensitive interference. These are distinct applications of barrier penetration and coupling with quantum coherence, not particles borrowing energy to climb over a barrier.

c OurBigBook.com
Addition of two spin-one-half angular momenta gives total spin S=0 or S=1. The normalized singlet state and triplet states are
∣0,0⟩=2​∣↑↓⟩−∣↓↑⟩​,∣1,1⟩=∣↑↑⟩,∣1,0⟩=2​∣↑↓⟩+∣↓↑⟩​,∣1,−1⟩=∣↓↓⟩.
(26)
They have S2 eigenvalues 0 and 2ℏ2, and Sz​=Mℏ. The singlet state is antisymmetric under a particle exchange operator, whereas all three triplets have symmetric spin-exchange parity. For two identical electrons the full state must be antisymmetric: a singlet state pairs with a spatial state with symmetric exchange parity, a triplet state with an antisymmetric spatial state. Thus two electrons in the same atomic orbital must form a singlet state.
The identity S1​⋅S2​=(S2−S12​−S22​)/2 gives −3ℏ2/4 in the singlet state and +ℏ2/4 in the triplet state. Hence an exchange interaction distinguishes their energies, with ordering determined by its coupling sign. The singlet state has perfect opposite-spin correlations along any common measurement axis and is entangled. The M=0 triplet state is also entangled, while the M=±1 triplet states are product states along the chosen axis.

Bayes' theorem updates a parameter distribution using measured data:
p(θ∣D)=∫p(D∣θ′)p(θ′)dθ′p(D∣θ)p(θ)​​.
(27)
The likelihood describes the measurement process, the prior distribution encodes information before the present data, and the denominator normalizes the posterior distribution. Include instrumental noise and known selection effects in the likelihood. nuisance parameters, such as an uncertain background, should be jointly inferred and marginalized, rather than silently fixed.
For n independent readings with a Gaussian distribution with known common variance σ2 and prior distribution θ∼N(μ0​,τ02​), completing the square gives
τn−2​=τ0−2​+nσ−2,μn​=τn2​(μ0​τ0−2​+nxˉσ−2).
(28)
The posterior distribution is N(μn​,τn2​): prior distribution and data contribute in proportion to precision. A broad prior distribution recovers the usual estimate xˉ with standard deviation σ/n​. Report a posterior distribution estimate and credible interval, specify the prior distribution, and examine sensitivity when the data are weak. A credible interval is a posterior distribution probability statement about the parameter conditional on the model; it is not defined by the repeated-sampling coverage of a confidence interval.

Uniform samples at tn​=n/fs​ cannot distinguish frequencies separated by integer multiples of fs​:
e2πi(f+kfs​)tn​=e2πiftn​,k∈Z.
(29)
For a real cosine, opposite frequencies also coincide. Thus an unknown high-frequency component can appear in the sampled record at its folded frequency falias​∈[0,fs​/2]. For example, 2 Hz and 8 Hz cosines give identical samples at 10 samples per second. This ambiguity is aliasing, rather than uniform quantization error.
The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem permits reconstruction of an ideally bandlimited function of bandwidth B when fs​>2B. Use an analogue anti-aliasing filter before conversion and allow a transition band between the desired passband and the frequencies that fold into it. Sampling faster relaxes the filter requirement. Digital filtering after undersampling cannot separate already identical sample sequences. Finite observation time, clock jitter, noise and imperfect filter rejection impose further practical limits.

c OurBigBook.com
Identify the physical coupling that causes a systematic error and change the apparatus so that it can be measured or cancelled. measurement calibration against known reference standards at several points reveals zero and gain errors; blanks and background runs measure additive contributions. Carry uncertainties of the standards and fitted corrections into the final result.
Reversal provides a particularly transparent test. If D+​=S+b and D−​=−S+b, the combination (D+​−D−​)/2 isolates S while (D+​+D−​)/2 measures the measurement zero offset. Interleave and repeat the reversals so drift can be detected. The cancellation assumes a stable measurement zero offset and a genuinely odd signal; a common gain error remains. null measurements and bridge comparisons can reduce dependence on detector gain. Swapping sensors or leads exposes asymmetry; checking alignment, shielding, grounding and temperature control tests specific unwanted couplings. Varying signal amplitude or measurement frequency can reveal heating, nonlinear response or pickup.
Use independent measurement principles where possible, and test the predicted response to each deliberate change. Repeated readings alone do not remove a fixed systematic error. Correct identified significant effects and retain uncertainty for imperfectly known corrections; complete elimination cannot generally be demonstrated. Type A and Type B classify how uncertainty is evaluated, rather than simply meaning random measurement errors and systematic errors.[ref]

D13 OurBigBook.com
An ideal operational amplifier draws no current at either input. With stable operational-amplifier negative feedback in its unsaturated linear range, infinite open-loop gain also gives a virtual short, V+​=V−​. The amplifier adjusts its output to enforce that equality; it is not a conducting connection between the inputs. Here V+​=0, so the ideal summing node is a virtual ground. Kirchhoff's current law gives Vin​/R1​=−Vout​/R2​, hence the ideal gain is −R2​/R1​.
For the finite-input-resistance inverting amplifier let the inverting-node voltage be v, and continue to neglect output impedance. The controlled output and node-current balance are
Vout​=−Av,R1​v−Vin​​+R2​v−Vout​​+Rin​v​=0.
(30)
Eliminating v gives
Vin​Vout​​=−1+A1​(1+R1​R2​​+Rin​R2​​)R2​/R1​​​.
(31)
Dividing the denominator by R2​ gives the requested resistance-sum form. The ideal result follows whenever ∣A∣≫1+R2​/R1​+R2​/Rin​; taking Rin​→∞ alone is insufficient if A is small. At finite frequency A and the gain are phasor ratios, subject to feedback stability.
With equal resistors and sufficiently large loop gain, Vout​≃−Vin​. This gives a controlled sign reversal or 180-degree phase inversion, useful for subtraction and opposite-phase signal paths, with amplitude set by a resistor ratio rather than uncertain open-loop gain. The source still sees approximately R1​, so this is not a high-input-impedance voltage follower. For the ideal-input, single-pole model, the noise gain of an operational amplifier is two and the closed-loop bandwidth is approximately half the gain-bandwidth product. Output swing, slew rate and load constraints must also permit linear operation.
