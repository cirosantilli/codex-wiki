Linear regression (Y=Xβ+ε)

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Statistical model Statistical modelling Normal linear model
OurBigBook.com Words: 5k Articles: 58
Linear regression models a response's conditional expectation as a linear combination of specified predictors. In matrix form,
Y=Xβ+ε,E(ε∣X)=0,
(1)
where the design matrix X describes the observations, β contains the regression coefficients, and ε contains the errors. The fitted values Xβ​ estimate the response means. The model is linear in its regression coefficients; its columns may include powers, interactions, or other transformed predictors, so polynomial regression can fit curved relationships using the same machinery.
For a concrete example, take five constructed observations
(xi​,yi​)=(0,1),(1,2),(2,2),(3,4),(4,5).
(2)
An ordinary least squares fit with an intercept gives the line y​=0.8+x. Its regression residuals are (0.2,0.2,−0.8,0.2,0.2) and its residual sum of squares is 0.8. A one-unit increase in the predictor corresponds to a one-unit increase in the fitted response. This is a description of this fitted association; interpreting a change as a causal effect requires the assumptions of a causal inference problem.
The geometric reason for the fit is simple. The possible mean vectors Xβ form the column space of X. Ordinary least squares chooses the orthogonal projection of the data vector onto that vector subspace, so the remaining regression residual is orthogonal to every permitted fitted direction. The least-squares normal equations express this orthogonality as XT(Y−Xβ​)=0. The data's explained component and unexplained component are therefore separated by the same geometry used in a Hilbert space.
The optimization definition makes sense for any observed data. Quantifying the uncertainty of the regression coefficients needs additional assumptions about the sampling and errors. With fixed X of full column rank and mean-zero errors with covariance matrix σ2I, ordinary least squares is unbiased and has covariance σ2(XTX)−1; the Gauss-Markov theorem compares it with other linear unbiased estimators. A normal linear model additionally makes Student t-distribution intervals and tests exact in finite samples. Model adequacy and predictive assessment remain relevant even when these calculations are available.
Two historical settings help explain the subject's appeal. Adrien-Marie Legendre's Nouvelles méthodes pour la détermination des orbites des comètes (1805) includes an appendix on the method of least squares: observations give more equations than unknowns, and squared errors provide a criterion for reconciling them. Its astronomical setting shows the motivation for combining imperfect measurements. [ref] Francis Galton's Regression Towards Mediocrity in Hereditary Stature (1886) examines the association between parental and offspring heights and the tendency of group averages toward the population average. This is a historical use of the regression name; the modern linear model is a general statistical formulation. [ref]
