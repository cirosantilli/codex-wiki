OurBigBook.com Words: 7k Articles: 362
Biochemistry studies the chemistry of living systems: what their molecules are, how they recognize one another, and how their chemical reactions support the organization of a biological cell. It connects biology to chemical kinetics, stoichiometry and thermodynamics. A useful biochemical explanation follows both material and energy: which substrate becomes which reaction product, what sets the reaction rate, and how the transformation is driven.
Consider three connected examples. The stereochemistry of a sugar helps determine how it interacts with an enzyme's active site; the molecular formula alone cannot describe that recognition. Selective enzymes then direct material through metabolism, while ATP can participate in energy coupling that drives otherwise unfavourable transformations. These are different questions about the same working system, rather than three names for its energy supply.[ref]
This viewpoint is useful because it separates possible explanations for a stopped process. Missing substrate, inactive enzyme, depleted ATP and accumulated reaction product call for different interventions. Studying a cell-free extract can isolate some of those causes while leaving a substantial chemical reaction network intact.

Dialysis (biochemistry)

Words: 1k Articles: 5
Biochemistry OurBigBook.com
Dialysis exchanges small solutes between a sample and an external bath through a semipermeable membrane, while retaining suitably large macromolecules, such as proteins. The transport is passive diffusion: random motion carries permeant molecules both ways, with net transport down their concentration gradient. The bath is the dialysate. At equal permeant solute concentrations, microscopic exchange continues but net exchange stops.[ref]
For example, a protein sample containing excess sodium chloride can lose the small ions to a low-salt dialysate without deliberately transferring the protein. Conversely, components of a new buffer solution can enter from the bath. This is useful before studying enzyme kinetics: changing the surrounding chemical solution can remove an interfering small solute while keeping the enzyme available for the next experiment.
The striking feature is that the retained protein does not have to be captured and released again to change its surroundings. However, retention is conditional on the semipermeable membrane and the particular macromolecule, and dialysis is poorly suited to separating proteins of nearly the same size. The molecular weight cut-off is a nominal retention rating, not a sharp geometrical boundary.

Dialysate

Words: 42
Dialysis (biochemistry) OurBigBook.com
The external chemical solution on the other side of a dialysis semipermeable membrane. Its composition supplies the boundary condition for exchange: it can receive unwanted small solutes or supply desired small solutes. A bath replacement discards the old dialysate and introduces a new reservoir with a specified composition.

Molecular weight cut-off

Words: 104
Dialysis (biochemistry) OurBigBook.com
The molecular weight cut-off rates retention of reference molecules. One manufacturer's definition uses the mass at which approximately 90% remains after seventeen-hour dialysis, tested with globular proteins. A 10kDa rating uses dalton units: a mass, not a length or an absolute passage threshold.[ref]
A semipermeable membrane has a distribution of passage sizes, and a molecule's shape affects passage as well as its mass. Near the nominal MWCO, slow passage can make substantial retention compatible with eventual leakage. The protein-retention experiment below makes retention an observable quantity rather than a universal yes-or-no property inferred from the label.

Buffer exchange

Words: 93
Dialysis (biochemistry) OurBigBook.com
Buffer exchange changes the small components surrounding a retained macromolecule toward those of a desired buffer solution. Components absent from fresh dialysate tend to leave; supplied components can enter. Each permeant solute has its own exchange problem.[ref]
For a dilute, nonbinding permeant solute, an ideal equilibrium calculation approaches the bath's specified concentration. That approximation need not describe a solute bound to a protein or a charged mixture with additional electrical constraints. Retaining a protein also does not by itself establish that its protein structure or catalytic function survives the change of buffer solution.

Consider an original ideal model with sample volume Vs​, bath volume Vb​, and initial sample solute concentration c0​. Assume each volume is fixed, a freely permeant, nonbinding solute, no chemical reactions, complete mixing on each side, and equal concentrations at equilibrium. Each fresh bath initially contains none of this solute. The protein is perfectly retained in this model.
The first equilibration redistributes the initial amount of substance c0​Vs​ through both compartments. Conservation gives
c0​Vs​=c1​(Vs​+Vb​),c1​=c0​Vs​+Vb​Vs​​.
(1)
This denominator includes the sample itself. For the chosen Vs​=1mL and Vb​=9mL, c1​/c0​=1/10, rather than 1/9. Nine-tenths of the initial solute is in the bath and one-tenth remains in the sample; nothing has vanished during equilibration.
Discarding the equilibrated bath removes its solute. A second fresh bath starts with only c1​Vs​ of solute in the entire system, so the same argument applies again. Induction gives the geometric progression
q=Vs​+Vb​Vs​​,cn​=qcn−1​=c0​qn,c0​cn​​=10−n(Vs​=1, Vb​=9).
(2)
Here n counts completed equilibrations with fresh baths: n=1 is the first bath, n=2 follows one replacement, and n=3 follows two replacements. The normalized sample concentrations are respectively 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001. Leaving the first bath in place for longer cannot lower the ideal equilibrium value below 0.1.
The same conservation argument handles a fresh bath already containing a permeant solute at fixed concentration b:
cn​=Vs​+Vb​Vs​cn−1​+Vb​b​,cn​−b=q(cn−1​−b),cn​=b+(c0​−b)qn.
(3)
Thus the sample approaches b, not necessarily zero. For example, if a hypothetical new buffer component starts at zero in the sample and at b in every bath, its sample concentration reaches 0.9b, 0.99b and 0.999b in the three equilibrations. Outward removal and inward replacement follow the same mathematics.
Equilibrium fractions do not specify elapsed time. An illustrative well-mixed linear ordinary differential equation model within one bath is
Vs​dtdcs​​=−K(cs​−cb​),Vb​dtdcb​​=K(cs​−cb​),K>0.
(4)
Here K is an effective transfer coefficient with units of volume per time. Adding the two equations conserves Vs​cs​+Vb​cb​; subtracting their normalized forms gives
dtd(cs​−cb​)​=−K(Vs​1​+Vb​1​)(cs​−cb​).
(5)
The difference undergoes exponential decay. A bath replacement resets cb​, renewing the driving difference without making cs​ jump instantly. The plotted points are equilibrium endpoints, not a measured time course or a timetable for an arbitrary protein.
Real sample volume can change through osmosis; proteins can be lost through leakage or adsorption to the semipermeable membrane. Consequently, a measured decrease in protein concentration alone cannot distinguish dilution from loss of protein. Measuring recovered volume as well as protein concentration permits an estimate of recovered protein mass.[ref]

Dialysis salt-removal and protein-retention experiments

Words: 271
Dialysis (biochemistry) OurBigBook.com

Glycolysis carbon and ATP ledger

Glycolysis cofactor recycling

Words: 376

In the payoff stage of glycolysis, oxidation of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate is coupled to reduction of NAD+. Each NADH carries a two-electron reducing equivalent. The formal half-chemical equation
NAD++H++2e−⟶NADH
(11)
makes the electron count explicit: two NADH per glucose carry four electrons. This is redox bookkeeping, not a claim that free electrons travel through the chemical solution or that NADH is itself ATP.
Fermentation can return NADH to NAD+ by reducing an organic substrate; reduction of pyruvate to lactate is one example. In aerobic cellular respiration, reducing equivalents reach an electron transport chain, reducing molecular oxygen. Other forms of cellular respiration use other electron acceptors, so absence of oxygen does not uniquely imply fermentation.[ref]
The need for NAD+ regeneration follows from a finite-pool argument. In a hypothetical closed preparation with 12μmol of initially oxidized NAD+ and no regeneration, at most 6μmol of glucose can complete the two NAD+-consuming conversions per glucose. This is an upper bound even with excess glucose, not a predicted stopping time: other limitations can stop the preparation earlier. With an intact fixed nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide pool and no side reactions, converting NAD+ into NADH preserves their sum but depletes the usable oxidized form.
ATP recycling imposes a second condition. Glycolysis needs some initial ATP for investment and consumes two net ADP and two inorganic phosphate per completed glucose. In a closed ideal preparation with only 8μmol of initial ADP, no other source of ADP, and enough initial ATP and inorganic phosphate, at most 4μmol of glucose can finish. This bound follows from the net ADP consumption, even though investment temporarily produces additional ADP. Initial investment also means that adequate final inventories alone cannot guarantee the route can start.
For continued operation at fixed carrier inventories, other chemical reactions must match the two net ATP formed per glucose with ATP use that restores ADP and inorganic phosphate, or exchange must supply those reactants. ATP hydrolysis coupled to cellular work is one route. Thus glycolysis can sustain useful energy coupling while neither its ATP inventory nor its NADH inventory grows indefinitely; matter cycles while glucose supplies fresh chemical free energy.

Three is a stipulated coupling count, not a universal ATP synthase stoichiometry; transport costs and leaks can also consume the available free energy.

Respiratory uncoupling

Words: 89
Oxidative phosphorylation OurBigBook.com
Respiratory uncoupling allows proton return to bypass ATP synthase, dissipating the electrochemical gradient. Electron transport and oxygen consumption can continue while less ATP is recovered.[ref]
In the thermodynamic example under chemiosmosis, reducing both the activity ratio to one and the electric potential difference to zero removes the driving contribution. The hypothetical ATP-forming conversion then costs +45kJmol−1. Continued donor oxidation would not restore coupling unless it rebuilt a usable electrochemical gradient. This distinction makes respiratory uncoupling useful conceptually: abundant oxygen consumption can coexist with poor recovery of ATP.

Substrate-level phosphorylation

Words: 92
Energy metabolism OurBigBook.com
Substrate-level phosphorylation forms ATP by enzyme-catalyzed transfer of a phosphate group from a phosphorylated substrate to ADP. In canonical glycolysis, two such transfers occur for each three-carbon route, giving four gross ATP per glucose. Unlike oxidative phosphorylation, these transfers do not require an electron transport chain.[ref]
The pyruvate kinase example under Coupled-enzyme readout of glycolytic ATP formation separates the material transfer from its detection. A favourable coupled chemical reaction supplies the driving change in Gibbs free energy; the enzyme supplies a catalytic route. Counting ATP-forming transfers gives a gross yield, while subtracting upstream ATP use gives the net yield.
