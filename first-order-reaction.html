First-order reaction

 ... Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Reaction rate Rate law Order of reaction
OurBigBook.com Words: 248 Articles: 1
A first-order reaction has a reaction rate proportional to one reactant concentration in the simple single-species case. For an irreversible A⟶B in a closed system at fixed volume, take [A](0)=A0​, [B](0)=0, and constant rate constant k>0. Its rate equation and solution are
dtd[A]​=−k[A],[A](t)=A0​e−kt,[B](t)=A0​(1−e−kt),v(t)=kA0​e−kt.
(41)
A short check is enough: differentiating A0​e−kt gives −kA0​e−kt, and the two concentrations sum to A0​, as required by the one-to-one stoichiometry. Thus the same exponential decay describes both remaining reactant and decreasing reaction rate. Fixed k means a fixed fractional loss per unit time, not a fixed absolute consumption rate.
Choose illustrative values A0​=1molL−1 and k=0.2s−1, with t measured in seconds. Then [A]=e−0.2tmolL−1, [B]=(1−e−0.2t)molL−1, and v=0.2e−0.2tmolL−1s−1. The half-life is
t1/2​=kln2​=0.2ln2​s≈3.4657s.
(42)
At each successive half-life, half the remaining reactant is consumed; t1/2​ is independent of A0​ for this first-order reaction. At t=10s, the calculated values are [A]≈0.135335molL−1, [B]≈0.864665molL−1, and v≈0.027067molL−1s−1. These are model predictions for chosen parameters, rather than experimental measurements.
The left panel tracks the two concentrations; the right tracks their instantaneous conversion reaction rate. Their different vertical units matter: v=k[A], so the numerical curves need not have the same height. This gives a practical way to translate a measured concentration into a forecast of both conversion and waiting time.
