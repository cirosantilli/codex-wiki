Group isomorphism (φ:G∼​H)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Morphism Isomorphism
OurBigBook.com Words: 228
A group isomorphism is a bijective group homomorphism φ:G→H. It matches the entire group operation: φ(ab)=φ(a)φ(b), with each product taken in its own group. Its inverse is automatically a group homomorphism. A group automorphism is the special case H=G.[ref]
The target is part of the claim. The doubling example under isomorphism is a group isomorphism d:Z→2Z, between the additive integers and the additive even integers. Exactly the same rule d(k)=2k, declared as d:Z→Z, is an injective endomorphism that misses 1 and every other odd integer, so is not a group automorphism. Changing the declared target changes surjectivity, even though it does not change the formula or its compatibility with addition.
An isomorphism lets a calculation in one model solve the same problem in another. The example under group identifies addition modulo four with function composition of rotations of a square via k↦rk. Through this group isomorphism, adding exponents corresponds exactly to composing motions. More generally, a group isomorphism preserves order of a group element, carries subgroups to subgroups, and carries a generating set of a group to a generating set of a group. Having equally many elements is insufficient: the permutation in group automorphism already fails compatibility on two inputs.
Why does the inverse preserve the group operation? Write u=φ(a) and v=φ(b), possible because of surjectivity. Then
φ−1(uv)=φ−1(φ(a)φ(b))=φ−1(φ(ab))=ab=φ−1(u)φ−1(v).
(8)
This proves the inverse condition directly. Analogous calculations work for each operation of a ring and for addition and scalar multiplication in a vector space. The argument relies on preserving those operations; it supplies no general continuity claim for the inverse of a continuous bijection between topological spaces.
