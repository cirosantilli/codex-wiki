Variance (Var(X))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Expected value
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 54
The variance measures the mean squared distance of a random variable from its expected value. For a real square-integrable random variable X,
var(X)=E[(X−EX)2]=E[X2]−(EX)2.
(1)
The square prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling. It also gives the variance squared units: if X measures a length in metres, its variance has units of square metres. Its nonnegative square root is the standard deviation, which has the same units as X.
For example, let X take −1, 0 and 1 with equal probabilities. Its expected value is zero, but its variance is [(−1)2+02+12]/3=2/3, and its standard deviation is 2/3​. The constant random variable zero has the same expected value and zero variance: a mean alone does not describe the spread. The variance is zero exactly when X=EX almost surely.
The variance is useful because squared deviations interact neatly with linear combinations. The covariance supplies the cross terms when combining different random variables, and a covariance matrix organizes those terms for many variables. The characterization of variance as the minimum mean squared error of a constant also explains why the expected value is the best constant prediction under squared error.

Conditional variance (Var(X∣G))

Words: 404 Articles: 1
Variance OurBigBook.com
The conditional variance measures the remaining squared uncertainty after specified information has been used to form a conditional mean. For a real square-integrable random variable X and a sub-sigma-algebra G, let m=E[X∣G]. Then
Var(X∣G)=E[(X−m)2∣G]=E[X2∣G]−m2.
(4)
It is a G-measurable random variable, nonnegative almost surely, rather than necessarily a single number. With the information G=σ(Y), its value is the variance in the group identified by Y. Its expected value is the mean squared error of predicting X by its conditional mean.[ref]
In the exact mixture distribution in law of total expectation, group A contains 0,4 around mean two, and group B contains 6,10 around mean eight. Both groups have conditional variance four because their deviations from their own means are −2,2 with equal probabilities. Therefore E[Var(X∣Y)]=4. Meanwhile, the two group means have variance 41​(2−13/2)2+43​(8−13/2)2=27/4. The law of total variance gives
Var(X)=4+427​=443​.​
(5)
Direct calculation gives E[X2]=53 and Var(X)=53−(13/2)2=43/4, checking the decomposition.
For any square-integrable G-measurable predictor Z, the error identity is
E[(X−Z)2]=E[Var(X∣G)]+E[(m−Z)2].
(6)
The cross term vanishes because E[X−m∣G]=0. Thus using the conditional mean minimizes mean squared error, while conditional variance quantifies what this information still cannot predict. This is the orthogonal projection interpretation in L2 space.[ref]

Average conditional variance decreases with more information (EVar(X∣H)−EVar(X∣G)=E[(mG​−mH​)2]≥0)

Conditional variance OurBigBook.com
For square-integrable X and nested information H⊆G, let mH​=E[X∣H] and mG​=E[X∣G]. The predictor mH​ is also G-measurable, so the conditional variance error identity gives
E[Var(X∣H)]−E[Var(X∣G)]=E[(mG​−mH​)2]≥0.
(7)
More information cannot increase the average best squared prediction error. Equality holds exactly when the two conditional means agree almost surely; additional information can change the conditional distribution without changing its mean.
This is an averaged statement. Take X=−4,0,4 with equal probabilities, and learn only whether X is zero. The unconditional variance is 32/3. In the nonzero group, the conditional variance is 16, which is larger; in the zero group it is zero. Their weighted average remains (2/3)16+(1/3)0=32/3. Both groups have conditional mean zero, so the extra information has not improved squared-error prediction, even though it has revealed whether the outcome is extreme or central. An individual group's conditional variance need not decrease.

Covariance (Cov(X,Y))

Words: 2k Articles: 42
Variance OurBigBook.com
The covariance measures the average product of two centered deviations. For real square-integrable random variables X and Y,
Cov(X,Y)=E[(X−EX)(Y−EY)]=E[XY]−EXEY.
(13)
Deviations with the same sign contribute positively; deviations with opposite signs contribute negatively. Their magnitudes and probabilities determine the net result. Taking Y=X recovers the variance. The definitions and algebraic properties are presented in Orloff and Bloom, MIT 18.05, Covariance and Correlation, sections 2–2.1.
For a worked example, let X,Z be independent random variables, each equally likely to be −1, 0 or 1, and put Y=2X+Z. All nine pairs (X,Z) have probability 1/9. The expected values are zero, while linearity of covariance and the vanishing covariance of independent random variables give
Var(X)=Var(Z)=32​,Cov(X,Y)=2Var(X)=34​,Var(Y)=4Var(X)+Var(Z)=310​.
(14)
The shared X term makes large X accompany large Y on average. Replacing 2X by −2X reverses the covariance to −4/3 without changing either variance.
The covariance is useful whenever deviations are added or subtracted. Expanding the square gives
Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y)+2Cov(X,Y),Var(X−Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y)−2Cov(X,Y).
(15)
For the worked model, these are 20/3 and 4/3. Positive covariance increases variation in the sum while reducing variation in the difference: the same shared fluctuation reinforces one combination and partly cancels in the other. The identity applies beyond independent random variables.
The size of a covariance depends on the measurement scale. For constants a,b,c,d, linearity of covariance gives Cov(aX+b,cY+d)=acCov(X,Y). Adding offsets has no effect; rescaling either measurement rescales the covariance. The Pearson correlation coefficient divides by both standard deviations to describe the standardized linear relationship.

(16)

Uncorrelated random variables

Words: 191 Articles: 1
Covariance OurBigBook.com
Two square-integrable random variables X,Y are uncorrelated random variables when Cov(X,Y)=0. Independent random variables with finite second moments are uncorrelated random variables, but zero covariance does not generally imply independence.
For an explicit counterexample, let X take −1, 0, 1 with equal probabilities, and let Y=X2. Then EX=EX3=0, so Cov(X,Y)=EX3−EXEX2=0. Nevertheless, Y is completely determined by X: P(Y=0∣X=0)=1, while P(Y=0)=1/3. Thus these are uncorrelated random variables with statistical dependence. In this example, opposite contributions from the odd function x3 cancel; the even function x2 still retains information about ∣X∣. The last panel of the covariance diagram shows the relationship.
There are useful special cases. Uncorrelated jointly normal variables are independent, and the Bernoulli independence from zero covariance result supplies another setting where the converse holds. Their distribution assumptions do the extra work; zero covariance alone does not.

Bernoulli independence from zero covariance (Cov(X,Y)=ad−bc)

Words: 65
Uncorrelated random variables OurBigBook.com
For two Bernoulli random variables with joint probabilities a,b,c,d in the order (0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1), their covariance is d−(c+d)(b+d)=ad−bc. Zero covariance fixes the (1,1) probability to the product of its marginals; subtracting this from each marginal fixes all three remaining joint probabilities to their products. Thus uncorrelated random variables taking only zero and one are independent, including degenerate marginals. The implication is special to this two-point setting and fails for general distributions.

Covariance matrix (Σ=E[(X−EX)(X−EX)T])

Words: 2k Articles: 31
Covariance OurBigBook.com
For a real random vector X=(X1​,…,Xp​)T with finite second moments, its covariance matrix collects all pairwise covariances:
Σ=E[(X−EX)(X−EX)T],Σij​=Cov(Xi​,Xj​).
(18)
The diagonal entries are variances. The covariance matrix is a symmetric matrix and a positive semidefinite matrix, because for every real vector a,
aTΣa=Var(aTX)≥0.
(19)
Thus one matrix describes the variance of every linear combination of the coordinates. It is a positive-definite matrix exactly when no nonzero linear combination is constant almost surely. A deterministic linear relation produces a zero eigenvalue.
For example,
Σ=(10.8​0.81​)
(20)
has unit coordinate variances, covariance 0.8, and eigenvalues 1.8 and 0.2. Its unit eigenvectors (1,1)T/2​ and (1,−1)T/2​ describe directions of greatest and least projected variance. For a unit vector a=(cosθ,sinθ)T, direct multiplication gives aTΣa=1+0.8sin(2θ): the variation in the sum direction is nine times that in the difference direction.
The spectral theorem supplies an orthonormal basis of these directions. This connects covariance matrices to principal component analysis: its leading eigenvector identifies the unit-direction projection with the largest variance. For a linear transformation A, the transformed random vector has covariance matrix AΣAT, so uncertainties propagate through a linear model by matrix multiplication.

Pearson correlation coefficient (ρX,Y​)

Words: 591 Articles: 7
Covariance matrix OurBigBook.com
The Pearson correlation coefficient is the covariance divided by the product of the standard deviations. For real random variables with finite, strictly positive variances,
ρX,Y​=Var(X)Var(Y)​Cov(X,Y)​.
(23)
It is the expected value of the product of the two centered variables after dividing each by its standard deviation. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives −1≤ρX,Y​≤1. This removes measurement units, while retaining the direction and strength of the linear relationship. Constant random variables have zero standard deviation, so their Pearson correlation coefficient is undefined.
For the covariance model Y=2X+Z, with X,Z independently uniform on {−1,0,1}, the calculation gives
ρX,Y​=(2/3)(10/3)​4/3​=5​2​≈0.894427.
(24)
The positive correlation is less than one because the independent Z term introduces extra variation. For Y=−2X+Z the correlation is −2/5​. Both examples appear in the covariance diagram.
For nonzero real a,c, the correlation of aX+b and cY+d is sgn(ac)ρX,Y​. Positive rescaling and added offsets therefore leave the correlation unchanged; reversing one scale reverses its sign. Equality ∣ρX,Y​∣=1 means that one random variable is an affine function of the other almost surely, with a nonzero slope of the corresponding sign. This is the equality condition in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and includes perfect positive correlation.
Zero correlation says that the centered variables are orthogonal in their square-integrable inner product. It permits nonlinear statistical dependence, as the example Y=X2 in uncorrelated random variables demonstrates. More generally, a correlation is a statement about a joint probability distribution; identifying effects of interventions is the separate task of causal inference. This distinction keeps an observed association from being mistaken for a demonstrated mechanism.
An early source for standardized association is Francis Galton's 1888 paper “Co-relations and their Measurement, chiefly from Anthropometric Data”. He compared stature and forearm measurements and changed each measurement to units of its own variability so the two regression directions could be compared. His scale used probable error, so it should not be identified word for word with the modern standard deviation formula above. [ref].
