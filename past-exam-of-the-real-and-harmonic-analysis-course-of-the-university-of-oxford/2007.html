The absolute continuity of measures condition is
ν≪κ⟺κ(E)=0⟹ν(E)=0(E∈M).
(1)
The Radon-Nikodym theorem says that if both positive measures are sigma-finite measures, then this condition is equivalent to the existence of a nonnegative measurable function h, unique up to equality κ-almost everywhere, such that
ν(E)=∫E​hdκ(E∈M).​
(2)
One writes h=dν/dκ, the Radon-Nikodym derivative. Under these hypotheses h is finite κ-almost everywhere. If ν is finite, then h∈L1(κ). A finite signed measure absolutely continuous with respect to a sigma-finite positive measure likewise has an integrable signed Radon-Nikodym derivative.
The sigma-finiteness hypothesis on the dominating measure cannot simply be omitted. On the Borel subsets of [0,1], let κ be counting measure and ν Lebesgue measure. Absolute continuity holds because the only κ-null set is empty. A Radon-Nikodym derivative would satisfy h(x)=ν({x})=0 at every point, contradicting ν([0,1])=1. Here counting measure is not sigma-finite.

For a signed measure define its total variation measure by
∣μ∣(E)=sup{∑r=1m​∣μ(Er​)∣:E=⨆r=1m​Er​, Er​∈M}.
(3)
The value at the empty set is zero. For disjoint measurable sets Aj​ with union A, any finite partition Er​ of A gives
∑r​∣μ(Er​)∣≤∑j​∑r​∣μ(Er​∩Aj​)∣≤∑j​∣μ∣(Aj​).
(4)
Taking the supremum proves one inequality. In the reverse direction choose, for the first k sets, finite partitions approaching their variation values. Combining these partitions and the remaining set A∖⋃j≤k​Aj​ gives
∣μ∣(A)≥∑j=1k​∣μ∣(Aj​)−ε.
(5)
If one term is infinite, its partition sums can instead be made arbitrarily large. Letting ε↓0 and k→∞ proves countable additivity, including infinite values. Thus ∣μ∣ is a positive measure. This uses only countable additivity of μ, without assuming a Hahn decomposition.

An outer measure on X is a function μ:P(X)→[0,∞] satisfying μ(∅)=0, monotonicity, and countable subadditivity:
μ(⋃j​Ej​)≤∑j​μ(Ej​).
(12)
Its measurable sets are those satisfying Caratheodory measurability criterion:
Mμ∗​={A⊆X:μ(E)=μ(E∩A)+μ(E∖A) for every E⊆X}.​
(13)
The test runs over all subsets E, including nonmeasurable ones.

b OurBigBook.com
The Caratheodory measurability criterion yields a complete sigma-algebra Mμ∗​, and μ restricted to it is a countably additive measure. Completeness means that every subset of a measurable null set is measurable.
For the requested algebra proof, X and ∅ plainly satisfy the criterion. Replacing A by X∖A merely exchanges the two terms, proving closure under complements. If A,B are measurable, apply their splitting identities successively to any E:
μ(E)=μ(E∩A)+μ((E∖A)∩B)+μ(E∖(A∪B)).
(14)
Subadditivity applied to the first two pieces implies
μ(E)≥μ(E∩(A∪B))+μ(E∖(A∪B)).
(15)
The reverse inequality is outer subadditivity. Hence A∪B is measurable, proving closure under finite unions and therefore the algebra of sets property. The argument never subtracts infinite values.

A metric outer measure on a metric space (X,d) is an outer measure satisfying
d(A,B)>0⟹μ(A∪B)=μ(A)+μ(B).​
(16)
Here d(A,B)=inf{d(a,b):a∈A,b∈B}. Every Borel set is measurable for a metric outer measure. Consequently its measurable sigma-algebra contains the Borel sigma-algebra; it may be strictly larger.

For E⊆Rn, define the Lebesgue outer measure by
L∗(E)=inf{∑j=1∞​vol(Qj​):E⊆⋃j​Qj​,Qj​=∏r=1n​[ajr​,bjr​)},
(17)
where the boxes are bounded and vol(Qj​)=∏r​(bjr​−ajr​). The empty covering handles the empty set. Monotonicity follows by inclusion of admissible covers. Taking covers within ε2−j of the outer measures of a sequence of sets proves countable subadditivity; when the sum is infinite the inequality is automatic. Lebesgue measure is the restriction of L∗ to its Caratheodory-measurable sets.
If d(A,B)=δ>0, subdivide each box of any countable cover of A∪B into finitely many half-open boxes of diameter smaller than δ, preserving its total volume. No small box can meet both A and B. The boxes meeting A cover A, and the disjoint family meeting B covers B, so the original cover costs at least L∗(A)+L∗(B). Taking the infimum and then using subadditivity gives
L∗(A∪B)=L∗(A)+L∗(B).​
(18)
Thus the outer measure underlying Lebesgue measure is a metric outer measure, and in particular every Borel set is Lebesgue measurable.

A regular outer measure has, for every E⊆X, a measurable hull H⊇E with μ(H)=μ(E).
Suppose C is an algebra containing Mμ∗​ and μ is finitely additive on C. Fix A∈C and an arbitrary E of finite outer measure. Take a measurable hull H of E. Both H∩A and H∖A belong to C, so
μ(E)=μ(H)=μ(H∩A)+μ(H∖A)≥μ(E∩A)+μ(E∖A).
(19)
Outer subadditivity gives equality. If μ(E)=∞, outer subadditivity already forces the sum on the right to be infinite, again giving equality. Therefore A satisfies the Caratheodory criterion. We have proved C⊆Mμ∗​, so
C=Mμ∗​.​
(20)
This proves the maximal additivity algebra of a regular outer measure without any sigma-finiteness assumption.

The Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem identifies the continuous dual of C(T) with the finite complex Radon measures on T: every bounded complex-linear functional L has a unique representation
L(f)=∫T​fdμ,∥L∥=∣μ∣(T).​
(21)
The right side is the total variation norm of the measure. On this compact metric space Radon measures are regular Borel measures. A functional is positive precisely when its representing measure is positive; then ∥L∥=L(1)=μ(T).

Let m be normalized arclength on the unit circle, so in angular coordinates dm=dt/(2π). It is a finite Radon measure. The given identity says that μ and m represent the same continuous linear functional on C(T). Uniqueness in the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem gives
μ(E)=m(E)=2π∣E∣​for every Borel E⊆T.​
(22)
The printed phrase “Borel sets in E” is interpreted as Borel subsets E of the circle.

Use normalized Haar measure dm=dt/(2π). A homogeneous Banach space on the circle is a Banach subspace B⊆L1(T) with ∥f∥1​≤∥f∥B​, such that translations τs​f(t)=f(t−s) preserve B, satisfy ∥τs​f∥B​=∥f∥B​, and obey ∥τs​f−f∥B​→0 as s→0.
For continuous g, the map s↦g(s)τs​f is continuous with values in the complete space B. Define its Banach-valued Riemann integral, equivalently its Bochner integral, by
f∗g=∫T​g(s)τs​fdm(s).
(30)
The triangle inequality for Riemann sums and passage to their norm limit give the canonical estimate
∥f∗g∥B​≤∫T​∣g(s)∣∥τs​f∥B​dm(s)=∥f∥B​∥g∥1​.​
(31)
The continuous inclusion into L1 carries the same Riemann sums to the ordinary convolution, so this definition agrees almost everywhere with ∫f(t−s)g(s)dm(s). Density of continuous functions in L1, completeness of B, and the estimate extend the operation uniquely to all g∈L1. The ordinary L1 convolution estimate identifies that extension with the same integral formula.

A summability kernel on the circle is a sequence of continuous periodic functions with
∫kn​dm=1,supn​∥kn​∥1​=C<∞,∫δ≤∣t∣≤π​∣kn​(t)∣dm(t)⟶0(0<δ<π).
(32)
The frequently used stronger condition of uniform convergence to zero off each neighborhood of zero implies the last condition. For f in a homogeneous Banach space,
∥f∗kn​−f∥B​≤∫∣kn​(s)∣∥τs​f−f∥B​dm(s).
(33)
Given ε>0, translation continuity bounds the difference inside a sufficiently small neighborhood by ε; its integral is at most Cε. Outside, use 2∥f∥B​ and the vanishing tail condition. Hence f∗kn​→f in B.
With the indexing needed in part (c), the Fejér kernel is
Kn​(t)=n+11​​∑r=0n​eirt​2=∑∣j∣≤n​(1−n+1∣j∣​)eijt,n≥0.
(34)
In particular K0​=1. The squared-modulus expression proves Kn​≥0, and its constant Fourier coefficient proves ∫Kn​dm=∥Kn​∥1​=1. The geometric series yields
Kn​(t)=n+11​sin2(t/2)sin2((n+1)t/2)​≤(n+1)sin2(δ/2)1​(δ≤∣t∣≤π).
