The restriction of a measure to a measurable subset is (μ∣M​)(A)=μ(A∩M) for every measurable A. Absolute continuity of measures, written μ≪κ, means κ(A)=0⟹μ(A)=0. The measures are mutually singular when a measurable S satisfies κ(S)=0 and μ(X∖S)=0. Thus singularity means concentration on a null set of the other measure; it does not assert that every measurable set is null for one of them.

Suppose μ=α+σ=β+τ are two such decompositions. Choose measurable supports S,T of the singular parts with κ(S)=κ(T)=0, and put N=S∪T. Absolute continuity makes both α and β vanish on N. On X∖N the two singular parts vanish, so for every measurable A,
α(A)=α(A∖N)=μ(A∖N)=β(A∖N)=β(A).
(1)
Subtracting from the finite measure μ gives σ=τ. This proves uniqueness of the Lebesgue decomposition without subtracting infinite masses.
For the specified M, the restriction to M is singular because κ(M)=0, and the complementary restriction is absolutely continuous by assumption. Their sum is μ. Uniqueness therefore gives
μs=μ∣M​,μa=μ∣X∖M​.​
(2)

For the finite nonnegative measures there exist a unique nonnegative integrable density h∈L1(κ), up to κ-almost-everywhere equality, and a unique nonnegative measure μs⊥κ, such that
μ(A)=∫A​hdκ+μs(A)(A∈M).​

This combines the Lebesgue decomposition theorem with the Radon-Nikodym theorem. In particular μa=hκ and h=dμa/dκ; if μ≪κ the singular term is zero.

Write J(A) for the partition supremum. Empty cells are permitted, so J≥μi​ for each i, and J(A)≤∑i​μi​(A) is finite. Also J(∅)=0.
Let A=⨆j≥1​Bj​. Any measurable partition A=⨆i​Ai​ yields
∑i​μi​(Ai​)=∑j​∑i​μi​(Ai​∩Bj​)≤∑j​J(Bj​).
(4)
Taking its supremum gives one inequality. Conversely, for any ε>0 choose for each j a partition Bj​=⨆i​Bji​ whose sum exceeds J(Bj​)−ε2−j. The sets Ai​=⨆j​Bji​ partition A, and countable additivity of each μi​ gives J(A)≥∑j​J(Bj​)−ε. Let ε↓0. Thus J is a finite measure.
If another measure ν dominates every μi​, then for every such partition ∑i​μi​(Ai​)≤∑i​ν(Ai​)=ν(A). Consequently J≤ν. Hence J=⋁i​μi​ is the least dominating measure, the finite supremum of positive measures. It generally differs from the pointwise maximum of the numbers μi​(A): the maximizing index may differ between pieces of A.

Choose measurable sets Si​ carrying μis​ with κ(Si​)=0, and put S=⋃i​Si​. Every absolutely continuous component vanishes on S, and every singular component vanishes on its complement. The given restriction identity therefore gives
μ∣S​=⋁i​μis​,μ∣Sc​=⋁i​μia​.
(5)
The first is singular with respect to κ. The second is absolutely continuous because it is bounded above by ∑i​μia​. They sum to μ, so uniqueness identifies them as its Lebesgue components.
Let hi​=dμia​/dκ and h=maxi​hi​, using measurable representatives. Each partition sum is at most ∫A​hdκ. For the reverse inequality, partition A measurably by assigning a point to the smallest index attaining this finite maximum. On its assigned cell hi​=h, and the partition sum is exactly ∫A​hdκ. Thus
dκdμa​=1≤i≤Nmax​dκdμia​​κ-almost everywhere.​
(6)
The maximum of finitely many integrable nonnegative densities is integrable, since it is at most their sum.

The Lebesgue outer measure is
L∗(E)=inf{∑j​∣Qj​∣:E⊆⋃j​Qj​, Qj​ bounded open axis-parallel boxes},
(7)
where ∣Q∣ is the product of its side lengths. The infimum ranges over countable covers; allowing other standard endpoint conventions gives the same outer measure by arbitrarily small enlargement. Lebesgue measure is its restriction to Lebesgue measurable sets.
Enlarged unit cubes give L∗([0,1]n)≤1. For any countable open-box cover of this compact cube choose a finite subcover. A finite grid formed from its box endpoints and the cube endpoints partitions the cube into rectangular cells. The sum of the volumes of covering boxes is at least the sum of these cell volumes, which is one. Boundary faces have elementary zero volume and do not affect the finite volume comparison. Thus every covering sum is at least one, proving
L∗([0,1]n)=1.​
(8)

b OurBigBook.com
A set H is Lebesgue measurable when the Caratheodory measurability criterion holds:
L∗(E)=L∗(E∩H)+L∗(E∖H)for every E⊆Rn.
(9)
Subadditivity always gives the inequality with left side at most right side. For H={xi​>t}, split each covering box Qj​ across the plane xi​=t. Its two pieces have total volume ∣Qj​∣. The piece with a closed cut face can be enlarged by arbitrarily little into an open box; hence its outer measure is at most its corresponding volume, just as for the other piece. Subadditivity applied separately on the two sides consequently gives
L∗(E∩H)+L∗(E∖H)≤∑j​∣Qj​∣.
(10)
For finite outer measure take a covering sum arbitrarily close to L∗(E); infinite outer measure follows already from subadditivity. Thus every coordinate half space is measurable.
Carathéodory's theorem says that the measurable sets form a sigma-algebra and the outer measure restricted to it is a complete measure. Coordinate half spaces generate all open boxes and then all open sets, using countably many rational-endpoint boxes. They therefore generate the Borel sigma-algebra. Hence every Borel set is Lebesgue measurable.

A regular outer measure has a measurable hull of equal outer measure for every set: for each A there is measurable G⊇A with L∗(G)=L∗(A). Lebesgue outer measure has even a Borel hull. If its value on A is finite, choose open-box covers of total cost below L∗(A)+1/j, and let Uj​ be their open unions. Then G=⋂j​Uj​ is Borel and
L∗(A)≤L∗(G)≤L∗(Uj​)≤L∗(A)+1/j.
(11)
For infinite outer measure the whole space is a suitable hull. Thus Lebesgue outer measure is regular.
If A is measurable, additivity immediately gives the requested equality. Conversely suppose the equality holds. Choose a measurable hull G of A and put B=G∩M. Then A⊆B⊆M and L(B)=L∗(A). Since B is measurable, its splitting of M∖A gives
L∗(M∖A)=L(M∖B)+L∗(B∖A).
(12)
The assumed equality and L(M)=L(B)+L(M∖B) imply L∗(B∖A)=0. These subtractions are legitimate because L(M)<∞. Outer-measure-null sets are measurable by the Caratheodory criterion. Hence A=B∖(B∖A) is measurable, proving the equivalence.

The Fourier coefficient of a measure on the circle and convolution of measures on the circle use the angular group law:
μ​(n)=∫T​e−inxdμ(x),(μ∗ν)(E)=∫T​∫T​1E​(x+y)dμ(x)dν(y).
(18)
Addition is modulo 2π. This is the pushforward of the product complex measure under addition; equivalently its integral against a continuous f is ∬f(x+y)dμ(x)dν(y). The integrands are bounded and the variation measures finite, so Fubini applies. In particular,
μ∗ν​(n)=∬e−in(x+y)dμ(x)dν(y)=(∫e−inxdμ(x))(∫e−inydν(y)).
(19)
Thus μ∗ν​(n)=μ​(n)ν(n). These measure coefficients use no additional Haar normalization factor: a probability has zeroth coefficient one.

Weak-star convergence immediately implies convergence of every coefficient by testing e−inx. Conversely coefficient convergence gives convergence against every trigonometric polynomial. Let Df(x) denote the upper limit of the displayed mean oscillation as r↓0. For a continuous integrable approximation g, continuity gives Dg(x)=0. With h=f−g, the triangle inequality yields
Df(x)≤Mh(x)+∣h(x)∣.
(24)
Therefore the maximal inequality and Chebyshev's inequality give
λ({Df>2ε})≤λ({Mh>ε})+λ({∣h∣>ε})≤ε4​∥f−g∥1​.
(25)
Density makes the right side arbitrarily small. For each positive rational ε the exceptional set is null, proving Df=0 almost everywhere, the Lebesgue differentiation theorem. Values where the chosen representative is not finite already form a null set.
For the fundamental theorem define F(x)=∫ax​f(t)dt, with oriented integration for x<a. This function is absolutely continuous: for disjoint intervals the sum of ∣F(bj​)−F(aj​)∣ is at most the integral of ∣f∣ over their union, which is small when its measure is small by absolute continuity of the integral. At a Lebesgue point of f, for h=0,
​hF(x+h)−F(x)​−f(x)​≤∣h∣1​∫x−∣h∣x+∣h∣​∣f(t)−f(x)∣dt→0.
(26)
Thus F′=f almost everywhere and F(b)−F(a)=∫ab​f. Differentiability at every point is not claimed for an arbitrary integrable function.

For a set M of finite measure apply the preceding result to f=1M​. Since its local average minus its point value is bounded in absolute value by its mean absolute oscillation, it gives
2rλ(M∩(x−r,x+r))​⟶1M​(x)almost everywhere.​

Every x in the maximal superlevel set has a first index k(x) at which the average on its unique cell Mk​(x)∈Pk​ exceeds t. Refinement implies that all earlier cells containing x contain this cell, and their averages are at most t. Thus Mk(x)​(x)∈St​ and the selected cells cover the superlevel set.
Two cells at different levels are either disjoint or nested, because of refinement and the partition property. If selected cells of levels k<l intersect, the level-l one lies in the earlier cell whose average is above t, contradicting its first-crossing property. Cells at the same level are disjoint unless identical. Hence the selected cells form a countable disjoint partition of the superlevel set. A cell repeated unchanged at successive levels is counted once, at its first crossing; equal earlier ancestors are included in the comparison condition.

The first-crossing maximal inequality for refining partitions follows from countable disjointness and the strict average bound:
μ({Mf>t})=∑M∈St​​μ(M)≤t1​∑M∈St​​∫M​∣f∣dμ≤t∥f∥1​​.
(28)
Thus this partition maximal operator has weak-type constant one.
The conclusion of differentiation along refining metric partitions is
k→∞lim​μ(Mk​(x))1​∫Mk​(x)​∣f(y)−f(x)∣dμ(y)=0for μ-almost every x.​

The crucial geometric property is shrinking diameter, not merely refinement: since x∈Mk​(x) and diamMk​(x)<1/k, continuity makes the corresponding oscillation tend to zero for every continuous test function. Continuous L1 approximation and the maximal inequality then transfer this to f, exactly as before: the exceptional-set bound is 2∥f−g∥1​/ε for an oscillation threshold 2ε. Refinement supplied the disjoint first-crossing argument; shrinking diameter supplies differentiation.
