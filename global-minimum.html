Global minimum (f(x∗​)=minx∈D​f(x))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Critical point
OurBigBook.com Words: 286 Articles: 1
A real-valued function f:D→R attains a global minimum at x∗​ when
f(x∗​)≤f(x)for every x∈D.
(107)
The point x∗​ is a global minimizer; f(x∗​) is the minimum value. A global maximum reverses the inequality throughout the same domain of a function. Unlike a local minimum or local maximum, a global minimum or global maximum compares every permitted input, however distant. The domain of a function is part of the claim.
For f(x)=x4−2x2 on R, completing the square gives
f(x)=(x2−1)2−1≥−1,
(108)
with equality exactly at x=±1. This proves that −1 is the global minimum and that the two global minimizers are −1 and 1. Neither is a strict global minimizer, because the other attains the same value; both are strict local minima. There is no global maximum on R, whereas on [−1,1] the global maximum is 0, attained at zero.
Finding critical points supplies candidates rather than a global certificate. The squared expression above is beautiful because one inequality compares infinitely many inputs at once. A global minimum is always a local minimum under the relative-neighbourhood convention; the converse fails. For example, f(x)=x3−3x has a strict local minimum at x=1, but f(x)→−∞ as x→−∞, so it has no global minimum on the real line.
Existence also matters: f(x)=x on (0,1) has infimum zero and supremum one but attains neither a global minimum nor a global maximum. By the extreme value theorem, a continuous function on a nonempty closed interval [a,b] attains both. To find those values, compare all interior critical numbers, including points where the derivative does not exist, and both endpoints. Checking only stationary points can miss a corner or an endpoint. [ref]
