Terminal velocity under linear drag (vT​=Mg/b,τ=M/b)

 ... Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics Position Velocity Terminal velocity
OurBigBook.com Words: 239
Consider a point mass of fixed mass M>0 released from rest in a stationary medium. Choose downward as positive, assume constant gravitational acceleration g>0, neglect buoyancy, and impose linear drag Fd​=−bv with constant linear friction coefficient b>0. Here v is the signed downward velocity component; it equals speed during this descent. Newton's second law becomes
Mdtdv​=Mg−bv,v(0)=0,vT​=bMg​,τ=bM​.
(60)
The terminal velocity balances the two forces, and the time constant τ controls how quickly that balance is approached:
v(t)=vT​(1−e−t/τ),a(t)=dtdv​=ge−t/τ,t≥0.
(61)
For the illustrative parameters M=1kg, b=2kg/s and g=9.8m/s2, the time constant is 0.5s and the terminal velocity is 4.9m/s downward. At t=1s=2τ, the downward velocity is 4.9(1−e−2)m/s≃4.23686m/s and the downward acceleration is 9.8e−2m/s2≃1.32629m/s2. This is a toy linear drag model, not a claim that every 1kg falling object has this resistance law.
For a proof outline, subtract the equilibrium value: u=v−vT​ obeys du/dt=−u/τ. The exponential decay solution and the initial condition give u=−vT​e−t/τ; one derivative gives the acceleration. Every time constant multiplies the remaining gap vT​−v by e−1. The exact limit is reached only as t→∞. This makes the model useful for estimating a settling duration without mistaking vanishing acceleration for vanishing velocity.
Newton already analyzed vertical motion under uniform gravity and resistance proportional to velocity in Book II, Section I, Proposition III of the Principia. His geometric construction includes a greatest downward velocity, illustrating that a limiting rate can emerge from balancing a driving force and resistance. [ref]
