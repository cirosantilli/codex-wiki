A binary categorical product of objects of a category A,B is an object P with categorical morphisms pA​:P→A and pB​:P→B. Its universal property is that, for every object X and pair of arrows f:X→A, g:X→B, there is exactly one arrow h:X→P satisfying
pA​∘h=f,pB​∘h=g.
(6)
The arrows pA​,pB​ are called the product projections. Existence lets us combine two maps; uniqueness means there is no extra unspecified information in the combined map.
In the Category of sets, take A={0,1} and B={a,b,c}. Their Cartesian product contains six ordered pairs, and the projection maps select the first and second coordinates. Let X={u,v,w}, f(u)=0, f(v)=1, f(w)=1, and g(u)=a, g(v)=b, g(w)=a. The unique required map is h(u)=(0,a), h(v)=(1,b), h(w)=(1,a). Reading either coordinate recovers the corresponding input map.
In a partially ordered set viewed as a category, the projection arrows mean P≤A and P≤B. The universal property says that every common lower bound X satisfies X≤P, so P is the greatest lower bound. For subsets ordered by inclusion this is A∩B, the intersection; it is a different construction from the Cartesian product in the Category of sets because the allowed arrows have changed.
The universal property also compares any two proposed products: each supplies a unique map to the other compatible with the projections. The two composites have the same projections as the corresponding identity morphisms, so uniqueness makes them identities. Thus a categorical product is determined up to a unique projection-preserving isomorphism, which explains why we can use whichever concrete construction is convenient.
