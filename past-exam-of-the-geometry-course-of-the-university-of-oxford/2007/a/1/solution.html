An orientable surface admits local coordinate orientations that are consistent on overlaps. Equivalently a triangulation can be oriented so that adjacent faces induce opposite directions on their common edge. Use a polygon word to record its successive boundary edges, identifying equal letters in their indicated directions; a negative exponent reverses that edge direction.
Square models are P:abab, K:aba−1b, and T:aba−1b−1. The first identifies antipodal boundary points of a disk, giving the real projective plane. Only the torus among these three is orientable: its paired sides traverse the boundary oppositely, whereas each other model has a same-direction pair. The projective model has two vertex classes, and the other two one, giving Euler characteristics one and zero respectively.
The other possibility is the sphere, for example the word aa−1bb−1 with three vertex classes. There are no others: a paired square has one face, two edges and between one and three vertex classes, so its Euler characteristic is V−1∈{0,1,2}. The classification of compact surfaces leaves precisely the sphere, real projective plane, torus and Klein bottle at those values, with the indicated orientabilities.
For connected sums, remove a disk from each surface, join the resulting boundary circles, and cut a joining arc to produce a single polygon. Its word concatenates the two original words. The real projective plane also has the bigon word cc, so
T#P: aba−1b−1cc,T#K: aba−1b−1cdc−1d.​

These use respectively six and eight sides, proving the requested hexagon and octagon constructions. Their Euler characteristics are −1 and −2, also obtained from χ(X#Y)=χ(X)+χ(Y)−2.
