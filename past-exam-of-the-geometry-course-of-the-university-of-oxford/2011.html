For a finite CW complex with V vertices, E edges and F faces,
χ=V−E+F.​
(1)
This Euler characteristic is unchanged by subdividing an edge, which adds one vertex and one edge, or subdividing a face by an edge, which adds one edge and one face. Common refinements therefore give the same value for different subdivisions of the same surface.

Solution

Words: 136
b OurBigBook.com
Form the dual graph of the finite triangulation: its vertices are the triangles, and two vertices are joined when their triangles share an edge. Connectedness of the surface makes this graph connected. Choose a spanning tree and begin with one disjoint triangle. Attach the remaining triangles along the tree edges, one at a time, always joining a new triangle along one boundary edge. At every step the result is a topological disk, so the final union is a polygonal disk.
The edges not used for these attachments still occur twice on the boundary, once for each incident triangle. Identify each pair as in the original triangulation. The result recovers the original surface. This proves a compact connected closed surface is a polygon with its boundary edges identified in pairs. Closedness is the convention here; a boundary would leave some edges unpaired.

123, 134, 145, 156, 126, 235, 245, 246, 346, 356.​

For a nonconstant holomorphic map f:X→Y between compact connected Riemann surfaces, let n be its degree and ep​ its local degree at p. The Riemann-Hurwitz formula is
χ(X)=nχ(Y)−p∈X∑​(ep​−1).​
(7)
Equivalently, 2gX​−2=n(2gY​−2)+∑p​(ep​−1). Only finitely many terms are nonzero; their sum is the total ramification. In local coordinates the map has the form w=zep​ after suitable coordinate changes.

Let Λ=Zω1​+Zω2​ and T=C/Λ. Its fundamental parallelogram with opposite sides identified is a torus, so χ(T)=0. The Weierstrass elliptic function is even and doubly periodic. One way to verify the latter from its series is to differentiate: the locally normally convergent derivative series is
℘′(z)=−2∑ω∈Λ​(z−ω)−3,
(8)
which is absolutely convergent away from the lattice and periodic by reindexing. Thus ℘(z+ωj​)−℘(z) is constant. Evaluating at z=−ωj​/2 and using evenness shows that this constant is zero for each generating period.
Therefore [z]↦℘(z) descends to a meromorphic function on T, with one double pole at [0]. Viewed as a map to the Riemann sphere, a pole is a holomorphic point in the target coordinate 1/w. The map is consequently holomorphic everywhere and
T⟶CP1,[z]⟼℘(z),deg℘=2.​
(9)

The Riemann-Hurwitz formula gives total ramification four because 0=2⋅2−R. The double pole at [0] has local degree two and contributes one. Since ℘′ is odd and periodic, it vanishes at each nonzero point satisfying [h]=[−h]. These are
2ω1​​,2ω2​​,2ω1​+ω2​​(modΛ).​
(10)
They are three distinct finite points, each contributing at least one to ramification. Together with [0] they exhaust the total of four. Each therefore has local degree exactly two, meaning its derivative has a simple zero. There are no other zeros of ℘′, and their translates by Λ give the full list in the complex plane.

[2]:T→T,[z]↦[2z].

Put R(z)=41​(℘′′/℘′)2−2℘(z). This is an elliptic function. Its only possible poles modulo Λ are 0 and the three simple zeros h of ℘′. At such a nonzero half-period, periodicity and evenness give ℘(h+t)=℘(h−t), so
℘′(h+t)=At+O(t3),A=0,℘′(h+t)℘′′(h+t)​=t1​+O(t).
(14)
It follows that R(h+t)=1/(4t2)+O(1) with no simple-pole term. This is exactly the principal part of ℘(2h+2t)=℘(2t).
At zero the source expansion yields
℘=z−2+O(z2),℘′=−2z−3+O(z),℘′′=6z−4+O(1),℘′′/℘′=−3z−1+O(z3).
(15)
Consequently R(z)=1/(4z2)+O(z2), again matching ℘(2z). Thus R(z)−℘(2z) is a holomorphic function on a compact connected Riemann surface, hence constant. Its limit at zero is zero, proving
℘(2z)=41​(℘′(z)℘′′(z)​)2−2℘(z).​
(16)
The equality extends over the apparent singularities as an identity of meromorphic functions.

For a regular parametrization r(u,v), the first fundamental form is the induced Riemannian metric
I=dr⋅dr=Edu2+2Fdudv+Gdv2,E=ru​⋅ru​,F=ru​⋅rv​,G=rv​⋅rv​.
(17)
It measures squared lengths of tangent vectors and therefore lengths of curves. In the plane put x=ρcosθ, y=ρsinθ. The derivatives are orthogonal, of lengths one and ρ, so
dx2+dy2=dρ2+ρ2dθ2.​
(18)
These polar coordinates are regular for ρ>0.

Solution

Words: 75
b OurBigBook.com
Two regular coordinate patches are locally isometric under a coordinate correspondence exactly when it preserves their first fundamental forms. More explicitly, a diffeomorphism Φ:U→U of parameter domains induces a local isometry if and only if
Φ∗I=I.​
(19)
In common coordinates this means equality of the three coefficient functions E,F,G. The criterion follows because the first fundamental form gives the inner product of every pair of tangent vectors. Equality therefore preserves lengths of all curves; conversely a smooth isometry preserves tangent-vector lengths and their inner products by polarization.

a2du2+(1+a2u2)dv2=cosh2sds2+cosh2sdv2.

(sinhscosv,sinhssinv,v)⟼(coshscosv,coshssinv,s)

a OurBigBook.com
Choose representatives v1​,…,vn+1​ of the first n+1 points. The general-position condition says that these vectors form a basis of Cn+1. Write a representative of the last point as vn+2​=∑i​ci​vi​. Every ci​ is nonzero; otherwise the last point and the other n basis points would lie in a hyperplane. Rescaling vi​ by ci​ gives representatives with vn+2​=∑i​vi​. Normalize representatives wi​ of the second frame in the same way.
There is a unique invertible linear map L with L(vi​)=wi​ for i≤n+1. It also maps vn+2​ to wn+2​, so its projectivization has the required effect. For uniqueness, the quotient of two candidate projective transformations fixes the normalized frame. A representing matrix must be diagonal in the vi​ basis, with entries ai​. Fixing [∑i​vi​] forces all ai​ to be equal. Such a scalar represents the identity projective transformation. Thus the ordered projective frame determines the transformation uniquely.

Represent each point by a nonzero column vi​. The source's cross-ratio is
det(v1​,v4​)det(v2​,v3​)det(v1​,v3​)det(v2​,v4​)​.
(27)
Rescaling representatives cancels between numerator and denominator. If the projective transformation is represented by A∈GL2​(C), then det(Avi​,Avj​)=det(A)det(vi​,vj​). Two factors of det(A) occur in both numerator and denominator and cancel. Hence the cross-ratio is invariant under every projective transformation.

Injectivity makes the images of any three distinct points distinct. By the ordered-frame result, there is a projective transformation L agreeing with the given map on three chosen points. Composing by L−1 reduces to an injective map fixing those points and preserving every cross-ratio. Change coordinates so that the three points are ∞,0,1.
For any other point with affine coordinate z, the convention in the source gives
(∞0:1z)=z.
(28)
Preservation therefore forces the image of z to have the same coordinate z. The normalized map fixes every point and is the identity. Undoing the normalization proves the original map is exactly L, hence projective. No prior continuity or surjectivity assumption is needed.

At λ=−1 and s=i, the chosen map is
σ[x:y:z]=[−x:iy:z],σ2[x:y:z]=[x:−y:z],σ4=1.
(35)
The square is not the identity on the cubic, since a generic point has y=0. Thus ord(σ)=4​.
Choose the cubic's point [0:1:0] as the origin in its complex torus description. Since σ fixes it, its torus form is z+Λ↦az+Λ, with aΛ=Λ. Exact order four implies a=±i; a2=−1. Therefore Λ is stable under multiplication by i.
To identify its shape without an unproved uniqueness assertion, choose a shortest nonzero period ω. For any λ0​∈Λ, approximate λ0​/ω by a Gaussian integer m+ni within distance 1/2​<1. The difference λ0​−(m+ni)ω belongs to Λ and is shorter than ω, so it must vanish. Hence
Λ=ω(Z+iZ),C−1​≅C/(Z+iZ).​
(36)
The lattice is determined up to a nonzero complex scaling: it is square.

Take λ=eπi/3 and the chosen s=eπi/6. Then λs=i, so i/(λs)=1, and −1/λ=λ2. In affine coordinates the chosen lift becomes
υ(x,y)=(1+λ2x,y).
(37)

ord(υ)=3for the lift chosen above.​
(38)
The other square-root choice replaces this map by (x,y)↦(1+λ2x,−y). Its cube is (x,y)↦(x,−y), so that lift has order six. This sign dependence is why a lift was specified explicitly.
Both fix the point at infinity. In the complex torus model their linear multipliers have order three or six, so the lattice is stable under a primitive cube root ζ=e2πi/3 and hence under eπi/3=1+ζ. Choose a shortest nonzero period ω. The triangular lattice Z+ζZ has covering radius 1/3​<1: its equilateral triangles of side one each have circumradius 1/3​. Approximating any period divided by ω by this lattice leaves a strictly shorter period, which must be zero. Therefore
Λ=ω(Z+eπi/3Z).​
(39)
Thus the cubic has the hexagonal lattice shape, independently of which of the two lifts is chosen.

The genus-degree formula for a nonsingular plane curve gives
g=2(d−1)(d−2)​.​
(40)
A divisor is a finite formal sum D=∑p​np​[p] with integer coefficients; its degree is degD=∑p​np​. An effective divisor has all np​≥0. A hyperplane divisor is the intersection with a projective line not containing the curve, counted with local intersection multiplicities. Its degree is d.
Define L(D)={0}∪{f meromorphic:(f)+D≥0} and ℓ(D)=dimC​L(D). For a canonical divisor K, the Riemann-Roch theorem states
ℓ(D)−ℓ(K−D)=degD+1−g.​
(41)
Here (f) records zero orders positively and pole orders negatively, and degK=2g−2.

A meromorphic function on a compact Riemann surface has finitely many poles, say pj​ of orders rj​. For each pj​, choose a projective line through it that does not contain C. Its hyperplane divisor contains pj​ with multiplicity at least one. Include rj​ copies of this divisor. The sum then dominates the pole divisor of f.
Add further arbitrary hyperplane divisors until their number m satisfies m≥d and md>2g−2. Adding effective divisors cannot destroy the pole bound, so for D=H1​+⋯+Hm​ we have
(f)+D≥0,f∈L(D).​
(42)
For a constant function, including zero, no initial poles need to be covered; simply add sufficiently many lines.

d OurBigBook.com
Write Li​=ai​x+bi​y+ci​z and S=∏i​Li​. The numerator and denominator have the same homogeneous degree, so their ratio is well-defined on projective points away from its poles and extends as a meromorphic function on C. Locally the pole order cannot exceed the zero order of S∣C​, whose divisor is D. Thus fQ​∈L(D).
Equality of two ratios is equivalent to Q−Q′ vanishing on C. A nonsingular projective plane curve is irreducible: distinct positive-degree components would intersect by Bézout's theorem, producing a singular point. Its homogeneous vanishing ideal is therefore generated by its irreducible defining polynomial P. One can also see this using Bézout's theorem: a homogeneous polynomial not divisible by P has only finitely many intersections with C, so cannot vanish on the whole curve. Consequently
fQ​=fQ′​⟺Q−Q′=PR,R∈Vm−d​.​
(45)
Multiplication by the nonzero polynomial P is injective. The linear map Vm​→L(D) therefore has kernel PVm−d​ and image dimension
(2m+2​)−(2m−d+2​)=md+23d−d2​=md+1−g.​
(46)
The assumption m≥d makes both polynomial-space dimensions applicable directly.

Given f, choose the hyperplane divisors as in (a). The ratios fQ​ form a linear subspace of L(D) by (d), and its dimension equals md+1−g=ℓ(D) by (b). A linear subspace of a finite-dimensional vector space with the same dimension is the whole space. Hence f=fQ​ for some homogeneous polynomial Q of degree m, giving
f([x:y:z])=∏i=1m​(ai​x+bi​y+ci​z)Q(x,y,z)​.​
(47)
The zero function corresponds to Q=0. Thus every allowed meromorphic function is a ratio of homogeneous polynomials of equal degree, with a denominator that can be chosen as a product of linear forms.
