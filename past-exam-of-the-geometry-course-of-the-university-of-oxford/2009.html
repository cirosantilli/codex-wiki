i OurBigBook.com
The classification of compact surfaces states that every compact connected topological surface without boundary is homeomorphic to exactly one of the sphere, a connected sum of surfaces consisting of g≥1 tori, or a connected sum of h≥1 real projective planes. The torus family is orientable and the projective-plane family is nonorientable; their respective genera are unique. Including g=0 makes the sphere the genus-zero orientable case. If boundaries are allowed, the number of removed open discs is an additional invariant.

ii OurBigBook.com
A finite subdivision into vertices, edges and polygonal faces defines the Euler characteristic
χ(M)=V−E+F.​
(1)
Count cells after all identifications. Subdividing an edge adds one vertex and one edge; splitting a face adds one edge and one face. Each operation leaves V−E+F unchanged. Comparing subdivisions through a common refinement proves independence of the subdivision.

For the connected sum of surfaces, remove an embedded open disc from each surface and identify the resulting boundary circles by a homeomorphism. The resulting closed connected surface is denoted M#N.
Removing a disc decreases the Euler characteristic by one. Gluing the remaining surfaces along their common boundary subtracts the characteristic of a circle, which is zero. Thus
χ(M#N)=χ(M)+χ(N)−2.​
(2)
This can be verified by choosing matching subdivisions of the boundary circles and counting the vertices and edges identified in the gluing.

A square model of the torus has one vertex, two edges and one face, so its Euler characteristic is zero. The connected-sum formula gives
χ(#gT2)=g⋅0−2(g−1)=2−2g.​
(3)
The real projective plane has a bigon model with one vertex, one edge and one face, hence characteristic one. Therefore
χ(#hRP2)=h−2(h−1)=2−h.​
(4)

Take M=T2, N=RP2#RP2 and S=RP2. The first is orientable, while N, the Klein bottle, is not, so M and N are not homeomorphic.
Both M#S and N#S are nonorientable and have Euler characteristic −1. By the classification of compact surfaces, both are homeomorphic to the connected sum of three projective planes. Hence
M#S≅N#S,M≅N.​
(5)
This is failure of cancellation for connected sums of surfaces.

For a nonconstant holomorphic map of degree d between compact connected Riemann surfaces, the Riemann-Hurwitz formula is
2−2gX​=d(2−2gY​)−p∈X∑​(ep​−1).​
(6)
Here ep​ is the local degree, characterized by local coordinates in which the map is z↦zep​. The sum is finite and its terms are nonnegative; a ramification point has ep​>1.

The given Weierstrass elliptic function is even, by replacing the lattice index ω with −ω. Differentiation gives
℘′(z)=−2∑ω∈Λ​(z−ω)−3.
(8)
For any λ∈Λ, reindexing the sum shows ℘′(z+λ)=℘′(z). Thus ℘(z+λ)−℘(z)=cλ​ is constant off the lattice. Replacing z by −z−λ and using evenness changes this difference to its negative, so cλ​=−cλ​ and cλ​=0. Hence ℘ is doubly periodic.
It therefore descends to a meromorphic function on C/Λ, with a double pole at zero modulo the lattice and no others. A meromorphic function is a holomorphic map to the Riemann sphere, sending poles to infinity. Locally at zero, 1/℘(z)=z2+O(z4) is holomorphic and vanishes there, so the sphere chart at infinity supplies the required extension. The map is nonconstant because it has a pole.

The total pole order in one period cell is two, so the map has degree two. The Riemann-Hurwitz formula for genus one to genus zero gives 0=2⋅2−R, hence R=4. Each local degree is at most two, so every ramification contribution is exactly one. Therefore
deg℘=2,four ramification points.​
(9)
They are zero and the three nonzero half-period classes. Zero is ramified because its reciprocal has a double zero. At a nonzero half-period, oddness and periodicity of ℘′ force ℘′=0; the count shows these exhaust the remaining points.

For a regular parametrization r(u,v), the first fundamental form is the induced metric
ds2=Edu2+2Fdudv+Gdv2,E=ru​⋅ru​, F=ru​⋅rv​, G=rv​⋅rv​.​
(11)
It measures the lengths and angles of tangent vectors. A geodesic is a curve whose unit tangent has zero covariant derivative along itself. For an arc-length parametrized surface curve in Euclidean three-space, this means its acceleration is normal to the surface, or γ′′⋅ru​=γ′′⋅rv​=0.

Since γ′=ru​u′+rv​v′, we have γ′⋅ru​=Eu′+Fv′. The normal-acceleration characterization of a geodesic gives
dsd​(Eu′+Fv′)=γ′⋅(ruu​u′+ruv​v′).
(12)
Expanding and differentiating the metric coefficients yields ru​⋅ruu​=Eu​/2, rv​⋅ruu​+ru​⋅ruv​=Fu​, and rv​⋅ruv​=Gu​/2. Therefore
dsd​(Eu′+Fv′)=21​Eu​(u′)2+Fu​u′v′+21​Gu​(v′)2.​
(13)
Applying the same calculation to γ′⋅rv​=Fu′+Gv′ gives
dsd​(Fu′+Gv′)=21​Ev​(u′)2+Fv​u′v′+21​Gv​(v′)2.​
(14)
These are also the Euler-Lagrange equations of the quadratic metric energy.

For the surface of revolution, the arc-length condition on its profile gives E=1, F=0, G=f(u)2. Thus the geodesic equations reduce to
u′′=ff′(v′)2,dsd​(f2v′)=0.

For every q∈L, the line pq meets the nonsingular conic in two points counted with multiplicity by the Bézout theorem. One is p; define α(q) as the second, using α(q)=p for the unique tangent line at p. Conversely, for r∈C∖{p}, intersect the line pr with L, and send p itself to the intersection of its tangent with L. Since p∈/L, each intersection with L is unique. These constructions are inverse, so α:L→C is a bijection preserving the stated collinearity. This is parametrization of a conic by projection.
For the particular conic take p=[1:0:1] and L={x=0}. If q=[0:s:t], points on pq have coordinates [a:bs:a+bt]. Substitution into the conic gives
b[−a(s+2t)+b(s2−t2)]=0.
(18)
Taking the second intersection gives the explicit map
α([0:s:t])=[s2−t2:s(s+2t):s2+st+t2].​
(19)
The three coordinates do not vanish simultaneously. If s+2t=0, the formula gives p, as required for the tangent direction.
For rational points, put r=s/t when t=0. The full affine list is
x=r2+r+1r2−1​,y=r2+r+1r(r+2)​,r∈Q,​
(20)
together with the parameter r=∞, which gives (x,y)=(1,1). The denominator is (r+1/2)2+3/4>0 for rational r. Conversely, every rational point other than p determines a rational joining line and hence a rational point of L; the point p is obtained at r=−2. Thus this lists all rational solutions.

An inflection point of an algebraic plane curve is a smooth point where the tangent has intersection multiplicity at least three. For a homogeneous defining polynomial P of degree d, its Hessian curve is defined by the determinant of the matrix of second partial derivatives, of degree 3(d−2). In characteristic zero, the smooth inflection points are exactly the smooth points of C∩Hess(C).
For a local verification, move a smooth point to [0:0:1] and its tangent to y=0, and scale so P(x,y,1)=y+ax2+bxy+cy2+⋯. The tangent contact is at least three exactly when a=0. The homogeneous Euler identities give the Hessian matrix at that point as ​2ab0​b2cd−1​0d−10​​, whose determinant is −2a(d−1)2. This proves the criterion for d>1.
For the given cubic, the singularity equations are x2+λyz=y2+λxz=z2+λxy=0. No coordinate can vanish at a projective solution, since that would force all three to vanish. Multiplying the equations therefore gives 1=(−λ)3, so singularity requires λ3=−1. Conversely, under that condition the point [1:1:−1/λ] satisfies all three equations, and the Euler identity places it on the cubic. Thus
C is nonsingular⟺λ3=−1.​
(21)

On the nonsingular cubic, x3+y3+z3=−3λxyz. Substituting this into the Hessian equation leaves −4(λ3+1)xyz=0. Since λ3+1=0, a flex has a zero coordinate. The remaining cubic equation then gives the nine points
[0:1:−ωk],[1:0:−ωk],[1:−ωk:0],k=0,1,2,​

A divisor on an algebraic curve is a finite formal integer sum D=∑p​np​p. Its degree is ∑p​np​. The Riemann-Roch space L(D) consists of zero and the meromorphic functions h with (h)+D≥0, and ℓ(D)=dimL(D). The Riemann-Roch theorem states
ℓ(D)−ℓ(K−D)=degD+1−g,
(27)
where K is a canonical divisor, of degree 2g−2. In genus one, deg(K−D)=−n<0 for degD=n>0. A negative-degree divisor on an algebraic curve has no nonzero section, since a principal divisor has degree zero and an effective divisor has nonnegative degree. Hence
ℓ(D)=n(g=1, n>0).​
(28)
A nonsingular plane cubic has genus one by the genus-degree formula. Therefore ℓ(p+q)=2, and there is a nonconstant f∈L(p+q). It has at most simple poles at p,q and no other poles. It cannot have no poles, since a holomorphic function on a compact Riemann surface is constant. It cannot have just one simple pole either, by the allowed fact, or by ℓ(p)=ℓ(q)=1. Thus f has simple poles at both p and q, and no others.

Apply the preceding argument to q+r to obtain a nonconstant meromorphic function g with simple poles exactly at q,r. Both f,g, and the constant function one, belong to L(p+q+r).
If a+bf+cg=0, examination at p, where only f has a pole, forces b=0. Examination at r, where only g has a pole, forces c=0, and then a=0. Thus the three functions are linearly independent. Since the Riemann-Roch theorem gives ℓ(p+q+r)=3,
{1,f,g} is a basis of L(p+q+r).​
(29)

Away from p,q,r, all three coordinate functions are holomorphic and the first is one, so they define a projective point. Near p, take a local coordinate z vanishing there. Write f=a/z+O(1) with a=0, while g is holomorphic. Then
[1:f:g]=[z:zf:zg]⟶[0:a:0]=[0:1:0].
(30)
The scaled coordinates are holomorphic and do not all vanish, proving a holomorphic extension. Similarly F(r)=[0:0:1]. At q, write f=b/z+O(1) and g=c/z+O(1) with b,c=0. The same scaling gives F(q)=[0:b:c], again well-defined. Hence
F:C⟶P2 is a well-defined holomorphic map.​
(31)

Every degree-three monomial evaluated at (1,f,g) is fagb with a+b≤3. Its possible pole orders at p,q,r are at most a, a+b, b, respectively, and hence at most three each. Therefore Q(1,f,g)∈L(3p+3q+3r).
The vector space of homogeneous degree-three polynomials in three variables has dimension (25​)=10. The target Riemann-Roch space has dimension nine, because its divisor on an algebraic curve has degree nine and genus is one. Thus the evaluation linear map
Q⟼Q(1,f,g)
(32)
has nontrivial kernel. Consequently
there is a nonzero homogeneous cubic Q with Q(1,f,g)≡0.​
(33)

Away from the three poles, the identity from the preceding part puts F(u) on the cubic Q=0. The local extensions and continuity put the three pole images there too. Thus F(C)⊆{Q=0}, a cubic curve.
