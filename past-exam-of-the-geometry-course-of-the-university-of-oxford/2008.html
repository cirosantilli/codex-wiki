The classification of compact surfaces says that every compact connected surface without boundary is homeomorphic either to the sphere with g≥0 handles, with Euler characteristic 2−2g, or to a connected sum of k≥1 real projective planes, with Euler characteristic 2−k. The first family is orientable and the second is nonorientable; the displayed invariants uniquely determine the type. With boundary one additionally removes a uniquely determined number of open disks.
Number the vertices of the fourteen-gon v0​,…,v13​ in boundary order. The printed edge identifications imply
v0​∼v5​∼v2​∼v3​∼v4​∼v1​∼v6​∼v13​∼v7​∼v12​∼v9​∼v10​∼v11​∼v8​∼v0​.
(1)
Thus there is one vertex, seven edges and one face in the quotient. Consequently
χ(S)=1−7+1=−5.​
(2)
The two occurrences of a1​ have the same sign, as do those of a5​, so the orientation on the polygon cannot extend consistently across those paired edges. Thus the surface is nonorientable, and classification identifies it as a connected sum of seven projective planes. The inverse on the first occurrence of a4​ and the positive second occurrence have been retained in the vertex calculation.

A Riemann surface is a connected Hausdorff second-countable space equipped with complex coordinate charts whose changes of coordinate are holomorphic with holomorphic inverses. Such charts give it the structure of a one-dimensional complex manifold.
A holomorphic change of coordinate has nonzero derivative. In real coordinates its derivative matrix has determinant ∣f′(z)∣2>0 by the Cauchy-Riemann equations. Thus every overlap preserves the standard orientation of the complex plane. These chart orientations fit together, proving every Riemann surface is orientable.

For a regular parametrization choose the unit normal n=(ru​×rv​)/∣ru​×rv​∣. The first fundamental form is the squared length induced on tangent vectors:
I=Edu2+2Fdudv+Gdv2,E=ru​⋅ru​,F=ru​⋅rv​,G=rv​⋅rv​.
(3)

II=Ldu2+2Mdudv+Ndv2,L=n⋅ruu​,M=n⋅ruv​,N=n⋅rvv​.
(4)

K=EG−F2LN−M2​.​
(5)
Regularity ensures EG−F2>0.

For the unit-speed curve, the normal curvature is κn​=t′⋅n, and the geodesic curvature is κg​=t′⋅(n×t). Differentiating γ=r(u,v) twice and taking the normal component kills the terms in ru​,rv​, giving
κn​=L(u′)2+2Mu′v′+N(v′)2.​
(13)
An affinely parametrized geodesic has zero tangential acceleration, or equivalently its covariant derivative of velocity is zero. For unit speed, t′⋅t=0, and t,n×t,n form an orthonormal frame. Therefore t′=κn​n+κg​(n×t), so zero tangential acceleration is exactly κg​=0.

Since Eu′+Fv′=γ′⋅ru​, differentiation gives
dsd​(Eu′+Fv′)=γ′′⋅ru​+21​Eu​(u′)2+Fu​u′v′+21​Gu​(v′)2.
(14)
The corresponding calculation with γ′⋅rv​=Fu′+Gv′ gives
dsd​(Fu′+Gv′)=γ′′⋅rv​+21​Ev​(u′)2+Fv​u′v′+21​Gv​(v′)2.
(15)
Thus the two requested equations hold exactly when γ′′ is perpendicular to both tangent basis vectors, which is precisely the geodesic condition.
On expanding the left sides, the coefficient matrix of (u′′,v′′) is the positive-definite metric matrix. It is invertible, so the equations form a smooth second-order ordinary differential equation in local coordinates. The local existence and uniqueness theorem gives a unique solution for each initial point and coordinate velocity. For an arbitrary initial tangent vector w this is an affine parameter of constant speed ∣w∣; it is arclength when ∣w∣=1. For w=0 the solution is the constant curve. This specifies how the arbitrary-velocity statement relates to the preceding unit-speed convention.

Let R be reflection in the plane. Choose a point of the regular intersection curve and a unit tangent vector w to it. Both the point and w are fixed by R. The unique local geodesic with these initial data is carried by the surface isometry R to another geodesic with the same initial data. Uniqueness therefore gives Rγ=γ. Its image lies in the fixed-point set of the reflection, namely the plane, hence locally in the intersection curve. Thus the intersection curve is a geodesic, when parametrized by arclength.

For a positively oriented curvilinear polygon bounding a disk D in one coordinate patch, with exterior turning angles θi​, the Gauss-Bonnet theorem states
∫D​KdA+∫∂D​κg​ds+i∑​θi​=2π.​
(16)
For a closed compact surface the global formula is
∫S​KdA=2πχ(S).​
(17)
Triangulate the surface with each triangle contained in a coordinate patch. For each face, writing its exterior angles as π minus its interior angles gives ∫K+∫κg​=∑interior angles−π. On summing, the two contributions on each internal edge cancel, and all angles at each vertex sum to 2π. Thus ∫S​K=2πV−πF. Since 3F=2E, this equals 2π(V−E+F), as required. For a nonorientable surface apply the orientable version to its orientation double cover: both the curvature integral and Euler characteristic double. With boundary, the same summation retains the boundary geodesic-curvature integral and the exterior angles at boundary corners, giving the corresponding global boundary formula with right side 2πχ(S).

For the sphere, choose the inward normal so that the second fundamental form is I/a. Both principal curvatures are 1/a, giving K=1/a2; the outward choice reverses both signs and gives the same product.
The octant has area 4πa2/8=πa2/2, so its curvature integral is π/2. Its sides are great-circle geodesics, hence have zero geodesic curvature. Each interior angle is π/2, so each exterior angle is also π/2. Therefore
∫D​KdA+∫∂D​κg​ds+i∑​θi​=2π​+0+23π​=2π,​
(18)
verifying the polygon formula directly.

If two distinct geodesic arcs enclosed a disk, regard its boundary as a curvilinear two-sided polygon, with interior angles α,β. These are positive; zero angle would make the arcs tangent and local geodesic uniqueness would make them coincide. The geodesic-curvature terms vanish, so Gauss-Bonnet gives
∫D​KdA+(π−α)+(π−β)=2π,∫D​KdA=α+β>0.
(19)
This contradicts K≤0. Thus no such disk-bounding geodesic bigon exists. The disk hypothesis matters because it fixes the Euler characteristic on the right side.

For a projective frame, choose representative vectors v1​,…,vn+1​ forming a basis. Express the final point by ∑ci​vi​. Every ci​ is nonzero, since otherwise some n+1 of the given representatives would be dependent. Rescale the first representatives to ci​vi​, so the last point is represented by their sum. Do the same for the target frame, with basis wi​. The linear map taking each rescaled source basis vector to the corresponding target vector induces the required projective transformation. For uniqueness, a transformation fixing the first n+1 coordinate points is represented by a diagonal matrix. Fixing their sum forces every diagonal entry to agree. It is therefore scalar, giving a unique projective transformation.
If three intersection points of the conics lay on a line, Bézout's theorem would force that line to be a component of either nonsingular conic, impossible. Thus the four points form a projective frame and can be moved to the three coordinate points and [1:1:1]. Vanishing at the coordinate points removes the square terms from each conic equation; vanishing at the last point gives a+b+c=0.
Substitution gives, by direct expansion,
ayz+bzx+cxy=au2+bv2+cw2+(a+b+c)(uv+uw+vw)=au2+bv2+cw2.
(20)
The substitution matrix
S=​011​101​110​​
(21)
has determinant two, hence is invertible over C. If the initial frame transformation is represented in the direction old coordinates equal Q times new coordinates, take P=QS. Both quadratic forms then have no mixed terms, proving PTAP and PTBP are simultaneously diagonal. The same argument applies when an individual conic is reducible but the intersection consists of exactly four distinct points: a line containing three would be a common component, contradicting finiteness of the intersection.

An inflection point is a smooth point where the tangent line has intersection multiplicity at least three with the curve. For a nonsingular plane curve of degree n≥3, these are the intersections with its Hessian curve, whose degree is 3(n−2). The original curve is not a component of its Hessian: otherwise in each graph chart the tangent direction would have zero derivative identically, making the curve locally a line and hence a line globally. Bézout therefore gives the upper bound 3n(n−2), counting multiplicity. A nonsingular conic has no inflection points; lines are excluded from this finite-contact definition.
Take F=y2z−x3+xz2. Its Hessian determinant is
detD2F=8(3xy2−3x2z−z3).
(22)
At infinity the curve has only O=[0:1:0], which is an inflection point: its tangent is z=0 and the restricted equation is −x3=0. In the chart z=1, substitute y2=x3−x into the Hessian equation to obtain
3x4−6x2−1=0,x2=1±3​2​.
(23)
Thus the full answer is
O=[0:1:0],[x:y:1] with x2=1±2/3​,y=±x3−x​.​
(24)
The four complex values of x are distinct and none is 0,1,−1, so each gives two distinct nonzero values of y. Together with O these are all nine inflection points, exhausting the Bézout bound.

For a degree-d nonconstant holomorphic map from a compact connected Riemann surface of genus g to the projective line, the Riemann-Hurwitz formula is
2g−2=−2d+p∈X∑​(ep​−1),​
(25)
where ep​ is the local degree. A meromorphic function defines a map to the sphere by giving its ordinary finite values away from poles and value infinity at each pole. The reciprocal function is holomorphic in a neighborhood of a pole, proving holomorphicity there. For use in Riemann-Hurwitz the function must be nonconstant; a nonzero constant still defines a holomorphic map, but of degree zero.
Let X=C/Λ. The double pole of ℘ gives a degree-two map to the sphere. The torus has genus one, so total ramification is four. One simple ramification point is zero, above infinity. At the three nonzero half-period classes hi​, evenness and periodicity give ℘′(hi​)=0. Each is a ramification point of local degree two, and these account for all remaining ramification. Put ei​=℘(hi​). They are distinct, since a degree-two fiber cannot contain two points each of local degree two.
At each hi​, ℘−ei​ has a double zero and ℘′ has a simple zero. Consequently the elliptic quotient
R(z)=(℘(z)−e1​)(℘(z)−e2​)(℘(z)−e3​)℘′(z)2​
(26)
has removable singularities there and no zeros or poles elsewhere except possibly zero. Near zero, ℘=z−2+O(z2) and ℘′=−2z−3+O(z), so R→4. Thus R is holomorphic on the compact torus and constant, proving
℘′2=4(℘−e1​)(℘−e2​)(℘−e3​).​
(27)
The printed additional requirement that all three ei​ be nonzero is false. For the square lattice Λ=Z+iZ, invariance of the defining series under multiplication by i gives ℘(iz)=−℘(z). At h=(1+i)/2, one has ih=h−1, so periodicity gives ℘(h)=℘(ih)=−℘(h) and hence ℘(h)=0. This is one of the three branch values. Distinctness, which is all the cubic construction needs, remains valid.
Define
Φ(z)=[℘(z):℘′(z):1](z∈/Λ),Φ(0)=[0:1:0].
(28)
The image lies on Y2Z=4(X−e1​Z)(X−e2​Z)(X−e3​Z). Distinct roots make its affine part smooth, and the point at infinity is smooth because the derivative of its equation with respect to Z is nonzero there. Near zero the projective coordinates X/Y=℘/℘′∼−z/2 and Z/Y=1/℘′∼−z3/2 extend holomorphically. Every finite X/Z value has two preimages under ℘ except at a branch value. Evenness pairs those preimages as z,−z, and oddness of ℘′ distinguishes them unless they are the same half-period class. Therefore the two coordinates together give injectivity and reach both signs of Y, proving surjectivity onto the cubic. Thus Φ is a bijective holomorphic map to a nonsingular cubic, including when one branch value is zero.

A divisor on an algebraic curve is a finite formal sum D=∑np​p with integer coefficients. Its degree is degD=∑np​. The principal divisor of a nonzero meromorphic function is (f)=∑ordp​(f)p. For a nonconstant f, zeros and poles are fibers over zero and infinity of the same map to the sphere, each of total multiplicity equal to its degree; hence deg(f)=0. A nonzero constant has zero divisor.
Write L(D)={f:(f)+D≥0}∪{0} and ℓ(D)=dimL(D). The Riemann-Roch theorem states
ℓ(D)−ℓ(KC​−D)=degD+1−g,​
(29)
where KC​ is a canonical divisor. Taking D=p1​+⋯+pg+1​ gives ℓ(D)=2+ℓ(KC​−D)≥2. Constants span only one dimension, so there is a nonconstant f∈L(D). It has no poles outside these points and at most a simple pole at each. Thus the required nonconstant function exists, with actual poles at some nonempty subset because a holomorphic function on a compact connected curve is constant.

Since every pi​ is fixed, f∘φ has poles only among the first g+1 points, with order at most one: a biholomorphic change of coordinate preserves pole order. Thus h=f−f∘φ also has at most g+1 poles, counted with multiplicity. At every pi​ with i>g+1, both terms are finite and equal, so h(pi​)=0.
If h were not zero, its principal divisor would have degree zero. It would therefore have at most g+1 zeros counted with multiplicity. But it has at least n−(g+1) distinct zeros. When n>2g+2 this number exceeds g+1, a contradiction. Consequently
n>2g+2⟹f=f∘φ.​
(30)

Choose an actual pole pi​ of f; one exists by nonconstancy and compactness. In a local coordinate z centered there, its simple pole has expansion f(z)=a−1​/z+O(1) with a−1​=0. Hence
w(z)=1/f(z)=z/a−1​+O(z2),w′(0)=0.

The holomorphic inverse function theorem makes 1/f a local coordinate. Here the source notation f−1 denotes the reciprocal, rather than an inverse map of the meromorphic function.
The chosen pole is among the fixed points, so φ maps a sufficiently small neighborhood of it into the coordinate neighborhood. If f=f∘φ, then w=w∘φ there. Injectivity of this coordinate forces φ(x)=x throughout a nonempty open set. The stated identity principle then gives φ equal to the identity everywhere.
