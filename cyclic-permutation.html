Cyclic permutation ((a1​a2​…ak​))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Combinatorics Permutation
OurBigBook.com Words: 360
A cyclic permutation, also called a permutation cycle, sends k≥1 distinct elements a1​↦a2​↦⋯↦ak​↦a1​ and fixes every element outside this list. Its notation is (a1​a2​…ak​). For example, (1342) is the permutation with image list (3,1,4,2): all four labels lie on one directed loop. In contrast,
ρ=(13​24​31​42​)=(13)(24)
(88)
has two separate loops, exchanging 1 with 3 and 2 with 4. It is a permutation composed of two transpositions, rather than a single cyclic permutation.
Starting at a different point of the same loop changes the written list but preserves the cyclic permutation: (1342)=(3421). Reversing the direction generally changes the permutation; it gives the inverse function, here (1243). A transposition is a cyclic permutation of length two; a one-element permutation cycle records a fixed point. In a cycle decomposition of a permutation, one-element permutation cycles are usually omitted, with the underlying set understood.
The beauty of this representation is that repeated rearrangements become motion around independent loops. Applying (1342) four times returns every label to its start, while applying (13)(24) twice already does so. Disjoint permutation cycles commute under function composition, since each moves only labels the others fix. On n≥1 labels, there are (n−1)! cyclic permutations whose single loop contains every label: fix 1 as the first written entry and order the other n−1 labels freely, giving a factorial. This is also the count of cyclic orderings; choosing a starting position would otherwise count each loop n times. Rotations are identified here, while reversing direction is allowed to give a different cyclic ordering.
Proof outline for decomposition. Starting at any label in a finite set, repeatedly apply the permutation. The pigeonhole principle forces a repeated label. If the first repetition were at a label other than the starting one, its two predecessors would be different labels with the same image, contradicting the injective function property. This produces a permutation cycle returning to the start. Start again at a label not yet visited; the new permutation cycle cannot meet an earlier one, since the bijection gives every label a unique predecessor. Continue until every label belongs to a loop. The resulting cycle decomposition of a permutation is unique up to the order of the disjoint permutation cycles and the starting point written in each one. [ref]
