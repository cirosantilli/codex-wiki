Perfect elimination ordering

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory Chordal graph
OurBigBook.com Words: 683 Articles: 2
A perfect elimination ordering of a finite simple graph G=(V,E) is a permutation (v1​,…,vn​) of its vertices such that each set of later graph neighbours
Ni+​={vj​:j>i, {vi​,vj​}∈E}
(3)
is a clique. Thus vi​ is a simplicial vertex in the remaining induced subgraph G[{vi​,…,vn​}]. Empty and one-vertex sets satisfy the clique condition automatically. The order here is the order of deletion: v1​ goes first.
The characterization is powerful: a finite simple graph is a chordal graph if and only if it has a perfect elimination ordering. For one proof outline, the earliest vertex of an induced cycle of length at least four would have two later graph neighbours on that graph cycle that are not adjacent, contradicting the clique condition. Conversely, every nonempty chordal graph has a simplicial vertex, and every induced subgraph of a chordal graph is a chordal graph; repeatedly deleting such a vertex supplies the ordering.[ref]
In the square-plus-diagonal chordal graph above, (b,d,a,c) is a perfect elimination ordering, with later graph neighbourhoods {a,c}, {a,c}, {c}, and ∅. Starting instead with a fails: its graph neighbours b,c,d do not form a clique, because bd is absent. Thus even a chordal graph can have a poor elimination ordering.
For a larger example, join three triangles along shared edges, using
V={a,b,c,d,e},E={ab,ac,bc,bd,cd,ce,de}.
(4)
The exact graph neighbourhoods are N(a)={b,c}, N(b)={a,c,d}, N(c)={a,b,d,e}, N(d)={b,c,e}, and N(e)={c,d}. The ordering (a,b,c,d,e) has later graph neighbourhoods {b,c}, {c,d}, {d,e}, {e}, and ∅, all cliques; this certifies that the graph is a chordal graph without enumerating its graph cycles. Its maximal cliques {a,b,c}, {b,c,d}, {c,d,e} form a junction tree in that order, with intersections {b,c} and {c,d}.
To find an ordering, maximum cardinality search starts with all scores zero, repeatedly selects an unselected vertex of largest score, and increases each unselected graph neighbour's score by one. Reverse the selection order to obtain a candidate perfect elimination ordering. For the three-triangle graph, one valid selection sequence is (e,d,c,b,a), with scores at selection 0,1,2,2,2; reversing it gives the displayed deletion order. On a chordal graph every tie choice works. On an arbitrary graph, test the resulting later graph neighbourhoods for the clique condition to recognize chordal graphs. Suitable adjacency lists, score buckets and a linear-time verification method give O(∣V∣+∣E∣) recognition.[ref]
The deletion rule also explains sparse Gaussian elimination. For a symmetric positive-definite matrix whose matrix graph is G, eliminating pivot v applies the Schur complement update
auw​⟵auw​−avv​auv​avw​​.
(5)
Only pairs of remaining graph neighbours of v can acquire new off-diagonal entries. A perfect elimination ordering already has all those edges, so Cholesky decomposition creates no symbolic fill-in. Positive definiteness ensures nonzero pivots in exact arithmetic. Special numerical cancellation can suppress predicted entries, and floating-point rounding affects their values.[ref] In the three-triangle graph, deleting c first instead connects every pair in {a,b,d,e} and adds exactly ad,ae,be. The graph was already a chordal graph; the extra fill-in comes from the ordering.
One further reward is optimal graph coloring. Process a perfect elimination ordering backwards and give each vertex the smallest positive color absent from its already colored graph neighbours. Those graph neighbours form a clique, so this greedy algorithm uses at most the clique number ω(G) colors; any clique of that size requires at least that many. In the three-triangle graph, coloring (e,d,c,b,a) gives colors (1,2,3,1,2), so both the chromatic number and the clique number are three. Every maximal clique also occurs among {vi​}∪Ni+​: take its earliest vertex and use maximality. The same ordering therefore supports graph coloring, maximal clique extraction and sparse computation.
Historically, Donald J. Rose, Robert E. Tarjan and George S. Lueker's 1976 paper connected graph elimination to symmetric sparse matrices and gave a search-based O(∣V∣+∣E∣) algorithm for finding a perfect elimination ordering when one exists. Its abstract distinguishes a minimal elimination ordering from a minimum one: removing avoidable fill-in does not by itself minimize the total number of added edges.[ref]
