Internal direct product theorem

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Group theory Direct product of groups
OurBigBook.com Words: 259
An internal direct product finds independent coordinates already present inside a group. Let H,K be normal subgroups of G with
H∩K={e},HK=G.
(10)
Then the multiplication map
Φ:H×K⟶G,Φ(h,k)=hk
(11)
is a group isomorphism. Thus every element of G has a unique factorization into one element of each subgroup. Conversely, the embedded factors G×{eH​} and {eG​}×H inside an external direct product of groups satisfy these conditions.
For a concrete example, take the additive cyclic group C6​=Z/6Z and its subgroups H={0,3} and K={0,2,4}. They are normal subgroups because C6​ is an abelian group. Their intersection is {0}, and the sums h+k give every residue class exactly once. For instance, 1=3+4mod6. The table makes the unique decomposition visible.
The proof explains each hypothesis. Normality puts the group commutator [h,k]=h−1k−1hk in both H and K, so it is the identity element. Therefore the factors commute, and Φ preserves the group operation. The condition HK=G makes it surjective. If hk=e, then h=k−1 belongs to the intersection, forcing both to be the identity element; thus its group kernel is trivial and it is injective.
Normality cannot simply be omitted. In the symmetric group S3​, take H=⟨(123)⟩ and K=⟨(12)⟩. Their intersection is trivial and HK=S3​, but K is not a normal subgroup. With permutations composed right to left, (123)(12)=(13) while (12)(123)=(23), so the factors do not commute. The setwise decomposition is unique, but its multiplication map is not a group homomorphism from H×K. An independent-coordinate interpretation requires the group operations to agree, not merely a bijection of the underlying sets.
