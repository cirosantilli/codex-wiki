Second virial coefficient (B2​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Virial expansion
OurBigBook.com Words: 601 Articles: 4
The second virial coefficient is the leading interaction correction to the ideal gas law: at fixed temperature, B2​(T)=limn→0​(Z−1)/n, where Z=p/(nkB​T) and n is number density. For identical classical particles in a homogeneous three-dimensional gas, with an isotropic pair potential u(r) and r the centre-to-centre separation,
B2​(T)=−21​∫R3​f(∣r∣)d3r=2π∫0∞​[1−e−u(r)/(kB​T)]r2dr,f(r)=e−u(r)/(kB​T)−1.
(44)
The function f is the Mayer f-function. The factor 4πr2dr counts the volume of a thin spherical shell; the factor 1/2 counts each unordered pair once. The result has units of volume. Repulsive regions, u>0, contribute positively, while attractive regions, u<0, contribute negatively. [ref]
This is a striking bridge from a microscopic pair potential to a macroscopic pressure reading. At sufficiently small number density, B2​>0 raises pressure relative to an ideal gas at the same temperature and number density, while B2​<0 lowers it. A single value of B2​, however, cannot determine the entire pair potential: many different functions have the same weighted integral. Measurements over several temperatures supply more information.
The integral must exist. For example, a bounded short-range attraction outside a hard core has no difficulty. If instead u(r)∼A/rs as r→∞, then 1−e−u/(kB​T)∼u/(kB​T) and the radial tail behaves like ∫r2−sdr: it converges only for s>3. A bare unscreened Coulomb potential therefore falls outside this formula's ordinary short-range setting. This condition on the tail does not by itself establish the stability or convergence of the full virial expansion.
For an exactly integrable toy attraction, choose the following square-well pair potential, with diameter σ>0, attraction depth ϵ>0, and range multiplier a>1:
u(r)=⎩⎨⎧​+∞,−ϵ,0,​0≤r<σ,σ≤r<aσ,r≥aσ.​
(45)
Set T∗=kB​T/ϵ and bHS​=2πσ3/3, the second virial coefficient of a hard-sphere gas. Integrating the core and attractive shell separately gives the exact toy result
B2​(T)=bHS​[1−(a3−1)(e1/T∗−1)].
(46)
The core contributes bHS​; the shell contributes −bHS​(a3−1)(e1/T∗−1). Heating weakens the relative Boltzmann factor enhancement of attractive configurations, so B2​ approaches the positive hard-core value as T∗→∞. For the illustrative choice a=3/2, the zero occurs at
T0∗​=log(1+a3−11​)1​=log(27/19)1​≈2.846.
(47)
At this temperature, Z=1+B3​n2+⋯: cancellation removes the linear correction, not every correction. At low T∗, the negative shell contribution becomes large, requiring a correspondingly smaller number density for a useful truncated virial expansion. The square well is a deliberately chosen model, not a fit to the carbon dioxide experiment.
