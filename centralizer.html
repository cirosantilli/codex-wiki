Centralizer (CG​(x))

 ... Area of mathematics Algebra Group theory Group action Conjugation action Centralizer and normalizer
OurBigBook.com Words: 834 Articles: 6
The centralizer of an element x of a group G consists of all elements that commute with x:
CG​(x)={g∈G:gx=xg}={g∈G:gxg−1=x}.
(27)
It is the stabilizer subgroup of x under the conjugation action, hence a subgroup of G. In conjugacy classes of the symmetric group on three letters, the three representative computations are
CS3​​(e)=S3​,CS3​​((12))={e,(12)},CS3​​((123))={e,(123),(132)}.
(28)
For the transposition (12), a commuting permutation must preserve the exchanged pair {1,2} and fix 3; only e and (12) do so. For r=(123), a commuting permutation is determined by where it sends 1: the equation g(r(i))=r(g(i)) forces the images of 2 and 3, leaving exactly e,r,r2. For e, every permutation commutes. These centralizer sizes 6,2,3 give conjugacy class sizes 1,3,2.
The centralizer is useful because it measures how much freedom remains after fixing one operation. Its index of a subgroup counts the distinct results of conjugation, and the intersection of all element centralizers is the center of a group. A centralizer need not be an abelian group: CG​(e)=G even when G is a nonabelian group. In an abelian group, all element centralizers equal G. These observations also explain why the surrounding group matters: the centralizer of (123) inside the alternating group A3​ is the whole A3​, whereas its centralizer inside S3​ is a proper subgroup. See McKernan, the centralizer definition preceding lemma 23.6.
For an arbitrary subset A⊆G, the centralizer of a subset fixes every element of A individually under the conjugation action:
CG​(A)={g∈G:ga=ag for all a∈A}=⋂a∈A​CG​(a).
(29)
This is a pointwise stabilizer of the elements of A, not of the vertices of a polygon in a geometric realization. In particular, r=(123) belongs to CS3​​(r) although its rotation moves every vertex of a polygon of the equilateral triangle. If A generates a subgroup H, commuting with every element of A implies commuting with every product of those elements and their inverse elements; hence CG​(A)=CG​(H). For the triangle rotation subgroup H=⟨r⟩, this gives CS3​​(H)=CS3​​(r)=H.
To see the commutation test directly on the equilateral triangle, compare a rotation r with the reflection s=(12). Their permutations satisfy
rs=(13),sr=(23),srs−1=r−1.
(30)
The two orders send vertex of a polygon 1 to different places: rs(1)=3, but sr(1)=1. Thus s∈/CS3​​(r). By contrast r and r2 commute, with rr2=r2r=e. The reflection reverses the chosen rotation while preserving the subgroup of all triangle rotations, so s∈NS3​​(H). The distinction is visible before any counting: commuting preserves an operation itself; membership in the normalizer permits exchanging it with another operation in the same subgroup.
