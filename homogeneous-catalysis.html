Homogeneous catalysis

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Catalysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 432
In homogeneous catalysis, the catalyst and reactants occupy the same thermodynamic phase.
For a liquid example, specify hydrogen peroxide as the reactant, dissolved iodide as the catalyst, and water as the solvent. If both reacting chemical species remain dissolved in that one liquid thermodynamic phase, this is homogeneous catalysis. The University of Wisconsin–Madison's The Rate of a Reaction identifies iodide-catalysed hydrogen peroxide decomposition in its final example; the aqueous setting here makes the phase roles explicit.[ref] Its net chemical equation is
2H2​O2​(aq)⟶2H2​O(l)+O2​(g).
(25)
The molecular oxygen is a reaction product: its bubbles do not make the dissolved catalytic route heterogeneous catalysis. The classification compares the participating catalyst and reactants, rather than demanding that everything in the vessel share a phase.[ref]
Being in the same thermodynamic phase describes where the chemical species are; it does not specify their reaction mechanism. A uniform-looking liquid can contain unresolved solid particles, so appearance alone is insufficient. Conversely, suspended solid particles do not establish that their surfaces supply the observed catalysis: dissolved material released from them could contribute. Phase identification and catalytic participation need separate evidence.
The bookkeeping of a catalyst in homogeneous catalysis is particularly transparent in the schematic catalytic cycle A plus C forming AC, followed by AC releasing B plus C. This is the generic model in catalyst, not an asserted iodide reaction mechanism. Suppose a fixed volume initially contains 1.00mmol of A and 0.20mmol of C. At a chosen snapshot, let 0.60mmol of B and 0.12mmol of AC be present, with no other chemical reactions. Each AC contains one A unit and one C unit, so
nA​+nAC​+nB​=1.00mmol,nC​+nAC​=0.20mmol.
(26)
Thus free A is 0.28mmol and free C is 0.08mmol. B formation already exceeds the initial amount of substance of C by a factor of three, while the total C inventory remains 0.20mmol. Adding the two steps cancels C and AC, giving the net A-to-B chemical reaction; counting their constituents proves these balances. Conserved total catalyst does not mean constant free catalyst concentration.
Dissolved contact is useful because reactants can encounter the catalytic chemical species throughout the mixed liquid, without first reaching a macroscopic solid surface. Recovering a dissolved catalyst from the same chemical solution can instead require a separation. Neither ease of contact nor phase classification determines an activation energy or an equilibrium constant; those depend on the relevant chemical kinetics and endpoint thermodynamics.
