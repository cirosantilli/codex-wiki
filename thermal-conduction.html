Thermal conduction

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Heat
OurBigBook.com Words: 5k Articles: 28
Thermal conduction transfers heat through microscopic interactions without bulk transport of matter. To predict the heating load through a bar, combine Fourier's law with conservation of energy and the imposed end temperatures. In the isotropic model with positive thermal conductivity k, the conductive heat flux points from high to low temperature, opposite to the temperature gradient. A spoon warming along its handle and heat escaping through a wall are familiar examples.
Consider a bar of length L=0.10m, constant cross-sectional area A=0.010m2 and constant thermal conductivity k=2Wm−1K−1. Maintain its ends at Th​=40∘C and Tc​=20∘C, with x increasing toward the colder end. Assume a steady state, one-dimensional thermal conduction, no internal sources and no heat transfer through the sides. The temperature falls by 20K over 0.10m, giving
dxdT​=−200Km−1,q=−kdxdT​=400Wm−2,Q˙​=Aq=4W.
(4)
The difference between the two readings in degree Celsius units is the same 20-kelvin difference. The positive heat flux carries heat toward increasing x.
The general solution under these assumptions is
T(x)=Th​−LTh​−Tc​​x,q=Lk(Th​−Tc​)​,Q˙​=LkA(Th​−Tc​)​.
(5)
A proof outline is enough to explain the straight line: conservation of energy in any small slice, with no accumulation, sources or side losses, gives dQ˙​/dx=0. Constant cross-sectional area gives dq/dx=0; constant thermal conductivity and Fourier's law then give d2T/dx2=0. Two integrations and the end boundary conditions determine the displayed affine function. This is why a visually simple temperature profile can encode a continuous flow of energy. [ref]
Doubling the length halves the rate, while doubling the cross-sectional area doubles it, at fixed end temperatures and thermal conductivity. This explains why a long narrow support can limit unwanted heat transfer, while a short broad path can help remove heat. Joining materials leads to thermal resistance in series. During initial warming, internal energy can accumulate and the temperature profile need not be a straight line; the time dependence is described by the heat equation and thermal diffusivity.
Now join two layers, each of length L1​=L2​=0.10m, with constant cross-sectional area A=0.010m2 and stipulated constant thermal conductivities k1​=1 and k2​=4Wm−1K−1. Keep the outer ends at 40 and 20∘C. Assume a steady state, one-dimensional thermal conduction, no internal sources or side losses, and perfect contact: the temperature is continuous at the interface, with no extra contact drop. For each layer, use the area-specific thermal resistance ri​=Li​/ki​ and Ri​=ri​/A. The series calculation gives
r1​+r2​=k1​L1​​+k2​L2​​=0.100+0.025=0.125m2KW−1,R1​+R2​=Ar1​+r2​​=12.5KW−1,q=0.125m2KW−120K​=160Wm−2,Q˙​=Aq=1.6W,ΔT1​=qr1​=16K,ΔT2​=qr2​=4K,Tinterface​=40∘C−16K=24∘C.​
(6)
The heat flux is continuous: otherwise energy would accumulate at the interface. Yet Fourier's law gives different temperature gradients, dT/dx=−q/k1​=−160Km−1 and dT/dx=−q/k2​=−40Km−1. Lower thermal conductivity requires a steeper temperature gradient to carry the same heat flux; equal lengths therefore give drops in the ratio 4:1. A kink in the temperature profile records the change of material while perfect contact keeps the temperature itself continuous.
