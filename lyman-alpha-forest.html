Lyman-alpha forest

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Intergalactic medium
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 5
The Lyman-alpha forest is the multitude of foreground Lyman-alpha absorption features in a distant quasar's spectrum, at wavelengths shorter than its own redshifted Lyman-alpha emission. The quasar supplies a bright background; intervening neutral hydrogen atoms remove photons near their local Lyman-alpha line resonance. Many dips can therefore come from one atomic transition encountered at different redshifts, rather than from many different chemical elements. Some features are instead transitions of other ions, so a dip alone does not identify Lyman-alpha absorption. This makes faint foreground gas detectable through the light it removes, even when its own emission would be difficult to see. [ref].
Ignoring peculiar velocity for the moment, an absorber at cosmological redshift za​ puts its Lyman-alpha line at
λobs​=λα​(1+za​),za​=λα​λobs​​−1,λα​=1215.67A˚.
(5)
The rest wavelength is in the ultraviolet; at za​=2 it appears at 3647.01A˚ in the optical spectrum. In a toy sightline to a quasar at zQ​=3, absorbers at za​=2, 2.35, 2.7 appear at 3647.01, 4072.4945, 4497.979A˚, all shortward of the quasar's Lyman-alpha emission position 4862.68A˚. The absorbing atoms see the selected photons at their own resonance; the observed dip's redshift belongs to the foreground gas. Peculiar velocity adds a Doppler shift, so the inferred za​ is not a pure distance coordinate. The rest wavelength and resonance interpretation are given in Rauch (1998), section 1.

The smooth comparison explains the Gunn-Peterson effect: neutral hydrogen distributed throughout an expanding intergalactic medium can absorb continuously over a wavelength interval, because different parts of the path resonate with different observed wavelengths. The resolved Lyman-alpha forest instead has strong transmission fluctuations and distinguishable absorption lines. These need not represent isolated compact clouds: diffuse sheets and filaments in the cosmic web can generate peaks in optical depth along one line of sight. A continuously varying gas distribution can look like a collection of lines. [ref]. [ref].
An early observation makes the identification concrete. Lynds's 1971 paper analyzed six spectrograms of the quasar 4C 05.34, with emission redshift 2.877, taken in spring 1970 using an image-tube spectrograph on Kitt Peak's 84-inch telescope. Five covered 3200–7500A˚ with approximately 10A˚ resolution; the most informative covered about 3500–6000A˚ at 5A˚ resolution. Its reproduced spectrogram shows the strong Lyman-alpha emission flanked mostly on the short-wavelength side by numerous absorption lines. Lynds tabulated their wavelengths and estimated strengths, identified five absorption redshift systems, and proposed that many remaining unidentified lines were the same Lyman-alpha line at additional redshifts. He explicitly warned about blending, an incomplete census of weak lines, and wavelength errors around 0.5–1A˚. The result established a striking spectral pattern; the paper's proposed interpretation did not measure a separate physical cloud for every dip. [ref].
A contrasting observation demonstrates smooth, very strong absorption. Becker and collaborators (2001) used the Echelle Spectrograph and Imager on Keck II to observe the quasar SDSSp J103027.10+052455.0 at zQ​=6.28. Sky lines gave an instrumental full width at half maximum of about 1.8A˚ near 8500A˚, or spectral resolving power R≃4700. After sky subtraction and flux calibration, the mean continuum-normalized transmission over 8450–8710A˚ was 0.0038±0.0026, consistent with zero; this corresponds to absorption redshifts approximately 5.95–6.16. They identified a Gunn-Peterson trough. Crucially, resonant Lyman-alpha absorption is so sensitive that even a small neutral fraction can make transmission undetectable: the trough alone does not establish a mostly neutral intergalactic medium or uniquely date reionization. [ref].
What a spectrum constrains is a radiative transfer problem. For an ideal foreground screen with negligible emission into the observed beam, the intrinsic continuum-normalized transmission is
T(λ)=Fcont​(λ)F(λ)​=e−τ(λ),NHI​=∫nHI​dℓ.
(6)
Here F is spectral radiative flux, Fcont​ is its unabsorbed continuum, τ is optical depth, nHI​ is neutral hydrogen number density, and NHI​ is neutral hydrogen column density along proper path length ℓ. A Voigt profile model constrains redshift, column density and a velocity width together. A nearly black line has lost much of its sensitivity to further increases in optical depth; blending and an uncertain continuum can also admit different fitted components. Finite spectral resolving power and noise limit which widths and weak features can be measured. [ref]. [ref].
For a purely thermal Doppler core, use the velocity convention τ(v)∝exp[−(v−v0​)2/b2]. The Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution then gives
bth​=mH​2kB​T​​≃12.85104KT​​kms−1,
(7)
where kB​ is the Boltzmann constant, T is temperature and mH​ is a hydrogen atom's mass. This b is 2​ times the Gaussian distribution's standard deviation. Higher temperature spreads the same column density over more velocities. An instrument also broadens a line: locally, for a nearly flat continuum, the recorded transmission is approximately the convolution of T with the normalized instrumental response, not the exponential of a convolved optical depth. A Gaussian response with full width at half maximum c/R has standard deviation c/[R8log2​], where c is the speed of light and R the spectral resolving power.
The upper model fixes the integral ∫τ(v)dv, hence the neutral hydrogen column density for this transition, while changing temperature. A cooler line blurred by an instrument resembles a warmer intrinsic line. Both panels use invented profiles, Gaussian instrumental responses, and no noise or damping wings. They illustrate an inverse problem, rather than measurements of temperature or a universal relation between a dip and a cloud.
Better observations provide the inputs to that inverse problem. Kim, Cristiani and D'Odorico (2001) analyzed three quasars with the VLT's UVES spectrograph. For HE2217–2818, Table 1 records zQ​=2.413 and 16200s of exposure in the 3050–3860A˚ setup in October 1999. Isolated thorium-argon lamp lines measured R≃45000; the data were resampled into 0.05A˚ bins, with signal-to-noise ratios about 45–50 per pixel over 3550–4050A˚. They fitted a local polynomial continuum, fitted Voigt profiles, and compared statistics of the transmitted flux. Their analysis excluded the region near the quasar to avoid the quasar proximity effect and used the interval between its Lyman-beta and Lyman-alpha emission to reduce confusion with other Lyman-series absorption. Features below NHI​=1012.5cm−2 became confused with noise, setting their adopted lower column density limit. Figure 1 shows the measured spectrum, fitted profiles and residuals. This was a calibrated absorption measurement, with explicit selection limits. [ref].
Even after instrumental correction, a width need not be purely thermal Doppler broadening. Hubble flow across extended gas, peculiar velocity structure and unresolved blends can contribute. If an isolated line's intrinsic width obeys b2=bth2​+bnt2​ with an independent Gaussian nonthermal contribution bnt2​≥0, then T≤mH​b2/(2kB​); b=25kms−1 gives a computed upper bound about 3.79×104K. A complex blend need not satisfy this single-component model. Noise can also bias the fitted widths of weak lines. This is why the thermal cutoff of Lyman-alpha line widths, calibrated against gas models, is more informative than assigning a temperature to every dip. [ref].
Finally, neutral hydrogen column density is not total gas mass density. In a pure hydrogen approximation, with gas optically thin to ionizing radiation and nearly fully ionized in photoionization equilibrium, the Lyman-alpha forest photoionization scaling gives
nHI​≃Γα(T)nH2​​,xHI​≃Γα(T)nH​​,
(8)
where nH​ counts all hydrogen nuclei, Γ is the photoionization rate, α(T) is the radiative recombination coefficient and xHI​=nHI​/nH​ is the neutral fraction. At fixed temperature and Γ, doubling nH​ quadruples nHI​; doubling Γ halves it. These computed examples explain both the sensitivity to density contrast and the ambiguity: stronger absorption can mean denser gas, weaker ionizing radiation, a different temperature, or more path length. The Jeans-length ionization correction for the Lyman-alpha forest adds a size model rather than an independently measured depth. Optically thick Lyman limit systems and damped Lyman-alpha systems require different treatment. [ref]. [ref].
The beauty of the Lyman-alpha forest is that a spectrum converts otherwise faint foreground gas into a sequence of markers through cosmic time. With a model of radiative transfer, velocities, ionizing radiation and the temperature-density relation of photoionized intergalactic gas, its transmission statistics probe the cosmic web and gas thermal history. A single line of sight remains one narrow sample, not a unique three-dimensional density map. [ref].
