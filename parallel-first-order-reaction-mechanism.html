Parallel first-order reaction mechanism (AkP​​P,AkQ​​Q)

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics Reaction mechanism
OurBigBook.com Words: 277
A parallel first-order reaction mechanism consumes the same reactant through competing routes. Extend the product-split idea in catalytic selectivity by following both reaction products over time. Stipulate irreversible first-order reactions AkP​​P and AkQ​​Q in a uniformly mixed closed system of fixed volume and temperature. Initially only A is present, with concentration A0​. With K=kP​+kQ​,
[A]=A0​e−Kt,[P]=A0​KkP​​(1−e−Kt),[Q]=A0​KkQ​​(1−e−Kt).
(17)
Here K abbreviates a sum of rate constants, not an equilibrium constant. The rate equations are −d[A]/dt=K[A], d[P]/dt=kP​[A], and d[Q]/dt=kQ​[A]. Integrating the last two after solving the first proves the expressions and their conserved total [A]+[P]+[Q]=A0​.
Choose two alternative models: model U has (kP​,kQ​)=(0.30,0.10)s−1; model V has (0.10,0.30)s−1. Both have K=0.40s−1, so every measurement of A alone is identical, even with no measurement error. Yet their P-to-Q concentration ratios at any t>0 are 3 and 1/3, respectively. This is a precise failure of identifiability from that one observable. A calibrated measurement distinguishing P from Q resolves it within the stipulated two-route model.
At t=5.00s, both models give [A]=e−2A0​≈0.135335mmolL−1. Model U gives ([P],[Q])≈(0.648499,0.216166)mmolL−1; model V exchanges these. Measuring only the summed reaction product concentration would also fail to distinguish them. Product-specific observations therefore answer a question that total conversion cannot. As with any reaction mechanism, fitting these observables still does not rule out a more elaborate untested route.
