Between about 1880 and 1920 a recognizable investigation of subatomic entities emerged from work on electrical discharge, radiation and atomic model. Calling it physics is retrospective: it was not yet the later high-energy specialization with accelerators, and boundaries with atomic physics, nuclear physics and chemistry remained fluid. Its formation nevertheless had both scientific and institutional causes.
Better vacuum pumps, discharge tubes, high voltages, photographic plates and sensitive electrical instruments made new phenomena experimentally reproducible. Cathode rays could be deflected by electric and magnetic fields, letting investigators compare their charge-to-mass ratio under different conditions. In 1897 Joseph John Thomson argued that their carriers were common constituents of matter with far smaller mass than an atom. This made a divisible atom an experimental research problem rather than simply a speculative possibility.[ref]
Wilhelm Röntgen's X-rays, Henri Becquerel's spontaneous uranium radiation and the work of Marie Curie and Pierre Curie on strongly radioactive substances revealed additional emissions and transformations. Their classification by penetration, charge and deflection separated physically different entities. Radioactive decay introduced characteristic lifetimes and suggested that atoms could change identity. Chemical separation and quantitative electrical measurements were therefore as important as a purely mechanical picture of particles.[ref]
Rutherford scattering made the atom's internal structure accessible to an incisive quantitative probe. Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden measured rare large deflections, and Ernest Rutherford's 1911 analysis connected them to a concentrated nuclear charge rather than a diffuse one. The experimental task involved a collimated source, thin target foils and detection of individual scintillations at different angles; changing thickness and angle tested explanations of single versus multiple scattering. This style of inferring invisible structure from statistical distributions became characteristic of the developing field.[ref]
Specialization also depended on laboratories capable of maintaining apparatus, obtaining rare sources, training assistants and students, and circulating standardized measurements. Research schools sustained linked problems across many experiments; new journals, meetings and prizes gave their practitioners a recognizable identity. Industry supplied pumps and electrical equipment, while medical interest in radiation supplied additional demand and resources. These conditions help explain why the specialization consolidated in that period rather than emerging automatically from one discovery.
The date range should not imply completion. By 1920 the electron, nuclear electric charge, radioactivity and early nuclear transformations had established a new scale of questions, but the neutron, mature quantum mechanics and accelerator-based high-energy research still lay ahead. The emerging specialty was the joint product of experimentally identifiable new objects, a distinctive toolkit and institutions able to make their investigation a continuing career.
