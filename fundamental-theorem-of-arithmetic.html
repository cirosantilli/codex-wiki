Fundamental theorem of arithmetic (n=∏p∣n​pvp​(n))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory Prime number
OurBigBook.com Words: 786 Articles: 3
Every integer n>1 is a product of prime numbers, uniquely up to reordering the factors. More precisely, there are unique distinct prime numbers p1​<⋯<pr​ and unique positive integer exponents a1​,…,ar​ such that
n=p1a1​​⋯prar​​.
(5)
In the header's displayed form, vp​(n) is the prime valuation, the exponent of p in this prime factorization; it is zero when p does not divide n. Reordering or regrouping the prime factors does not give a different prime factorization.
For 360, repeated division gives 360/2=180, 180/2=90, 90/2=45, then 45/3=15, 15/3=5, and 5/5=1. Thus
360=2⋅2⋅2⋅3⋅3⋅5=23⋅32⋅5,23⋅32⋅5=8⋅9⋅5=360.
(6)
Its nonzero prime valuations are v2​(360)=3, v3​(360)=2 and v5​(360)=1. Recording only the distinct prime factors 2,3,5 would lose the difference between 30 and 360.
This turns divisibility into coordinatewise comparison of nonnegative exponents. A positive divisor of 360 chooses an exponent from 0,1,2,3 for 2, from 0,1,2 for 3, and from 0,1 for 5, giving 4⋅3⋅2=24 positive divisors. The greatest common divisor takes the smaller exponent at each prime number; the least common multiple takes the larger. The theorem is a structural description, not an assertion that finding the factors of every large integer is computationally easy.
In general, for positive integers d,n, the exact divisibility criterion is
d∣n⟺vp​(d)≤vp​(n) for every prime p.
(7)
For example, 72=23⋅32 divides 360, with quotient 5, whereas 48=24⋅3 does not: it requires four factors of two, but 360 supplies only three. Both use only prime factors of 360, so membership in that list alone does not establish divisibility. Every positive divisor of 360 is uniquely 2a3b5c with 0≤a≤3, 0≤b≤2, 0≤c≤1. Sorted, all 24 positive divisors are
1,2,3,4,5,6,8,9,10,12,15,18,20,24,30,36,40,45,60,72,90,120,180,360.​
(8)
Thus the divisor function gives τ(360)=(3+1)(2+1)(1+1)=24. These include divisors that are composite numbers, as well as the prime factors and one.
For another positive integer m, the prime valuations give exact formulas for the greatest common divisor and least common multiple:
vp​(gcd(n,m))vp​(lcm(n,m))​=min{vp​(n),vp​(m)},=max{vp​(n),vp​(m)}.​
(9)
With n=360 and m=84=22⋅3⋅7, their prime valuations at 2,3,5,7 are (3,2,1,0) and (2,1,0,1). The smaller exponents give (2,1,0,0), and the larger give (3,2,1,1), hence
gcd(360,84)=22⋅3=12,lcm(360,84)=23⋅32⋅5⋅7=2520.
(10)
Indeed, 360/12=30, 84/12=7, 2520/360=7 and 2520/84=30. The same rules also explain gcd(n,m)lcm(n,m)=nm: the smaller and larger exponents sum to the two original exponents. Here 12⋅2520=360⋅84=30240.
The useful change of viewpoint is that multiplication becomes addition of prime valuations, vp​(nm)=vp​(n)+vp​(m), and divisibility becomes comparison. Many different-looking calculations therefore share one representation. The prime valuations are coordinates for multiplication, rather than digits in positional notation: they do not turn ordinary addition of integers into coordinatewise addition.
Different intermediate splits lead to the same prime ingredients. Starting with 360=36⋅10 gives 36=6⋅6 and 10=2⋅5; starting with 360=8⋅45 gives 8=2⋅4 and 45=5⋅9. Continuing to split the composite numbers produces the same multiset of prime factors: three twos, two threes, and one five. The factorization tree records choices in the calculation, while the prime factorization records the invariant result.
The proof outline explains the uniqueness. Existence follows by splitting a composite number into smaller factors until only prime numbers remain. For uniqueness, Euclid's lemma says that a prime factor of one product must divide one factor of any other prime product equal to it. That factor must be the same prime number; cancel it and repeat. The crucial divisibility step appears in Book VII, Proposition 30 of Euclid's Elements. [ref]
The ancient text supplies specific precursors to the modern statement: Book VII, Proposition 31 establishes that every composite number has a prime factor, and Book IX, Proposition 14 excludes any new prime factor from the least common multiple of given prime numbers. These are the propositions being cited, rather than the complete modern exponent formulation of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic. [ref] [ref]
Excluding one from the prime numbers keeps uniqueness meaningful: otherwise 360=23⋅32⋅5=1⋅23⋅32⋅5=1⋅1⋅23⋅32⋅5 would give different collections of supposed prime factors. For n=1, the natural convention is the empty product, equal to one, with no prime factors and all prime valuations zero.
