a OurBigBook.com
At the initial negative membrane potential, most voltage-gated potassium channels have a closed activation gate. Depolarization moves charged residues in each voltage-sensing domain, particularly the S4 helices, through the electric field. Coupling between the voltage-sensing domains and the pore opens the intracellular gate. A classical voltage-gated potassium channel has four subunits, each with six transmembrane helices; S5, the pore loop and S6 contribute to the central pathway and selectivity filter. The rising open probability produces increasing outward potassium electric current, since the imposed positive membrane potential is above the potassium Nernst equilibrium potential. Here outward electric current is positive: IK​=NγpO​(V−EK​), where N is channel number and γ is single-channel electrical conductance.
The electric current subsequently falls because the rapidly inactivating Shaker form exhibits ball-and-chain inactivation. An intracellular N-terminal domain, tethered to the channel, blocks the open pore. The ion channel can therefore stop conducting while the membrane potential remains depolarized and the activation machinery remains activated. This is ion-channel inactivation, rather than reversal of the voltage step, loss of the ionic driving force, or ordinary gate closure. Repolarization favors removal of the block and recovery of channel availability. Slower C-type inactivation involving the outer pore can coexist, but is not the principal explanation of the rapid decay described here.
Several independent observations connect this functional explanation to structure. Small transient gating electric currents report movement of sensor electric charge before ionic conduction; charge-neutralization studies of S4/S2 change the measured gating charge and activation. Structural studies resolve separate sensor and pore regions.[ref][ref] Historical N-terminal removal abolished rapid ion-channel inactivation while preserving activation, and the corresponding free peptide restored block from the cytoplasmic side. These results separate the activation gate from the tethered blocker and identify which side of the cell membrane contains the blocking domain.[ref][ref]

For two-state ion-channel gating, write C⇄kc​ko​​O. Gain minus loss of open probability gives the ordinary differential equation
p˙​O​=ko​(1−pO​)−kc​pO​,pO​(t)=p∞​+[pO​(0)−p∞​]e−t/τ,p∞​=ko​+kc​ko​​,τ=ko​+kc​1​.
(1)
Thus the ratio of the transition rates determines the final open probability, while their sum determines the response speed. Both given channels have p∞​=1/2. For an initially closed population, with t measured in milliseconds,
pA​(t)=21​(1−e−t),τA​=1ms;pB​(t)=21​(1−e−10t),τB​=0.1ms.​
(2)
The mean individual open and closed lifetimes are 1/kc​ and 1/ko​: both are 2ms for A and 0.2ms for B. With the same channel number, single-channel electrical conductance and driving force, the steady macroscopic electric currents are equal, but B reaches that value ten times faster. If both populations were already at pO​=1/2, neither would show a mean relaxation under these fixed rates: a response also requires an initial condition or a change in rates.
The stationary variance of the number open is N/4 for independent channels. Its autocovariance is (N/4)e−∣t∣/τ, so B's fluctuations decorrelate ten times faster even though their instantaneous variance is unchanged. Faster gating allows an ion channel population to follow a brief stimulus and contribute to rapid action potential timing. Slower gating integrates over brief changes and can provide a delayed opposing electric current, useful in setting action potential duration or frequency selectivity. Those examples describe consequences of kinetics; the stipulated voltage-independent rates alone do not define a complete excitable neuron.

Add a nonconducting inactivated state to the continuous-time Markov chain:
C⇄kc​(V)ko​(V)​O⇄kr​(V)ki​(V)​I,pC​+pO​+pI​=1.
(3)
For example, p˙​O​=ko​pC​−(kc​+ki​)pO​+kr​pI​ and p˙​I​=ki​pO​−kr​pI​. Depolarization first increases pO​, then transfers population into I by ball-and-chain inactivation. A small recovery rate during the pulse permits a sustained decline of IK​=NγpO​(V−EK​). Repolarization favors recovery, eventually restoring the closed but available population. Several sequential closed states can represent the cooperative movements of four voltage-sensing domains; three states are the minimal explanation of rise followed by decay, rather than a quantitative fit to the missing trace.
Loss of outward potassium electric current weakens repolarization and can broaden an action potential. Reduced residual potassium electrical conductance can reduce the subsequent hyperpolarization and permit faster repeated firing. Conversely, prolonged depolarization can maintain sodium ion-channel inactivation, delay recovery and lower the maximum firing frequency or cause depolarization block. The sign of the frequency change depends on the other ion channels and recovery kinetics; rapid potassium ion-channel inactivation alone does not fix it.

Use the logic approximation for gene regulation, zero basal production and initially vanishing X, Y and Z. Let K=Kxy​=Kxz​=Kyz​=Kxx​/2, Y∞​=Z∞​=βy​/α, and assume the activated Q level exceeds the X activation threshold. The circuit combines a coherent feed-forward loop with negative autoregulation. While X is below its self-repression threshold it obeys X˙=βx​−αX, hence X first crosses K at
tX​=−α1​ln(1−βx​αK​).
(5)
Y production begins then. While X remains above K, Y=Y∞​[1−e−α(t−tX​)]. The AND gate for Z first becomes active when Y reaches K, so
Tmin​=tX​−α1​ln(1−βy​αK​)≃−α1​ln(1−βy​αK​).​
(6)
The approximation drops the short X build-up time because βx​≫βy​. Strictly the input must last longer than this onset time to produce a nonzero amount of Z. This requires βy​/α>K: at equality the threshold is approached only asymptotically, and below it Z never switches on. No numerical delay follows without the threshold-to-production ratio.
In the strong negative autoregulation limit, X is held close to Kxx​=2K; a smooth repression law regularizes the rapid switching of the ideal Boolean rule. After the input disappears at t0​, production of X stops and X≃2Ke−α(t−t0​). It takes (ln2)/α for X to fall below K. Until then Y remains at Y∞​>K and the Z AND gate stays on, so Z stays at Z∞​. After that Z decays by dilution, taking another (ln2)/α to halve. Therefore
tZ,1/2​−t0​≃α2ln2​.​
(7)
More generally replace the first ln2 by ln[X(t0​)/K]. This distinguishes disappearance of the external ligand from disappearance of the persistent X transcription factor.

M˙=r−KB​+MbM​.
(8)

M0​=VB​[B]−VR​[R]KB​VR​[R]​=b−rKB​r​.​
(9)

Attractant binding reduces receptor signalling, reducing CheA-dependent phosphorylation of CheY and initially reducing tumbling frequency, so smooth runs become longer. Let attractant reduce signalling activity per methylated receptor by a factor f∈(0,1), so the output is A=fM. Before stimulation take f=1 and M=M0​=KB​r/(b−r). Perfect adaptation requires the final methylated receptor concentration M′=M0​/f. With b′=VB′​[B], the new steady state condition is
r=KB​+M′b′M′​,VB′​=[B]r​(1+M0​fKB​​)=VB​M0​+KB​M0​+fKB​​.​
(10)
Equivalently b′=fb+(1−f)r. Since f<1, this reduces demethylation relative to the initial condition. Immediately after binding, activity and tumbling fall. The excess CheR flux then increases receptor methylation, compensating the activity loss until fM′=M0​. The methylated population must have sufficient remaining capacity to reach M′.
Increasing CheR concentration raises r and the baseline activity; increasing CheB concentration raises b and lowers it. If the attractant-dependent ratio c=VB′​/VB​ remains fixed after such a change, the new pre- and post-step outputs are
Abefore​=b−rKB​r​,Aafter​=cb−rfKB​r​.
(11)
Their equality requires c=f+(1−f)r/b. Thus a ratio tuned for one [R]/[B] generally loses perfect adaptation when either enzyme level changes independently. For a concrete example, initially r/b=1/2 and f=1/2 require c=3/4; changing r/b to 1/4 requires c=5/8, so keeping c=3/4 fails. Proportionally scaling both enzyme levels preserves the ratio and the adaptation condition, while changing response speed. This is fine tuning, rather than robust perfect adaptation.

In the simplified Barkai-Leibler model, let M be the total methylated receptor concentration and A=f(S)M the active, unoccupied part. Ligand-bound methylated receptors contribute zero activity. Because CheB acts only on active receptors,
M˙=r−KB​+AbA​,r=VR​[R],b=VB​[B].
(12)
At a finite steady state,
A∗​=b−rKB​r​,M∗​=f(S)A∗​​.​
(13)
The output A∗​ is independent of attractant concentration S: attractant changes the necessary receptor methylation level, while the flux balance fixes activity. This proves perfect adaptation for 0<r<b, f(S)>0 and enough methylatable receptors. At fixed S, dM˙/dM=−bf(S)KB​/[KB​+f(S)M]2<0, so this steady state is locally stable. If every receptor is permanently bound, f=0, or if the required M∗​ exceeds the available pool, compensation fails.
This feedback couples demethylation directly to the output. Lower activity lowers demethylation; continuing CheR action restores it. The structural dependence on active receptors removes the separate adjustment of VB′​ required in c.[ref]
Changing CheR or CheB concentration changes A∗​ and hence the baseline tumbling frequency, but the output still returns to that new baseline after an attractant step within the operating range. Raising CheR raises A∗​; raising CheB lowers it. Adaptation time also changes: linearization gives τ=(KB​+A∗​)2/[bf(S)KB​]. Thus robustness of the return property does not mean an invariant tumbling frequency or response time across different enzyme abundances.
If CheR instead has Michaelis-Menten kinetics on the unmethylated pool U=ET​−M, its flux is r(M)=VR​[R](ET​−M)/(KR​+ET​−M). Then
KR​+ET​−A/fVR​[R](ET​−A/f)​=KB​+AbA​.
(14)
The left side depends on ligand through f, even at fixed output A; consequently the equilibrium output generally depends on attractant and perfect adaptation is lost. It is recovered approximately when ET​−M≫KR​ throughout the response, so the CheR flux is effectively saturated.

Dense DNA packaging opposes three main physical effects. The negatively charged DNA backbones repel through electrostatic interactions, moderated by electrostatic screening and counterion condensation. At close approach, hydration forces and excluded volume become important. Bending a semiflexible DNA polymer on a radius comparable with its persistence length stores elastic energy. Confinement also removes configurational entropy, and concentrating mobile counterions can impose an additional osmotic pressure and free energy cost. Their relative importance depends on geometry and ionic conditions.
A schematic lytic bacteriophage life cycle is illustrated below.
Attachment recognizes a suitable host; delivery brings the genetic information into the host; host resources support production of components; assembly creates progeny that are released. Some bacteriophages instead have a latent integrated phase, but that is not a necessary stage of this lytic schematic.
In many double-stranded DNA bacteriophages, a portal-associated molecular motor uses ATP hydrolysis to move DNA into a preformed capsid. Chemical free energy pays for bending, repulsion and confinement, so the opposing force rises as filling proceeds. This is active packaging rather than spontaneous equilibrium compaction. Optical tweezers can infer that opposing force from displacement of a trapped bead and monitor packaging through the changing external DNA extension. Such measurements establish the mechanical principle without requiring any virus-handling procedure. The inverse spool model used below is an idealized packing geometry, not an exact structural claim for every bacteriophage.

The small binding RNA can serve conceptually as an RNA aptamer recognition probe. Coupling recognition to an optical label would make a specifically bound virus appear as a moving fluorescent spot. Single-particle tracking estimates successive spot positions and fits their displacement statistics. Specificity is assessed by comparison with a nonbinding recognition probe or a sample without the target, while the much faster diffusion of unbound probes distinguishes freely moving label from target-associated signal. Subnanomolar dissociation constant supports appreciable occupancy at low probe abundance, but affinity alone does not determine the dissociation lifetime because Kd​=koff​/kon​. This describes detection principles, not an operational virus preparation or labelling protocol.
For a dilute spherical particle of radius a=60nm in bulk water, the Stokes–Einstein relation gives, at an explicitly assumed T=300K,
D=6πηakB​T​=3.66μm2s−1.​
(21)
A 2nm free probe would have D≃110μm2s−1, roughly thirty times larger. The recognition probe changes the target's hydrodynamic size only modestly in this estimate.
For isotropic Brownian motion, the mean vector displacement is zero. In t=0.1s, distinct measures of how far it moves are
⟨∣Δr∣2⟩​=6Dt​=1.48μm,⟨∣Δr∣⟩=4Dt/π​=1.37μm.​
(22)
A two-dimensional image instead gives projected root-mean-square displacement 4Dt​=1.21μm and mean projected distance πDt​=1.07μm. The projected mean squared displacement is 4DΔt; independent localization errors of variance σ2 in each coordinate add 4σ2. Finite exposure blurs motion, and nearby walls reduce diffusion; those effects must be separated from particle size when interpreting a trajectory.

A major source of ATP is chemiosmosis: an electron transport chain uses downhill electron transfer to establish a proton-motive force, and ATP synthase converts that electrochemical gradient into ATP formation. In aerobic oxidative phosphorylation, reduced carriers supply electrons, ultimately reducing oxygen to water; selected respiratory complexes couple the favorable oxidation-reduction reaction to uphill proton translocation. In a mitochondrion, this takes place across the inner membrane, creating a relatively positive, acidic exterior side and a relatively negative, alkaline matrix. Bacteria use their cell membrane. In photosynthetic systems, light-driven electron transfer can supply the gradient instead. Substrate-level phosphorylation is another ATP-producing route, so the description is not universal to every organism and condition.
The membrane FO​ region of ATP synthase contains a rotating ring of c-subunits next to subunit a. Two offset aqueous half-channels expose the ring's proton-binding sites to opposite membrane sides. Proton binding and release bias ring rotation down the electrochemical gradient. The central shaft transfers torque to the F1​ catalytic head, while a peripheral stator prevents the head from simply co-rotating. This is the ATP synthase rotary mechanism, rather than unrestricted proton leakage through a pore.
The three catalytic beta subunits cycle through different binding states as the shaft turns: ADP and inorganic phosphate bind, bound ATP is favored, then ATP is released. The binding-change mechanism uses mechanical work to alter binding affinities and product release as well as the chemical step. A full coupled revolution produces three ATP molecules; a ring with nc​ c-subunits transfers approximately nc​ protons per revolution in the tightly coupled model, or nc​/3 per ATP.[ref][ref]
The overall cycle must have a favorable Gibbs free energy change. If the proton-motive force becomes too small, synthesis stops; under suitable conditions the same molecular motor can reverse, using ATP hydrolysis to pump protons uphill. A catalyst changes kinetics, not the thermodynamic condition for synthesis.

The given probability density function is the completion-time density for two independent sequential irreversible steps, with exponential distributions of waiting times at rates ka​,kb​>0. Let T=Ta​+Tb​. The convolution is

The observation supports at least two kinetically resolved stages before the recorded event, rather than one memoryless rate-limiting event. Magnetic tweezers apply force and controlled turns to a single torsionally constrained DNA tether joining a surface and a magnetic bead. A magnetic-field gradient pulls the bead; rotation of the field rotates the bead and changes imposed turns. Imaging measures bead height, hence DNA extension. At sufficient excess twisting and suitable force, DNA supercoiling creates writhed loops that shorten the tether. The extension-versus-turn curve supplies a calibration relating the removal or addition of supercoiling to vertical motion. Multiple attachments at both ends are needed conceptually to constrain rotation; freely swivelling DNA would relax the signal.
During transcription initiation, RNA polymerase binds a promoter in a closed complex and then opens a local DNA bubble, exposing template for RNA synthesis. In bacterial initiation a sigma factor helps recognize the promoter. Opening changes local helical twist. Because the total linking number is constrained,
Lk=Tw+Wr,ΔTwrest​+ΔWr=−ΔTwbubble​.
(40)
Unwinding the bubble means ΔTwbubble​<0, so the remainder acquires a positive turn increment. A negatively supercoiled tether therefore loses some negative writhe and becomes longer; a positively supercoiled tether gains positive writhe and becomes shorter. Ordinary DNA compaction instead shortens both tethers. Comparing both signs separates local unwinding from compaction rather than assigning every height change to opening.
If q is the number of turns unwound, s the magnitude of the extension change per imposed turn, and c the compaction, the symmetric-slope approximation predicts Δz−​=sq−c and Δz+​=−sq−c. Hence
q=2sΔz−​−Δz+​​,c=−2Δz−​+Δz+​​.​
(41)
Measured unequal slopes can be retained in the two separate calibration equations. Multiplying q by the helical repeat converts turns to an estimated number of unwound base pairs, subject to the mechanical model and the distinction between twist and writhe.
Repeated extension transitions reveal open-complex lifetimes, closed-complex lifetimes and their dependence on mechanical torque. Initial short RNA synthesis can occur while RNA polymerase remains associated with the promoter, including abortive initiation and DNA scrunching. Addition of nucleotides allows progression to promoter escape; an unwound bubble can then persist with elongation, so loss of the bubble is not the definition of escape. Successive cumulative unwinding events can identify renewed access to the same promoter after clearance. These are the measurement principles demonstrated by the paired-sign experiments.[ref]
The experiment observes mechanics rather than directly imaging individual opened base pairs. Drift, nonspecific binding, tether integrity, force-dependent buckling and limited time resolution can mimic or obscure steps. A control lacking productive promoter recognition and agreement of the opposite-sign responses help identify the origin of a signal.

A point mutation changes an individual nucleotide position, introducing heritable sequence variation. Replication errors and spontaneous chemical damage are possible origins; mutation does not occur because a future change would be useful. Natural selection changes the frequencies of variants through their effects on reproduction, while genetic drift causes random frequency changes, especially in small populations. A sequence difference fixed between lineages is a substitution; many new mutations disappear and never become substitutions. Under a simple neutral diploid model, 2Ne​μ new neutral variants per generation times fixation probability 1/(2Ne​) gives substitution rate μ, motivating a molecular clock for suitable neutral sites.
To infer a phylogenetic tree, compare homologous, preferably orthologous sequences and align corresponding sites. A nucleotide substitution model converts observed sequence differences into estimated evolutionary branch lengths, allowing for multiple changes at one site. For example, in the equal-frequency, equal-rate Jukes-Cantor model, a site changes to any alternative with rate μ/3, where μ is its total substitution rate. The probability of matching its initial state after elapsed time t is 1/4+(3/4)e−4μt/3. Thus for an evolutionary separation with expected substitutions per site d, the observed mismatch fraction is p=(3/4)(1−e−4d/3) and
d=−43​ln(1−34p​).​
(42)
For small p, d≃p; near saturation p=3/4, large evolutionary distances become difficult to distinguish. The correction does not rescue fully saturated data. Real analyses may allow unequal nucleotide frequencies, transition biases and variation of rates between sites.
Tree topology and branch lengths can be estimated using maximum likelihood estimation or Bayesian phylogenetic inference, with support assessed from resampling or posterior uncertainty. A gene tree need not equal the species tree, because incomplete lineage sorting, genetic recombination and horizontal gene transfer can produce different histories. Rooting with an appropriate outgroup establishes ancestral direction; pairwise differences alone do not supply a root.
Branch lengths measured in substitutions per site are not yet calendar times. Under a strict molecular clock with rate μ per site per year, a branch of duration t has length μt; two contemporaneously sampled descendants separated at time t have total path length d=2μt, hence t=d/(2μ)​. Calibration comes from independently dated constraints such as fossil calibration, geological information or sufficiently informative sampling dates. A dated common ancestor is not automatically identical in age to the earliest observed fossil. A relaxed molecular clock permits branch-specific rates while imposing a statistical model on them.
Without an external temporal calibration or an independently known rate, multiplying all times by a constant and dividing all rates by that constant leaves expected sequence divergence unchanged. Absolute dates are therefore unidentifiable from sequence divergence alone. A useful time-calibrated phylogenetic tree reports uncertainty from sequence sampling, substitution models, rate variation, topology and calibration bounds, rather than displaying an unjustified exact date for each ancestor.

An ion channel supplies a water-accessible pathway through the otherwise strongly insulating, hydrophobic lipid bilayer. Moving a hydrated ion directly into that low-permittivity region is energetically expensive. The channel's polar pore and coordinating groups reduce the barrier while retaining selectivity. Transport is passive: the electrochemical potential difference drives net movement, and the channel does not itself pump an ion uphill. For an ideal species of charge number z,
Δμ​out→in​=RTlncout​cin​​+zFV.
(43)
The net electric current of a pathway permeable only to that species reverses at its Nernst equilibrium potential. An open channel can carry zero net electric current at that voltage even while individual ions cross both ways.
The selectivity filter balances loss of the hydration shell against favorable coordination within the pore. In a typical potassium channel, backbone carbonyl oxygen atoms create appropriately arranged binding sites for a substantially dehydrated potassium ion. The smaller sodium ion is not automatically preferred simply because it fits geometrically: its hydration is stronger and the filter does not compensate its dehydration in the same way. Geometry, flexibility, coordination chemistry and ion occupancy all matter. Sodium-selective and calcium-selective channels use different arrangements, often involving charged side chains; a single rigid-sieve explanation is inadequate.
Rapid passage coexists with transient binding because several nearby sites have comparable free energies and relatively low barriers between them. Multiple ions occupying a pore influence one another through electrostatic interactions, enabling correlated exchange and displacement rather than irreversible trapping at one deep site. High throughput therefore requires favorable passage kinetics as well as strong discrimination against unwanted species. A more tightly bound species can even block a pathway instead of being its fastest permeant.
Gating and selectivity are distinct. A voltage-sensing domain couples membrane potential to activation-gate conformation; ligand-gated ion channels couple binding to opening; mechanosensitive ion channels couple membrane stress to opening. Ion-channel inactivation introduces additional unavailable states. None of these mechanisms alone identifies which ion the open pore prefers.
For N similar independent channels, a local Ohmic approximation with outward-positive electric current is
I=NpO​γ(V−Erev​).​
(44)
Here open probability controls availability, single-channel electrical conductance controls current per open pore, and V−Erev​ is the electrical driving factor in this approximation. Nonlinear current-voltage relations and mixed-ion permeation may require a fuller model. Single-channel recording resolves discrete openings; reversal shifts when ionic concentrations change test selectivity; gating electric currents test sensor motion; structural evidence identifies the pore and its coordinating groups. Together these distinguish rapid selective permeation from the separate process of opening the gate.

A small swimming member of the bacteria samples too little spatial separation across its own body to compare weak chemical gradients accurately. Temporal sensing in bacterial chemotaxis instead compares recent receptor activity with an adapted memory. Straight runs carry it between locations; tumbles reorient it. When conditions improve, tumbling becomes less frequent and favorable runs last longer. An unfavorable direction is abandoned sooner. Repetition creates a biased random walk without requiring the cell to identify the best direction at each tumble. Rotational diffusion and environmental obstacles eventually destroy the benefit of maintaining a direction indefinitely.
An order-of-magnitude transport estimate compares the time ℓ/v to swim a distance ℓ with the time ℓ2/D for an attractant to diffuse that distance. Their crossover is a Péclet number of order one:
vℓ​∼Dℓ2​,ℓ∼vD​=301000​μm≃33μm,τ∼v2D​≃1.1s.​
(45)
This estimates a useful run scale: over much shorter distances, nutrient diffusion rapidly replenishes the local environment compared with the organism's displacement. Runs of order a second also permit comparison along a substantial path before reorientation. The given D and v determine this scale, not an exact biological mean run length or a complete sensory optimization. If a one-coordinate root-mean-square diffusion length 2Dτ​ is equated with vτ, the resulting prefactor is two; a three-dimensional root-mean-square convention gives six. Such constants cannot be fixed by a scaling argument alone.
For a cilium of length d, compare its advective time d/u with the local diffusion time d2/D. Significant stirring relative to diffusion requires Ped​=ud/D≳1, so
u≳dD​=103μms−1=1mms−1(d=1μm).​
(46)
This is about 33 times the stated swimming speed. If the tip travelled on a circular path of radius d, that speed would correspond to an illustrative frequency u/(2πd)≃160Hz. That geometric conversion is not a specified beat pattern. At microscopic scales the flow has small Reynolds number; useful transport depends on the actual nonreciprocal motion and resulting flow, and rapid motion alone does not guarantee increased uptake. The scaling explains why simply stirring with a very short appendage is demanding when molecular diffusion is already fast.
