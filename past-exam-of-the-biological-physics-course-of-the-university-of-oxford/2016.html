Treat the membrane as a capacitor, and count the additional positive charge on its inner surface. With radius r=10−6 m and length ℓ=10−4 m, including both end caps gives
A=2πrℓ+2πr2=6.3460×10−10 m2,V=πr2ℓ=3.1416×10−16 m3.
(1)
The membrane capacitance is C=Cm​A=6.3460 pF. Thus ΔQ=CΔV=5.3941×10−13 C, corresponding to ΔN=ΔQ/e=3.3667×106 monovalent sodium ions. Converting charge to amount of substance using the Faraday constant,
ΔcNa​=FVCm​AΔV​=0.01780 molm−3=0.01780 mM,15 mMΔcNa​​=1.186×10−3.​
(2)
Omitting the ends changes the answer to 0.01762 mM, a one-percent correction. A large membrane potential change therefore requires only about 0.12% of the intracellular sodium population to enter. The bulk liquid remains nearly electrically neutral; excess charge resides chiefly near the cell membrane. This explains why an action potential need not exhaust the concentration difference that drives it, and why fixed reversal potentials are a useful approximation during one spike. This membrane capacitive ion budget assumes sodium supplies the minimum net charging requirement. Simultaneous opposing electric currents can require more sodium entry; repeated spikes eventually require the sodium-potassium ATPase to maintain the gradients.

A typical voltage-gated potassium channel has four subunits, each with six membrane-spanning segments. S1–S4 form a voltage-sensing domain; S5–S6 and their intervening loop form the pore and selectivity filter. Conserved positively charged side chains along S4 move through part of the membrane electric field when the voltage changes. Countercharges and hydrated crevices in the surrounding sensor help make this movement possible. Coupling between the sensor and pore, including the S4–S5 region and S6 gate, changes the probability of opening. A voltage-gated sodium channel usually combines four related domains in one large protein, rather than four identical independent proteins.
The evidence separates charge movement, structural arrangement and functional coupling. A transient ion-channel gating current can persist when permeating ionic electric current is suppressed: some charged parts of the channel itself move. Comparing integrated ion-channel gating charge with channel opening tests their coupling. Charge-neutralization experiments change both total moved charge and voltage dependence, implicating S4 basic residues and countercharges elsewhere; they do not show that S4 alone supplies every charge. [ref]
Crystal structure of a mammalian voltage-dependent Shaker family K+ channel by Stephen B. Long and others supplies structural evidence for the arrangement. Voltage-dependent accessibility and optical reports of sensor motion provide complementary functional tests. No one static structure is a movie of gating, and a perturbation that abolishes current can disrupt folding or pore function rather than selectively remove voltage sensing. Agreement between structural, electrical and motion-sensitive measurements supports the mechanism more strongly than any one observation alone.

Write the instantaneous electrical conductance as gK​(t)=gˉ​K​n(t)4. Here gˉ​K​ is the maximal electrical conductance of the recorded membrane, n is the activation variable, Vm​ is inside-minus-outside membrane potential, and VK​ is the potassium Nernst equilibrium potential. The factor Vm​−VK​ is the electrochemical driving voltage; positive IK​ conventionally denotes outward positive charge. For an electric current density, gˉ​K​ must instead be an electrical conductance per area.
In the Hodgkin-Huxley model, four equivalent independent activation gates must all be permissive, each with probability n, so their joint opening probability is n4. At fixed voltage each gate obeys
n˙=αn​(Vm​)(1−n)−βn​(Vm​)n,n∞​=αn​+βn​αn​​,τn​=αn​+βn​1​.
(3)
The model assumes approximately Ohmic open-channel conduction, a well-defined population-average electrical conductance, and an almost constant potassium reversal potential during a brief recording. Four phenomenological gates should not be mistaken for a proof of four physically independent molecular transitions. The simple delayed-rectifier description also omits ion-channel inactivation.
Reducing the available electrical conductance can weaken repolarization, broaden an action potential and alter repetitive firing. Depending on channel class and tissue, excess excitability or impaired recovery can follow; persistent depolarization can also reduce voltage-gated sodium channel availability. Cardiac potassium-current reductions can prolong the action potential and contribute to Long QT syndrome. These are conditional physiological consequences, not a claim that every potassium-current reduction has the same clinical effect.

A useful organizing principle is Gibbs free energy: at specified temperature and pressure a coupled process is favorable when its total ΔG is negative. For each chemical species,
μi​=μi∘​+RTlnai​,ΔG=ΔG∘+RTlnQ.
(6)
The relative amounts of chemical substances determine the available Gibbs free energy, beyond counting energy in individual bonds. Nutrient oxidation and light-driven chemistry can maintain nonequilibrium compositions. ATP hydrolysis can drive biosynthesis, transport and motion of molecular motors when reactions are coupled so that their total free-energy change is favorable. Breaking an inorganic phosphate bond itself costs energy; the favorable net hydrolysis includes formation and solvation of the products and depends on their thermodynamic activities.
Redox Gibbs free energy comes from transferring electrons between donors and acceptors: ΔG=−nFΔE. Electron transfer in respiration or photosynthesis can help establish an electrochemical gradient. For a mole of ions moving from side 1 to side 2,
Δμ​=RTln(a2​/a1​)+zF(ψ2​−ψ1​).
(7)
The concentration and electrical terms together drive transport and can power ATP synthase. ATP-driven pumps can conversely build gradients. Chemiosmosis is the coupling between this gradient and chemical synthesis, not the creation of Gibbs free energy by an enzyme.
Mechanical work and elastic storage are other forms: a motor can do ∫Fdx or ∫τdθ, and an approximately Hookean element stores kx2/2. ATP-driven motors convert Gibbs free energy into motion, while externally forced reverse motion can sometimes drive chemical synthesis. A photon brings energy hν; light photon absorption initiates chemistry in photosynthesis and light-driven pumps, with some energy inevitably dissipated. Living cells use these conversions for structure, movement, homeostasis and synthesis, while releasing heat and exporting entropy. At a single uniform temperature, dissipated heat cannot be completely recycled into useful work.

At saturating ATP, binding need no longer dominate the observed dwell, but saturation alone does not determine a dwell-time distribution. The standard interpretation of a distribution that rises from zero is that at least two sequential events remain rate limiting. Model a dwell as T=T1​+T2​, where the independent waiting times are distributed according to exponential distributions with rates k1​,k2​. Their convolution is
p(t)=∫0t​k1​e−k1​uk2​e−k2​(t−u)du=k2​−k1​k1​k2​​(e−k1​t−e−k2​t)​,t≥0.
(8)
This hypoexponential distribution starts at zero, rises to a mode ln(k2​/k1​)/(k2​−k1​), then has an exponential tail. When the rates are equal, the continuous limit is the two-stage Erlang distribution,
p(t)=k2te−kt,⟨T⟩=2/k,var(T)=2/k2,tmode​=1/k.​
(9)
A single slow memoryless event instead gives ke−kt, maximal at zero. Three 120-degree steps per revolution specify rotational stoichiometry, not three slow reactions within every dwell. The two-event interpretation is supported by the resolved catalytic-dwell chemical kinetics in the primary F1 experiment.[ref]
The fit assumes sequential approximately irreversible events, constant rates during each dwell, negligible ATP-binding delay, negligible mechanical transit time, and detection of all relevant steps. Parallel pathways, backward transitions, inhibited states, probe drag and missed short dwells can change the histogram. The mathematical fit alone does not identify each event chemically or prove that two ATP molecules are hydrolyzed per step.

Use signed ion valence z and define V=ψ1​−ψ2​. The molar electrochemical potential in side i of an ideal dilute solution is
μ​i​=μ∘+RTln(ci​/c∘)+zFψi​.
(13)
Transfer of an infinitesimal amount dn from side 1 to side 2 changes the total Gibbs free energy by (μ​2​−μ​1​)dn. At equilibrium this derivative is zero, so
RTln(c1​/c2​)+zF(ψ1​−ψ2​)=0,V=zFRT​lnc1​c2​​.​

For nonideal solutions the ratio must be a ratio of thermodynamic activities. The temperature and solvent must be common to both reservoirs, and an ion must actually be able to exchange for this equilibrium condition to be dynamically established.
Equal compartment volumes are unnecessary for deriving the voltage law, but help specify number conservation if concentration changes are appreciable. For equal volume V and a monovalent cation transfer n from side 1 to 2, c1​=c10​−n/V, c2​=c20​+n/V. Suppose initially c1​>c2​. Positive ions initially move by diffusion from side 1 to side 2. Since negative counterions cannot cross, side 1 acquires a negative interfacial charge and side 2 a positive one. The resulting electrical force opposes further positive-ion transfer. The two contributions balance when the electrochemical potentials are equal; individual ions still cross in both directions, but there is no net electric current of the permeant chemical species.
A simple capacitor model makes the feedback quantitative. If n moles of monovalent positive ions move from initially neutral side 1 to side 2, the charge on side 1 is −Fn, hence V=−Fn/C. The free-energy stationarity condition is
RTlnc20​+n/Vc10​−n/V​−CF2n​=0.
(15)
As n increases, both the concentration imbalance and the voltage drive toward reversal diminish the original diffusion tendency. The equilibrium lies before the bulk concentrations become equal. This demonstrates why a nonzero Nernst potential can coexist with very little change in bulk composition and approximate bulk electroneutrality. A concentration difference alone does not maintain an electrical voltage if both counterions can freely follow each other; selective permeability is essential to this model.

At a common membrane voltage, an ion is in electrochemical equilibrium only if its own Nernst potential equals that voltage. The four independently calculated equilibrium voltages are
EK​=−97.74 mV,ENa​=+66.60 mV,ECa​=+130 mV,ECl​=−90 mV.​
(19)
Thus at Vm​=−90 mV only chloride is at its listed equilibrium voltage. Potassium has an outward positive-current driving voltage Vm​−EK​=+7.74 mV. Sodium and calcium have negative driving voltages, −156.60 and −220 mV respectively, favoring inward positive-ion movement when their channels are open. Chloride has zero net electrochemical drive under these ideal conditions.
A resting potential is not a claim that every permeant chemical species is in thermodynamic equilibrium. A membrane with several electrical conductances can have zero total electric current even though individual ionic electric currents flow and cancel. Pumps maintain the concentration differences by consuming Gibbs free energy. Conversely, a closed channel can prevent substantial ion flux even when the ion's electrochemical potential difference is nonzero: absence of flux due to impermeability is not the same as electrochemical equilibrium. The listed concentrations could not all be passive equilibrium distributions at one voltage because their Nernst potentials differ.

A rising force branch means that an already exposed polypeptide segment is being stretched while the remaining structure resists further extraction. At a force peak a structural barrier is crossed; protein denaturation or release of additional chain increases the available polymer contour length, so the force falls at almost the same imposed separation. Stretching then loads the next remaining barrier. The sawtooth pattern therefore reports a sequence of mechanically resistant intermediates, not a regular spring oscillation. The cantilever deflection gives force, while its own deformation must be removed from the imposed separation to infer molecular extension.
A membrane protein also requires work to disrupt membrane anchoring, helix packing and contacts with lipids or neighboring molecules. The retinal-containing interior contributes a bulky, specifically packed scaffold; exposing chain must overcome these steric hindrance as well as hydrogen bond and electrostatic interactions. A force peak need not correspond one-to-one to one complete helix, and its height depends on loading rate and pathway, not just an equilibrium folding Gibbs free energy.
Salt changes electrostatic screening and specific ion binding. Screening can reduce either repulsion or attraction, so “more salt always weakens the protein” is false. The primary study underlying these kinds of traces identifies relatively salt-insensitive steric hindrance around the retinal and additional salt-sensitive barriers. Its interpretation associates some added barriers with screening of destabilizing electrostatic repulsion. The supplied traces likewise show a changed set of intermediate peaks, rather than a uniform rescaling of one curve.[ref]
Exact assignment of every arrow to a residue requires structural and statistical information beyond one representative trace. No such assignment is inferred here. The printed reference to titin in the following paragraph is inconsistent with the stated specimen and the membrane-protein traces; the physical interpretation here concerns bacteriorhodopsin, while the worm-like-chain expression is a generic polymer approximation.

On each branch the worm-like chain force-extension interpolation relates force to the extension of the already released chain. Fitting a sufficiently long branch estimates its polymer contour length L and, if the data constrain it independently, its persistence length Lp​. In practice Lp​ may be fixed across branches so that the changes in L are better identified. Consecutive fitted polymer contour lengths reveal how much additional chain became available at an protein denaturation event; using a justified polymer contour length per residue can then estimate residue counts. A projected folded length and the pulling geometry must be included before assigning a structural boundary.
Writing u=x/L, expansion at small extension gives
f=Lp​kB​T​(23​u+43​u2+⋯),keff​=dxdf​​0​=2Lp​L3kB​T​.
(20)

The main generic costs of strongly confining a charged semiflexible polymer are bending it away from its preferred straight shape, crowding neighboring segments, and reducing its available configurations. Closely approaching charged strands also repel through electrostatic and hydration interactions; mobile ions screen or modify these interactions, while counterion organization contributes to the free-energy balance. The bending cost is especially large in regions of high curvature. A pressure on the confining wall and a force resisting further insertion are related thermodynamic responses, but are not identical quantities with the same units.
At the level of measurement principles, an optical tweezers apparatus acts as a calibrated compliant force sensor. A displaced trapped bead experiences approximately Ftrap​=ktrap​Δx. A tether transmits tension between that sensor and the material under load; the opposing support fixes the other end. The conceptual force balance is
compliant sensor ⟷ tension-bearing tether ⟷ confined polymer system.
(21)
The extension and restoring force supply a mechanical record of the load. This is a force-sensing schematic, not a biological preparation or packaging protocol. The measured tension can include bending, interstrand interactions, dissipation and any other mechanically coupled contribution; it cannot be identified with only the bending term without additional assumptions. In a reversible generic model Fconf​=∂G/∂L at specified geometry, while an internal pressure is −∂G/∂V at specified polymer content. These derivatives clarify what a force measurement can and cannot determine.

A Jablonski diagram distinguishes electronic excitation from subsequent relaxation. Photon absorption takes a molecule from the electronic ground state S0​ to a vibrationally excited level of S1​ or a higher singlet state. Rapid vibrational relaxation and internal conversion usually populate the lowest vibrational levels of S1​. Fluorescence then emits a photon on a transition to S0​, often ending in one of its excited vibrational levels. Nonradiative relaxation competes with emission; intersystem crossing can also populate triplet states. Energy lost to relaxation and solvent reorganization commonly makes the fluorescence maximum occur at lower photon energy, hence longer wavelength, than the photon absorption maximum: the Stokes shift.
The intrinsic fluorescence quantum yield is the number of photons emitted by fluorescence per absorbed excitation. For a simple excited state with radiative rate kr​ and other decay rate knr​,
QD0​=kr​+knr​kr​​.
(29)
A nearby acceptor adds a nonradiative energy-transfer channel, not an emitted photon flying from donor to acceptor. The electric dipole-dipole interaction transfers the donor excitation while returning the donor toward its ground state and exciting the acceptor. The acceptor then relaxes and may fluoresce. The donor yield becomes
QDDA​=kr​+knr​+kT​kr​​=QD0​(1−E).​
(30)
For the acceptor, distinguish its intrinsic yield per acceptor excitation, QA​=kr,A​/(kr,A​+knr,A​), from the number of acceptor photons per donor excitation. Transfer increases the supply of acceptor excitations, giving sensitized emission EQA​ per donor excitation in the ideal model. It does not by itself change QA​ if the acceptor's own decay rates remain unchanged. Environmental changes, saturation or additional quenching can change those rates, but increased acceptor intensity alone does not establish an increased intrinsic fluorescence quantum yield.

The central prediction of the ATP synthase ATP synthase rotary mechanism is relative motion of the central shaft and catalytic head, not simply a change in bulk ATP concentration. In the foundational single-molecule experiment, a visible marker coupled to the central gamma shaft reported its angular position relative to the immobilized catalytic head. Sustained directional rotations during ATP hydrolysis demonstrated rotary motion directly. The catalytic head contains three beta sites within an alpha-three beta-three assembly; their changing nucleotide affinities motivate the binding-change mechanism. [ref]
An angular trajectory gives more information than an average rotational speed. At low ATP, pauses before roughly 120-degree advances become longer as ATP falls, supporting an ATP-binding-limited wait. Reducing marker drag and improving temporal resolution reveals substeps and ATP-independent catalytic dwells. The early high-speed study resolved approximately 90-degree and 30-degree substeps, with more than one slow event during the catalytic dwell. Numerical angles vary with preparation and operational definition, so these observations should not be imposed as exact universal geometric increments.[ref]

Ion-channel deactivation and ion-channel inactivation are different. Ion-channel deactivation closes an activation gate when the voltage returns toward rest. Ion-channel inactivation makes a channel unavailable even while depolarization remains favorable for activation. A minimal kinetic picture is a closed state, an open state and an unavailable state, with recovery required before reopening. This helps terminate inward electric current and produces a neuronal refractory period.
In rapid voltage-gated potassium channel N-type inactivation, a cytoplasmic terminal segment, sometimes supplied by an auxiliary subunit, blocks the open inner pore. The classic ball-and-chain inactivation picture is supported by loss of rapid ion-channel inactivation after removing the relevant terminal segment and restoration by an appropriate soluble peptide. This links a mobile structural element to open-pore blockade; it does not imply that all potassium channels use that mechanism.[ref]
Slower C-type inactivation involves rearrangement near the selectivity filter and external pore. Dependence on pore structure, ion occupancy and extracellular ions distinguishes it from a simple cytoplasmic plugging particle. Different channels can combine several unavailable states, so a single recovery time constant need not describe every trace.
Fast ion-channel inactivation in canonical animal voltage-gated sodium channels involves the intracellular linker between domains III and IV, including its hydrophobic IFM motif, and is coupled to domain-IV voltage-sensor activation. The modern structural interpretation is an allosteric latch acting on the gate, rather than a universal literal particle sitting in the ion pathway. The older hinged-lid analogy is useful only with that qualification.[ref]
Functionally, a voltage clamp trace shows electric current declining during a maintained step; a second test pulse measures recovery of availability. Holding-potential dependence separates an altered fraction of available channels from a mere change in maximal open electrical conductance. Persistent electric current after defective ion-channel inactivation, or delayed recovery that reduces subsequent responses, illustrates why both entry into and exit from unavailable states matter for excitability. A decaying macroscopic electric current alone does not uniquely identify the molecular mechanism.
