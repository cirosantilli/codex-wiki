Convert number density into molecule count: nˉ=XP,st​V. For the explicitly assumed representative cell volume V=1μm3, the two densities correspond to nˉ=104 and 102. The concentrations alone do not fix fluctuation sizes without a volume.
For activation from zero messenger RNA and protein, solving the two mean rate equations gives
nˉ⟨nP​(t)⟩​=1−αR​−αP​αR​e−αP​t−αP​e−αR​t​≃1−e−αP​t(t≫1/αR​).
(16)
Thus all three normalized mean curves are almost identical. Sample paths differ. For Poisson transcription with biological translation bursts, the stationary Fano factor and squared coefficient of variation are
nˉVarnP​​=1+αR​+αP​r​≃1+b,CV2≃nˉ1+b​.
(17)
One way to obtain the fast-messenger RNA limit is to use burst arrivals at rate a, a geometrically distributed burst B with ⟨B⟩=b and ⟨B2⟩=b+2b2, and independent unit protein degradation at rate αP​n. The first stationary moment gives ab=αP​nˉ; the second gives Varn=nˉ(1+b).
At nˉ=104,b=3, typical relative fluctuations are 4/104​=0.02 and many small bursts make the trajectory smooth. At nˉ=104,b=400, they are 401/104​≃0.20; individual bursts have mean normalized height 0.04, with only about 25 bursts per protein lifetime. At nˉ=100,b=3, the relative fluctuations are again 0.20, but the typical burst height is 0.03 and about 33 bursts occur per lifetime. The latter two examples can look similarly noisy after normalization even though their mean molecule counts differ by two orders of magnitude. Burst arrival times and sizes fluctuate: a high-burst trace is not a periodic sawtooth.
