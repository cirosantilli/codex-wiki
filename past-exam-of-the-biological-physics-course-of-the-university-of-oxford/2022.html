a OurBigBook.com
The bacterial RNA polymerase core associates with a sigma factor to form a holoenzyme. The sigma factor recognizes promoter features and helps position the DNA and stabilize its opening. Initial binding gives a closed promoter complex; local DNA unwinding creates an open promoter complex with the template accessible. Initial RNA synthesis can produce short abortive products while the RNA polymerase remains attached to the promoter, taking in downstream DNA by scrunching. Growth of the RNA eventually permits promoter escape and productive elongation. The sigma factor changes its interactions during this transition; immediate complete dissociation is not obligatory.
In magnetic tweezers, torsionally constrained DNA joins a surface to a magnetic bead. A field gradient applies upward force, rotation of the magnets controls the imposed DNA supercoiling, and optical tracking measures bead height. At suitable modest force, DNA supercoiling forms writhed loops, shortening the tether. Opening roughly one helical turn at fixed linking number makes the remaining DNA more positively twisted: ΔTwrest​+ΔWr=−ΔTwbubble​. Thus opening lengthens a negatively supercoiled tether by removing some negative writhe, but shortens a positively supercoiled tether by adding positive writhe. This opposite sign distinguishes unwinding from ordinary compaction, which shortens both tethers. Height-versus-turn calibration converts the two extension changes into an unwinding angle and a compaction contribution. Repeated height transitions give opening and closing dwell times.[ref]
The sketch is a schematic prediction, not a copy of an experimental trace. Opening must be distinguished from promoter escape: an unwound DNA region can remain associated with an elongating RNA polymerase.

Treat the target as an absorbing sphere of effective capture radius r0​, surrounded by a uniform dilute reservoir of RNA polymerase with number density C∞​. The steady diffusion equation, spherical symmetry, and boundary conditions give
∇2C=0,C(r0​)=0,C(∞)=C∞​,C(r)=C∞​(1−rr0​​).
(1)
By Fick's laws, the inward capture rate per target is 4πr02​DA​C′(r0​)=4πDA​r0​C∞​. Multiplying by the target number density and comparing with the bimolecular rate law therefore yields the Smoluchowski association rate
ka​=4πDA​r0​​.
(2)
Its units here are m3s−1, since the concentrations count particles rather than moles. The absorbing boundary assumes every encounter is productive; orientation constraints would reduce the effective association rate constant.
The Stokes–Einstein relation, for RNA polymerase radius a=15nm at an illustrative temperature T=300K, gives
DA​=6πηakB​T​=1.46×10−12m2s−1.
(3)
Neither cell volume nor the effective r0​ is specified, so a unique numerical time is unavailable. A representative Escherichia coli volume is V=1μm3=10−18m3. The 40-base pair target has a DNA contour extent of about 40(0.34)=13.6nm; using this as an order-of-magnitude capture radius gives ka​≃2.50×10−19m3s−1. For M=3000 independent searchers, the target's approximately constant encounter hazard is Mka​/V, hence
τ3​≃Mka​V​=1.33ms(1μm3V​)(r0​13.6nm​)(T300K​)​.
(4)
Using half the target's contour extent for r0​ instead gives about 2.7ms; the spherical capture model is not exact target geometry. One searcher's mean would be about 4s under the first convention, whereas the first arrival among 3000 is much faster.
If only a fraction f3​=0.1 of each searcher's time is available for three-dimensional diffusion, and nonspecific DNA binding neither scans nor delivers the target, the effective searching population is f3​M. Thus τ≃τ3​/f3​=10τ3​​, about 13ms under the stated estimate. This assumes stationarity, well-mixed searchers and no additional target-access route; nonspecific binding alone is sequestration, not facilitated diffusion.

Write A=V/D3​ and B=L/D1​. The search time is A/λ+Bλ: it decreases from infinity at small positive λ, reaches one minimum, and increases without bound at large λ within this idealized model.
During a facilitated diffusion search, roughly L/λ separate DNA landing-and-sliding episodes sample a chromosome. A three-dimensional encounter with any chromosomal landing region has characteristic time V/(D3​L), so the total bulk-search contribution is (L/λ)V/(D3​L)=V/(D3​λ). Each one-dimensional diffusion excursion lasts order λ2/D1​; multiplying by L/λ gives Lλ/D1​. Longer sliding therefore reduces the number of bulk relocations but increases time spent repeatedly exploring local DNA. Numerical constants and overlap corrections are already omitted in the given model.
Differentiating and using the positive second derivative gives
−D3​λ2V​+D1​L​=0,λ∗​=LD3​VD1​​​,τmin​=2D1​D3​VL​​​.
(5)
At λ∗​ the two time contributions are equal. The optimum is meaningful only if λ∗​ lies between the microscopic capture scale and the available chromosomal contour length.
A decrease in DNA concentration means a decrease in L/V, but its effect on the time is not determined by that ratio alone. At fixed chromosome length L, dilution by increasing V increases τ at fixed λ and increases τmin​ as V​; bulk relocation becomes slower. If instead L decreases at fixed V, while the target is retained, the stated model predicts a smaller sliding contribution and a smaller optimized time, proportional to L​. In both cases λ∗​∝(L/V)−1/2. This distinction prevents attributing a unique time trend to concentration without specifying how it changes.

Use the logic approximation for gene regulation: production is either β or zero, and every species has linear removal rate α. Put C=β/α and assume zero basal expression, inactive X before time zero, and all required thresholds strictly between 0 and C. The input is u(t)=1 for 0<t<T and zero otherwise. The common ordinary differential equation is c˙=βg−αc, with Boolean production gate g. Both networks therefore have
Y(t)=C(1−e−αt)(0<t<T),Y(t)=Ce−α(t−T)(t>T),
(9)
where the latter uses the stipulated attained steady state. Define the threshold-crossing delay
d(K)=−α−1ln(1−K/C),tr​=d(KYZ​),t1​=d(KYY1​).
(10)
Because KYY1​>KYZ​, t1​>tr​. In both networks, Y1​ stays zero until t1​ and then follows Y1​=C[1−e−α(t−t1​)] until the OFF response. Write KA​ for the activation threshold of Y1​ on Z in diagram (a), and KI​ for the level of Y1​ that blocks Y's repression in diagram (b); these thresholds were not supplied numerically.
Diagram (a). All three inputs X, Y and Y1​ must permit Z production. Since Y has already crossed its Z threshold before Y1​ starts, the final permission arrives at
ta​=t1​+d(KA​).
(11)
Thus Z=0 until ta​ and Z=C[1−e−α(t−ta​)] subsequently. Solving Z=C/2 gives
tZ,1/2​=d(KYY1​)+d(KA​)+αln2​​.
(12)
For the frequently adopted but extra convention that both required thresholds are C/2, this is 3ln2/α. At the OFF step, X's direct AND input disappears, so Z=Ce−α(t−T) immediately. Y also decays immediately. Y1​ remains at C while Y remains above KYY1​; it starts decaying at T+α−1ln(C/KYY1​). This is a coherent feed-forward loop persistence response: activation is delayed, while removal of the direct required input switches production off promptly.
Diagram (b). The production gate is gZ​=u[¬(Y>KYZ​)∨(Y1​>KI​)]. Z initially rises, Y switches Z production off at tr​, and Y1​ restores production at tb​=t1​+d(KI​). The continuous solution during the ON phase is
Z(t)=⎩⎨⎧​C(1−e−αt),C(1−e−αtr​)e−α(t−tr​),C+[Z(tb​)−C]e−α(t−tb​),​0<t<tr​,tr​<t<tb​,tb​<t<T.​
(13)
The derivative changes from positive to negative at tr​, and from negative to positive at tb​. Consequently the first temporary minimum following the initial pulse occurs at
tmin​=d(KYY1​)+d(KI​)​.
(14)
Its value is Z(tb​)=KYZ​e−α(tb​−tr​). At the OFF step Z again decays immediately because X is required for production, with Y and Y1​ behaving as above. If a required threshold is unattainable, these late switching events do not occur; equal production and removal rates alone do not fix threshold values.
The plotted thresholds are explicitly illustrative, with KYZ​/C=0.25, KYY1​/C=0.55 and KA​/C=KI​/C=0.45.

The protein production rate is proportional to the current messenger RNA concentration, not an independently constant βP​: βP​(t)=rXR​(t). Setting the two coupled rate equations to zero gives
XR,st​=αR​βR​​,XP,st​=αR​αP​rβR​​=αP​bβR​​​.
(15)
With first-order removal, a messenger RNA molecule has mean lifetime 1/αR​. At biological translation rate r per molecule, it produces an expected r/αR​=b proteins before its removal. Thus the translational burst size is a dimensionless mean number of offspring proteins per messenger RNA, not a concentration and not a fixed number produced in every event. When production and removal are independent Poisson events, mixing a Poisson number of biological translation events over an exponentially distributed lifetime makes the burst count geometrically distributed on the nonnegative integers. Its mean is b and its variance is b(1+b).

Normalize each reporter by its mean intensity, after correcting background and unequal brightness, so equal expression lies on y=x. Write x=1+e+iC​, y=1+e+iY​, where the common fluctuation e is extrinsic gene-expression noise and the independent reporter-specific fluctuations iC​,iY​ are intrinsic gene-expression noise. The extrinsic gene-expression noise includes shared variation in cell size, expression machinery and growth state; intrinsic gene-expression noise includes independent molecular production and removal events at each reporter.
If intrinsic gene-expression noise is much smaller, points form a narrow elongated cloud along y=x: a cell that is bright in cyan is usually bright in yellow. If the two contributions have comparable magnitude, the cloud is still positively correlated but has substantial width perpendicular to y=x, because the two reporters can differ within the same cell. With variance σe2​ in e and equal variance σi2​ in each independent intrinsic term,
Cov(x,y)=σe2​,Var(y−x)=2σi2​,Corr(x,y)=σe2​+σi2​σe2​​.
(18)
Equal intrinsic and extrinsic variances therefore give correlation coefficient 1/2, rather than an uncorrelated circular cloud. For mean-normalized reporters, ηint2​=⟨(x−y)2⟩/2 and ηext2​=⟨xy⟩−1. Measurement noise must be separately estimated before assigning perpendicular scatter to expression itself. These are the geometric diagnostics of the original dual-reporter experiment.[ref]
The lower panels of c show these two cases. Repeated observations of one cell can move both along and across the diagonal; a static population cloud alone does not determine the time scales of the fluctuations.

In the continuum cylindrical-pore estimate, the electrical resistance is Rp​=ρℓ/(πa2), with radius a=0.075nm, length ℓ=0.5nm and electrical resistivity ρ=80Ωcm=0.80Ωm. Therefore
Rp​=2.26×1010Ω,gp​=Rp−1​=44.2pS​.
(19)

Raccess​=2aρ​=5.33×109Ω,gtotal​=[πa2ρℓ​+2aρ​]−1≃35.8pS​.

The rising sigmoid shows activation by depolarization: Po​ is almost zero at very negative membrane potential, rises steeply around −20mV, and approaches about 0.8 at positive membrane potential. The graph describes the fraction of time a single ion channel is open, not a continuously variable opening amplitude. A convenient phenomenological fit is
Po​(V)=1+exp[−zeff​e(V−V1/2​)/(kB​T)]Pmax​​,Pmax​≃0.8,V1/2​≃−20mV.
(21)
Here V1/2​ refers to half the maximum, about 0.4, not absolute Po​=0.5. The effective gating charge describes the steepness; a sigmoid alone does not identify the microscopic gating sequence or explain why the observed maximum is below one.
A voltage-gated potassium channel has four pore-forming subunits. Each canonical subunit contains an S1–S4 voltage-sensing domain and S5–S6 pore elements. Positive charges in S4 respond to the electric field; their displacement during depolarization changes the energetic preference of sensor conformations. Coupling through the sensor–pore interface favors opening of the intracellular pore gate. The potassium selectivity filter is structurally distinct from this voltage sensor.
Several independent observations support this account. Voltage clamp detects transient gating electric currents before pore conduction, even when ionic conduction is blocked: charge movement precedes opening. Charge-neutralization studies change total gating charge and voltage dependence, with contributions from charged S4 residues and an S2 residue. Accessibility and voltage-sensitive optical measurements support conformational motion rather than permeating potassium itself acting as the sensor. Structural measurements locate the charged sensor beside the pore. These observations constrain a coupled moving-sensor mechanism, while the exact displacement cannot be deduced from this Po​ curve alone.[ref][ref]

In ball-and-chain inactivation, also called N-type inactivation, a flexible intracellular N-terminal region carries an inactivation particle. Once a voltage-gated potassium channel opens, this tethered particle can enter and occlude the inner pore, stopping electric current even while the activation machinery favors an open configuration. Recovery requires the particle to leave, after which the activation gate can return to its resting conformation. This supplies an additional unavailable state, not merely reversal of activation.
The decisive evidence separates necessity from sufficiency. Removing the relevant N-terminal region greatly reduces fast ion-channel inactivation while preserving activation. Applying a peptide corresponding to the missing N-terminal region on the cytoplasmic side restores blocking with similar properties. Changes in the blocking region alter blocking kinetics, and altering the tether affects the rate at which the particle reaches the pore. Open-pore access and competition with intracellular pore blockers support occlusion of the inner cavity. A tether makes the particle's local effective concentration high, explaining rapid intramolecular block.[ref][ref]
This mechanism is distinct from slower filter-associated C-type inactivation. An N-terminal particle is a mechanism for certain potassium ion channels, not a universal explanation of all ion-channel inactivation.

In a classical action potential, delayed activation of voltage-gated potassium channels supplies outward electric current that drives repolarization. In the Hodgkin-Huxley model, for example, IK​=gˉ​K​n4(V−EK​) and n˙=αn​(V)(1−n)−βn​(V)n. Increasing the opening rate at depolarized membrane potential, other properties fixed, recruits outward electric current earlier: the action potential generally becomes narrower and may have a smaller peak. Slower opening delays repolarization and broadens the action potential.
Slower closing after repolarization retains excess potassium electrical conductance, lengthening the after-hyperpolarization and often the interval of reduced excitability. Faster closing shortens this tail and permits earlier recovery. If activation or the available electrical conductance is insufficient, normal repolarization may fail; changing rates can also change the steady open fraction. Duration is therefore determined by the whole balance of inward and outward electric currents, not by a closing rate in isolation.

k1​Aeq​=k2​Beq​,Aeq​Beq​​=e−(GB​−GA​)/(kB​T).

(25)

The rigid-link freely jointed chain stores extension entirely in orientation. It predicts ⟨z⟩→L=Nb and a divergent force as that limit is approached. Real chemical bonds and bond angles can deform, adding energetic compliance. This permits the measured chain to stretch farther at the same large force and removes the rigid-link vertical asymptote over the experimentally accessible range. Other limitations include segment correlations, solvent effects and eventual failure; a fit alone does not isolate their contributions.
Introduce one axial stretch modulus S with units of force. A useful phenomenological extensible freely jointed chain law adds a linear strain contribution:
⟨z⟩=L[coth(kB​Tfb​)−fbkB​T​]+SL​f​.
(44)
Equivalently, if each aligned segment is a spring of stiffness ks​ in force per length, S=bks​ and the additional extension is Nf/ks​. At high force, ⟨z⟩≃L−NkB​T/f+Lf/S; the extra term supplies finite axial compliance. At small force, the compliances add: d⟨z⟩/df=Nb2/(3kB​T)+L/S.
This additive law is a simple requested one-term modification, rather than an exact angular average for harmonic links. Another common approximation is LL(fb/(kB​T))(1+f/S), which couples stretching to orientation. Neither should be extrapolated through bond rupture or claimed to predict a universal finite maximum for an extensible chain. A positive fitted S could improve the high-force extension while leaving the entropic part largely intact.

Position from many photons. An isolated fluorophore produces a diffraction-broadened point spread function, but its unknown center can be inferred much more precisely than the width of that function. Fit a calibrated two-dimensional Gaussian function plus background to the detected photon positions or pixel intensities. If the point spread function has width s and Nγ​ independent photons are detected, negligible background gives localization precision approximately s/Nγ​​. Pixelation and background worsen it; a commonly useful approximate variance is
σx2​≃Nγ​s2+a2/12​+a2Nγ2​8πs4σbg2​​,
(45)
where a is pixel size in the specimen plane and σbg​ is background noise per pixel in photon units. Bright, isolated emitters can therefore have nanometre localization precision despite a spot hundreds of nanometres wide. Stage drift, finite exposure motion, incorrect fitting, label displacement and photobleaching can dominate the photon limit.[ref]
Walking mechanism. Myosin V is a two-headed, processive molecular motor that advances along actin with center-of-mass steps of about 37nm. A simple inchworm model retains the identity of the leading head, so a label fixed to one head would move approximately 37nm on every cycle. In a hand-over-hand model the heads exchange lead: a label close to one head alternates a displacement near 74nm with a near-zero displacement while the other head passes. For a label at offset x from the midpoint, the more general alternating pair is 37−2x and 37+2x nanometres. The pair sums to 74nm; individual steps need not always equal 74nm.
Single-label imaging records a moving spot on a camera while the motor travels along surface-associated actin. Fitting the spot in successive frames resolves plateaus and jumps much smaller than the diffraction width. The observed alternating displacements distinguish a passing-head mechanism from uniform inchworm steps. This experiment combines a specific geometric prediction with single-molecule fluorescence microscopy; the center-of-mass stride alone would not make that distinction.[ref]
Resolving a labelled structure. Accurate localization of one emitter does not automatically resolve two simultaneously overlapping emitters. Stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy overcomes this by activating or switching on only a sparse subset at a time, localizing the well-separated spots, turning them off and repeating. The accumulated coordinates reconstruct a densely labelled structure at scales below the diffraction limit. The original demonstration used repeated photoswitching and localization of individual dyes to reconstruct subdiffraction features.[ref]
The useful structural resolution depends on both localization precision and label sampling density, as well as drift, fluorophore blinking, optical aberrations and linkage error. Sparse activation separates emitters in time; it does not shrink the diffraction-limited point spread function. A reconstructed image must have enough independent labels to represent its finest claimed feature.

An ionic atmosphere. A negatively charged molecule attracts positive counterions and repels negative co-ions; signs reverse for a positive molecule. The resulting electric double layer partially screens the bare electric field. Far from an isolated molecule, the electrolyte recovers its neutral bulk composition. At thermodynamic equilibrium, spatial uniformity of each species' electrochemical potential requires
μi​=μi0​+kB​Tlnni​(r)+zi​eψ(r),ni​(r)=ni∞​e−zi​eψ(r)/(kB​T)​.
(46)
The logarithm implicitly uses a common reference density. This result for Boltzmann ion densities in a symmetric electrolyte neglects ion correlations, finite size and nonideal activities, but shows immediately why equal concentrations of the two signs do not persist close to a charged surface.
Self-consistent screening. Insert these densities into Poisson's equation, with solvent permittivity ϵ and fixed charge density ρf​:
−ϵ∇2ψ=ρf​+∑i​zi​eni∞​e−zi​eψ/(kB​T).
(47)
This is the Poisson-Boltzmann equation. For a symmetric monovalent salt with bulk density ns​ of each sign, outside the fixed charge it becomes
∇2ψ=ϵ2ens​​sinh(kB​Teψ​).
(48)
For ∣zi​eψ∣≪kB​T, linearizing and using bulk neutrality gives the Debye–Hückel approximation
∇2ψ=κ2ψ,κ2=ϵkB​Te2​i∑​zi2​ni∞​,λD​=κ−1​.
(49)
Thus added salt shortens the Debye length: electrostatic screening becomes stronger over a shorter distance. The factor of two in a 1:1 salt comes from two ionic species, not from double-counting the density of salt pairs.
For example, a charged sphere of radius a and total charge Q has the exterior linearized solution
ψ(r)=4πϵ(1+κa)Q​re−κ(r−a)​,r≥a,
(50)
assuming fixed uniform surface charge and no ions inside. It follows from the decaying spherical solution and −ϵψ′(a)=Q/(4πa2). At zero salt it reduces to the Coulomb potential; at finite salt it decays exponentially as well as geometrically. The excess mobile charge integrates to −Q in an infinite neutralizing reservoir, although a finite sphere surrounding the molecule generally contains only part of that ionic atmosphere.
Biophysical scales and limitations. In water at room temperature, the monovalent formula is approximately λD​=0.304nm/I/(molL−1)​, with I the ionic strength. It gives about 0.79nm at 0.15molL−1 and 9.6nm at 1mmolL−1. Consequently the same charged protein can exert very different long-range interactions mediated by electrostatics at different salt concentrations. Close to strongly charged DNA, the linear approximation fails; nonlinear screening, counterion condensation, ion-specific adsorption and changes of surface ionization through charge regulation may matter. Mean-field theory also misses strong multivalent-ion correlations and finite-size saturation: the exponential law cannot be extrapolated to arbitrarily large near-surface concentrations.

An ion channel can disturb cellular electrical activity by changing unitary electrical conductance, open probability, gating kinetics or the number delivered to the cell membrane. A useful organizing relation is
Ij​=Nj​γj​Po,j​(V,t)(V−Ej​),Cm​V˙=Iapplied​−∑j​Ij​.
(51)
Here outward electric current is positive. Altering one channel's biophysical behavior changes the balance governing membrane potential and hence the timing or recurrence of action potentials.
Long QT syndrome, type 3. Certain disease-associated SCN5A variants impair fast ion-channel inactivation of the cardiac voltage-gated sodium channel NaV1.5. Instead of vanishing promptly after the upstroke, a small persistent inward sodium electric current remains during the plateau and repolarization. Because V<ENa​ in this phase, the residual electric current is inward and opposes the outward electric currents that would terminate the cardiac action potential. The ventricular action potential lasts longer, extending the QT interval on the electrocardiogram. Excessively prolonged or uneven repolarization permits early afterdepolarizations and dangerous ventricular rhythm disturbances.
Genetic association identifies the channel, while voltage clamp distinguishes defective ion-channel inactivation from a mere increase in channel abundance. Recordings of disease-associated channels show persistent or bursting openings; coupling measured mutant sodium electric current into an otherwise modeled cardiac membrane through dynamic clamp tests whether that kinetic defect prolongs the action potential. The phenotype is variant-specific: not every SCN5A variant increases late electric current or causes long QT syndrome.[ref][ref]
Myotonia congenita. CLCN1 encodes the skeletal-muscle ClC-1 chloride channel, which normally supplies a large resting stabilizing electrical conductance. Because the chloride reversal potential is near the resting membrane potential, that pathway resists small depolarizing disturbances. Loss of functional channels, reduced electrical conductance, or a shift in opening toward more positive membrane potential reduces the resting electrical shunt. Potassium accumulation in the transverse-tubule space during activity can then produce larger depolarizing afterpotentials and trigger further muscle action potentials after the intended stimulus has ceased. Continued electrical firing sustains contraction and delays relaxation: this is myotonia, rather than a primary inability of the contractile apparatus to relax.
Patient-family linkage and variant analysis connect the disease to ClC-1. Functional recordings of disease-associated channels demonstrate shifted voltage dependence and reduced availability near resting membrane potential; single-channel and noise measurements also resolve two conductive protopores and their gating behavior. Different variants can act through different mechanisms, so a gating shift is an example rather than an explanation of every case.[ref][ref][ref]
These examples connect contrasting current defects to their consequences: persistent inward sodium electric current prolongs cardiac action potentials, whereas loss of stabilizing resting chloride electrical conductance permits repetitive skeletal-muscle action potentials. Both causal chains require functional channel measurements in addition to a disease-associated sequence change.
