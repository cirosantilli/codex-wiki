Random-phase amplitude and the Rayleigh distribution

 ... Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability distribution Continuous probability distribution Rayleigh distribution
OurBigBook.com Words: 177
In an 1880 paper, John William Strutt, known as Lord Rayleigh, studied the resultant of many oscillations with a common frequency and arbitrary oscillation phases. His large-number calculation distinguished a mean intensity from a definite resultant amplitude and used planar coordinates followed by a polar integral. The resulting amplitude density is the Rayleigh distribution in a different scale convention. This is a large-number description, not an exact law for every finite collection of oscillations.[ref]
To see the connection in a chosen model, take independent oscillation phases Θj​ with uniform distribution on [0,2π) and equal component amplitude a. The phasor sum has coordinates
Xn​=a∑j=1n​cosΘj​,Yn​=a∑j=1n​sinΘj​.
(36)
Both have mean zero, variance na2/2 and zero covariance. The multivariate central limit theorem gives an approximately circular Gaussian random vector for large n, with σ=an/2​. Its amplitude Xn2​+Yn2​​ is therefore approximately Rayleigh. For every finite n, that amplitude is bounded by na, whereas a Rayleigh distribution has an unbounded tail. At n=1 it is exactly a. This simple endpoint check prevents the approximation from becoming a false exact identity.
