Infinite square well (En​=n2π2ℏ2/(2mL2))

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 516 Articles: 3
The infinite square well is an ideal confinement model with potential energy V(x)=0 for 0<x<L and infinite walls outside. The walls impose Dirichlet boundary conditions ϕ(0)=ϕ(L)=0 and a vanishing exterior wavefunction. Inside, the time-independent Schrödinger equation is
−2mℏ2​dx2d2ϕ​=Eϕ,ϕn​(x)=L2​​sinLnπx​,En​=2mL2n2π2ℏ2​,n=1,2,….
(184)
The first wall removes the cosine term; the second requires sin(kL)=0, hence k=nπ/L. The n=0 solution is identically zero and cannot satisfy wavefunction normalization. Each energy eigenstate has n−1 interior nodes of a standing wave, at x=jL/n for j=1,…,n−1. More curvature costs more kinetic energy, explaining the n2 growth. The positive ground-state energy is a confinement effect: a constant zero-energy wavefunction cannot satisfy both walls and remain normalized.
For an Electron with mass me​=9.1093837139×10−31kg in a box of length L=1nm, use h=6.62607015×10−34Js and 1eV=1.602176634×10−19J. These constants are from the 2022 CODATA adjustment. [ref]. Substitution gives
E1​=8me​L2h2​=6.024667×10−20J≈0.376030eV,E2​=4E1​≈1.504121eV.
(185)
Doubling L quarters every energy eigenvalue; doubling m halves them. This scaling makes the particle in a box useful for estimating confinement energies before attempting a more realistic calculation. A finite square well permits exterior tails for bound states and has only finitely many bound energy eigenstates. For an Electron, the scalar box calculation assumes fixed spin and no spin-dependent interactions.
For the ground state, the probability of finding the particle in the middle half of the box is fully computable:
Pr(4L​<x<43L​)=∫L/43L/4​L2​sin2Lπx​dx=[Lx​−2πsin(2πx/L)​]L/43L/4​=21​+π1​≈0.818310.
(186)
Thus half the available length contains about 81.8% of the probability. The figure contrasts the signed wavefunctions with their nonnegative probability densities: a negative lobe is an amplitude with opposite quantum phase, not negative probability.
An equal quantum superposition of the first two energy eigenstates gives a simple example of moving density:
ψ(x,t)=2​ϕ1​(x)e−iE1​t/ℏ+ϕ2​(x)e−iE2​t/ℏ​,∣ψ∣2=2ϕ12​+ϕ22​​+ϕ1​ϕ2​cosℏ(E2​−E1​)t​.
(187)
Its density period is T=h/(E2​−E1​)≈3.66608fs for the Electron box above. At t=0 the cross term enhances the left side; at T/4 it vanishes; at T/2 it enhances the right side. More quantitatively,
Pr(0<x<L/2)=21​+3π4​cosT2πt​,
(188)
which takes the values 0.924413, 0.5, and 0.075587 at those times. Orthogonality keeps the total probability one throughout. An energy measurement still returns E1​ or E2​ with equal probabilities: changing position density arises from relative quantum phase, not changing populations. A mixed state describing a statistical mixture with those populations has the constant probability density (ϕ12​+ϕ22​)/2; it lacks this matter-wave interference term.
