Chloroplast dye-reduction experiment

 Home Biology Biological cell Chloroplast Hill reaction
OurBigBook.com Words: 364
The Practical Biology protocol uses cold sucrose in buffer solution, ground green leaves, muslin filtration, centrifugation and cold pellet resuspension. A 100W lamp illuminates extract plus DCPIP from 12–15cm; a red-filter colorimeter monitors blue-color loss.[ref]
Compare extract plus dye in darkness, illuminated dye without extract, extract without dye, and supernatant plus dye: controls for light, extract dependence, background color and incomplete separation. Green supernatant can remain active; a centrifugation pellet establishes neither purity nor intact chloroplasts.[ref]
Here is an original logical check of the controls. Suppose only the illuminated extract-plus-dye condition loses blue color, while the dark mixture and illuminated dye-only mixture retain it. Removing light or removing extract each removes the effect in those comparisons. That supports a joint requirement under the tested conditions. If the dye-only mixture also loses blue color, the proposed attribution to extract has failed its control; a faster change in the extract mixture would require a quantitative comparison before attribution. These are conditional cases, not experimental results.
Even the favorable case leaves three distinct questions. Dye reduction demonstrates delivery of electrons to an artificial electron acceptor. An independently calibrated molecular oxygen readout is needed to measure molecular oxygen evolution. Demonstrating carbon fixation additionally requires evidence that carbon dioxide becomes incorporated into organic material. None of the latter quantities is supplied by a blue-color endpoint alone. An absorbance change also needs background and scattering controls before it can be treated as dye concentration; Beer-Lambert law states the relevant assumptions.
In particular, two hypothetical preparations can give the same dye endpoint while differing in whether their stromal Calvin cycle is functional. Observing only the dye discards that distinction. The counterexample establishes why dye decolorization cannot alone prove complete photosynthesis, carbon fixation, or an intact chloroplast. Temperature, extract loading and illumination must also be matched when comparing rates: a lamp can alter both light exposure and heating.
