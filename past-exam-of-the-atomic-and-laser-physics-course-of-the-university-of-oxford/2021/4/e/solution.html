Put kZ​=Ze2/(4πϵ0​). Gauss's law for a uniform spherical nuclear charge distribution, with the electrostatic potential matched at R, gives the electronic potential energy
Vsph​(r)=⎩⎨⎧​−2RkZ​​(3−r2/R2),−kZ​/r,​r<R,r≥R.​
(48)
Subtracting the point-nucleus Coulomb potential leaves the nonnegative perturbation
δV(r)=kZ​(r1​−2R3​+2R3r2​)(r<R),δV=0(r≥R).
(49)
The normalized nonrelativistic hydrogen-like ion 1s wavefunction has ∣ψ(r)∣2=Z3e−2Zr/a0​/(πa03​). Since ZR/a0​≪1, approximate it inside the atomic nucleus by ∣ψ(0)∣2. First-order time-independent perturbation theory then gives
ΔE1s​​≃4π∣ψ(0)∣2kZ​∫0R​(r−2R3r2​+2R3r4​)dr=4π∣ψ(0)∣2kZ​10R2​=52​4πϵ0​a0​Z4e2​(a0​R​)2.​
(50)
Define EH​=−Z2Ry, as the paper specifies for the hydrogen-like ion, rather than the neutral-hydrogen value. Using Ry=e2/(8πϵ0​a0​) gives
ΔE1s​=54​Z2∣EH​∣(a0​R​)2=54​Z4Ry(a0​R​)2​
(51)
to leading order in ZR/a0​. For A=208, R≃7.11fm, ZR/a0​≃0.0110, and
ΔE1s​≃+8.88eV,ΔE1s​/h≃+2.15×1015Hz.​
(52)
The finite nuclear charge radius makes the energy level less bound. The quoted frequency is a level shift divided by h: a line frequency shifts by (ΔEu​−ΔEl​)/h, so a decay into this shifted 1s level alone has the opposite sign.
The formula is a nonrelativistic, small-nuclear charge radius estimate. Keeping the exponential in the first-order integral gives ΔE1s​=ΔEleading​[1−5ZR/(6a0​)+O((ZR/a0​)2)], about a one-percent correction here. More seriously, Zαfs​≃0.598 for lead, so relativistic contraction is substantial. A precise physical prediction needs a finite-nucleus Dirac equation calculation; the boxed estimate is the model requested, not precision heavy-ion spectroscopy.
