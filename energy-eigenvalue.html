Energy eigenvalue

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum mechanics
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 14
An energy eigenvalue is an eigenvalue of the Hamiltonian operator H: there is a nonzero vector ψ in its operator domain D(H) with
Hψ=Eψ.
(1)
For a self-adjoint operator, E is real. Normalizing ψ produces an energy eigenstate; an ideal energy measurement then returns E with probability one. The associated energy eigenspace includes all vectors with this same energy eigenvalue, including the zero vector, which represents no quantum state. Different energy eigenvalues have orthogonal energy eigenspaces. [ref].
The energy eigenvalues form the point spectrum, which can be only part of the spectrum. The term discrete spectrum more specifically means isolated eigenvalues of finite multiplicity. The usual fixed-nucleus hydrogen atom model has discrete negative bound state energy eigenvalues together with a continuum at nonnegative energy. [ref]. A quantum system need not have a ladder of discrete energy eigenvalues: a whole-line free particle has continuous spectrum. A plane wave used for a free particle is a generalized eigenfunction of the Hamiltonian operator, rather than a normalized vector in the physical Hilbert space. [ref].
For the infinite square well 0<x<L, the Dirichlet boundary conditions give
ϕn​(x)=L2​​sinLnπx​,En​=2mL2ℏ2π2n2​,n=1,2,….
(2)
Substitution into Hϕn​=En​ϕn​ and wavefunction normalization verify these statements directly. Here E1​ is the ground-state energy, even though its index is one; the labeling convention does not determine which quantum state is lowest. Equal populations of ϕ1​ and ϕ2​ have energy expectation value (E1​+E2​)/2=5E1​/2, but an energy measurement returns E1​ or 4E1​, each with probability 1/2. An average of allowed energy eigenvalues need not itself be an energy eigenvalue.

Energy eigenstate (Hϕ=Eϕ)

Words: 129 Articles: 2
Energy eigenvalue OurBigBook.com
An energy eigenstate is a quantum state represented by an eigenvector of the Quantum Hamiltonian. For a time-independent Quantum Hamiltonian, a normalized eigenfunction ϕ with energy eigenvalue E evolves according to the Schrödinger equation as
ψ(x,t)=ϕ(x)e−iEt/ℏ,∣ψ(x,t)∣2=∣ϕ(x)∣2.
(3)
Here ℏ=h/(2π) is the reduced Planck constant. The changing factor is a global phase, so this is a stationary state: its position probability density is constant. For example, the second infinite square well mode has two density lobes separated by a node of a standing wave at x=L/2; both lobes stay fixed while its quantum phase rotates. An ideal energy measurement returns E with probability one, but a position measurement still has a distribution. Stationary density does not describe a particle sitting at the peak of a lobe. Distinct energy eigenvalues accumulate different quantum phases, making their quantum superposition behave differently.

Energy eigenspace

Words: 320
Energy eigenvalue OurBigBook.com
The energy eigenspace at E is the vector subspace
EE​=ker(H−EI)={ψ∈D(H):Hψ=Eψ},
(4)
where H is the Hamiltonian operator and I is the identity operator. It is nonzero exactly when E is an energy eigenvalue. Its dimension is the multiplicity of that energy eigenvalue; degenerate energy levels have multiplicity greater than one. The ground-state subspace is EE0​​, and its dimension is the ground-state degeneracy. A vector in an energy eigenspace need not be one of a specially chosen orthonormal basis of energy eigenstates.
Linearity makes the useful point immediate: if Hϕ1​=Eϕ1​ and Hϕ2​=Eϕ2​, then H(aϕ1​+bϕ2​)=E(aϕ1​+bϕ2​). Every normalized nonzero combination stays in the same energy eigenspace. For a time-independent Hamiltonian operator, its evolution is multiplication by the common global phase e−iEt/ℏ, so it is a stationary state. A quantum superposition within a degenerate energy eigenspace can change spatial shape when its coefficients are chosen differently, but its density does not subsequently beat under this unchanged Hamiltonian operator.
Across distinct energy eigenspaces, relative quantum phases evolve. For orthonormal eigenfunctions ϕ1​,ϕ2​ of the Hamiltonian operator with energy eigenvalues E1​=E2​, an equal-weight quantum superposition has
ψ(x,t)=2​e−iE1​t/ℏϕ1​(x)+e−iE2​t/ℏϕ2​(x)​,
(5)
and position probability density
∣ψ(x,t)∣2=2∣ϕ1​(x)∣2+∣ϕ2​(x)∣2​+Re[ϕ1​(x)∗ϕ2​(x)e−i(E2​−E1​)t/ℏ].
(6)
The interference term can change local probabilities even though the Born rule assigns the same time-independent probability 1/2 to each energy measurement outcome. Orthogonality makes its integral zero, preserving wavefunction normalization. This pure state is not a stationary state; some particular measurement distributions may nevertheless remain constant. An incoherent mixed state with the same energy populations has no such interference term.
For the pictured infinite square well, θ=3E1​t/ℏ and the interference repeats after T=2πℏ/(3E1​). At t=0, direct integration gives ⟨x⟩=L/2−16L/(9π2); halfway through the period it is L/2+16L/(9π2). This is a moving expectation value, rather than a trajectory of an individual particle. The distinction between an energy eigenspace and a combination of different energy eigenspaces is what makes the different behavior possible.

Excited state

Words: 2k Articles: 7
Energy eigenvalue OurBigBook.com
In the stationary-state terminology used here, an excited state is a normalized energy eigenstate with energy eigenvalue E>E0​, where E0​ is an attained ground-state energy. Its excitation cost above the ground state is ΔE=E−E0​>0. Several energy eigenstates can share one excited energy eigenvalue through degenerate energy levels. An arbitrary quantum superposition with energy expectation value above E0​ need not be a single stationary excited state; its energy content is described by its Born rule probabilities or, for a continuum, its scalar spectral measure.
An excited state can be a stationary state: the second infinite square well mode is an immediate example. Spontaneous photon emission is a transition caused by coupling matter to the electromagnetic field; it is absent from an isolated matter-only Hamiltonian operator. The relevant energy eigenstate of that isolated model is therefore not automatically an energy eigenstate of the full interacting system. This distinction explains how an ideal stationary excited state can approximate a state with a finite experimental lifetime. See electric-dipole interaction for the coupling used in the simplest optical model.
For a one-photon transition between stationary internal states, neglecting recoil, conservation of energy requires
hν=Ef​−Ei​for absorption,hν=Ei​−Ef​for emission.
(7)
Here h is the Planck constant and ν is the photon frequency. Energy matching alone does not guarantee a transition: in the electric-dipole interaction approximation its strength also involves the matrix element ⟨f∣d⋅ϵ∣i⟩, where d is the electric dipole moment operator and ϵ specifies photon polarization. A zero matrix element gives an electric-dipole selection rule. Thus an absent optical line does not prove the corresponding energy eigenvalue is absent; a different interaction can couple different states. [ref]. The Franck-Hertz experiment gives a particularly instructive comparison between collision excitation and photon emission.
The first excited state terminology assumes a least energy eigenvalue above E0​. Such a next level is not guaranteed by quantum mechanics: a continuous spectrum can start immediately above the spectral bottom, or energy eigenvalues can accumulate there. For example, a Hamiltonian operator with energy eigenvalues 0,ε,ε/2,ε/3,…, where ε>0, has a ground-state energy zero but no least positive energy eigenvalue: every candidate ε/n is exceeded downward by ε/(n+1). A free particle on the whole line has neither a ground state nor a first stationary excited state. Each use of a discrete excitation ladder therefore needs a specified Hamiltonian operator and its boundary conditions.

Franck-Hertz experiment

Words: 2k Articles: 4
Excited state OurBigBook.com
The Franck-Hertz experiment connects a reproducible loss of electron kinetic energy with excitation of a chemical atom. The original mercury-vapor experiments were performed during 1912–1914 while investigating ionization; they were not designed as a test of the Bohr model. Their later interpretation distinguished internal excitation from removal of an electron. [ref].
In a modern mercury tube, a heated cathode emits electrons and a positive perforated grid accelerates them through Hg vapor. A collector slightly below the grid's electric potential rejects slow electrons. The oven controls vapor density and hence the mean free path. Keep heating and retarding settings fixed, sweep the accelerating electric potential difference V, and record collector electric current I(V). These measured quantities indirectly probe atomic excitation. [ref].
The curve shows repeated current minima, typically about 4.9V apart. In the collision model, an electron gains approximately eV of kinetic energy, with e the positive elementary charge. An inelastic collision transfers about 4.9eV to internal excitation. Near the grid, this can prevent the electron crossing the retarding region, lowering collected electric current. Further acceleration restores collection; repeated excitation losses produce further minima. These represent multiple collisions, rather than equally spaced atomic energy eigenvalues. Contact potentials shift the first minimum, so compare several successive spacings instead of identifying the first dip's voltage with the excitation cost. [ref].
The optical comparison supplies a second observable: mercury radiation at a wavelength near 253.6nm was detected through a window in the tube. Its inferred photon energy hc/λ≈4.89eV agrees with the characteristic collision loss, with c the speed of light. This agreement supports the interpretation as a transition between internal atomic energies. It does not make the collector trace a complete spectrum. In particular, the lowest excited mercury level, labeled 63P0​, is metastable because an electric-dipole selection rule forbids its direct transition to the ground state; the nearby 63P1​ level emits the quoted ultraviolet line. Collision excitation and optical detection therefore have different sensitivities to the available excited states. [ref].
Initial electron kinetic energy spread and varying collision positions broaden real minima. Repeat traces at different retarding settings and oven temperatures to distinguish excitation from collection effects. This probes a discrete energy-transfer channel, rather than every energy eigenspace, its dimension, or an absolute ground-state energy. [ref].
James Franck and Gustav Ludwig Hertz presented their paper on 24 April 1914. Its heated platinum wire sat inside a cylindrical platinum mesh, with a collector 1–2 mm beyond it. A heated bath and pumping prepared the vapor; guard rings suppressed leakage. They differentiated retarding-electric current scans to study outgoing energies.[ref]
Their 1925 Nobel Prize preceded their lectures of 11 December 1926. James Franck described choosing pressure for many collisions before the mesh but few afterward.[ref] Gustav Ludwig Hertz explained how a field-free collision region narrowed excitation thresholds.[ref]

Franck-Hertz energy-loss model (Δ, d=Δ/e)

Words: 1k Articles: 3
Franck-Hertz experiment OurBigBook.com
A useful Franck-Hertz energy-loss model separates three operations: accelerating an electron, losing a discrete energy Δ in an inelastic collision, and selecting the surviving electrons with a retarding collector. The collector measures an accepted fraction of an outgoing energy distribution. This makes the experiment a small inverse problem: the electric current depends on both the collision physics and the readout.
For a deliberately simple one-channel model, let u≥0 be the incoming kinetic energy, and let p(u) be the probability that one encounter excites the chemical atom. Set p(u)=0 for u<Δ and 0≤p(u)≤1 otherwise. The conditional probability distribution of outgoing kinetic energy E is the discrete loss kernel
K(E∣u)=[1−p(u)]δ(E−u)+p(u)δ(E−u+Δ).
(8)
Here δ is the Dirac delta function. The kernel has unit integral, no negative outgoing energy, and mean outgoing kinetic energy u−p(u)Δ. It neglects the small recoil loss in an elastic collision, other excitation channels and ionization. The threshold permits excitation; it does not require p(u)=1 above threshold. In particular, choosing a single Δ=4.9eV here isolates the characteristic channel discussed above without claiming it is mercury's lowest possible excitation.
Franck-Hertz retarding collector transfer (T(E)=H(E−eVr​))
Words: 298
Franck-Hertz energy-loss model OurBigBook.com
For electrons directed normally toward an ideal planar collector, a retarding electric potential difference Vr​>0 imposes the barrier eVr​. Write its transfer as T(E)=H(E−eVr​), where H is the Heaviside step function and e the positive elementary charge. Thus a perfectly sharp loss need not produce a sharp drop in electric current: the drop also depends on where the outgoing energy lies relative to the barrier. For an oblique incident electron at angle θ to the normal, the same planar model instead tests Ecos2θ>eVr​; angular scattering can change collection even at unchanged total kinetic energy.
For example, choose a monoenergetic incoming beam with u=5.6eV, p=0.75 and Δ=4.9eV. After one encounter, 25% have 5.6eV and 75% have 0.7eV. A 1.0V retarder collects only 0.25I0​, whereas a 0.5V retarder collects I0​; here I0​ is the incident electric current with fixed geometrical acceptance and no background. The mean outgoing kinetic energy is 1.925eV, independently checked by 5.6−0.75(4.9). These are chosen inputs and calculated fractions, not historical measurements.
More generally, let f(E;V) be the normalized outgoing probability density function at a fixed accelerating electric potential difference V, for the normally incident idealization. A retarding scan gives
I0​I(V,Vr​)​=∫eVr​∞​f(E;V)dE,f(eVr​;V)=−eI0​1​∂Vr​∂I​.
(9)
The second equation follows by differentiating the lower integration limit, with Dirac delta functions understood for sharp beams. It explains why an integrated collector readout can reveal an energy distribution. With angles present it probes the distribution of normal kinetic energy; finite instrumental resolution smooths it, and numerical differentiation amplifies noise. Keep the incoming beam fixed during this scan. A scan of V changes f itself and cannot be differentiated by the same inversion formula.
Franck-Hertz repeated-loss collection model (En​=E0​+e(V−Vc​)−nΔ)
Words: 490
Franck-Hertz energy-loss model OurBigBook.com
Define a signed offset Vc​ so that the accelerating region adds kinetic energy e(V−Vc​). This convention concerns this gap; collector contact fields belong in the effective Vr​. An electron emitted with initial kinetic energy E0​ and undergoing n identical excitation losses reaches the grid with
En​=E0​+e(V−Vc​)−nΔ.
(10)
Each loss excites a fresh chemical atom initially in its ground state. These are repeated payments of the same excitation cost. The index n counts events along an electron trajectory, not the ordinal number of an atomic excited state. The outgoing energy also does not locate the last collision: after an early collision an electron can regain enough kinetic energy to cross the barrier.
At fixed V, let g(E0​) be the normalized initial-energy probability density function within the ensemble of electrons reaching the grid, and wn​(E0​,V) the conditional probabilities for n losses within that ensemble. Then the ideal collection law is
I0​I(V,Vr​)​=∫0∞​g(E0​)∑n=0∞​wn​(E0​,V)H(E0​+e(V−Vc​)−nΔ−eVr​)dE0​.
(11)
Take ∑n​wn​=1 and exclude paths with insufficient kinetic energy for a loss at its actual collision position. The weights contain collision positions and rates, mean free paths and angular acceptance. This equation is a bookkeeping model, not a derivation of those weights. Multiplying all incident particles by one constant transmission is an additional approximation; real transmission and I0​ can vary with V.
One analytical extreme assumes E0​=0, one-dimensional motion and an immediate loss whenever the kinetic energy reaches Δ. With d=Δ/e, x=V−Vc​≥0 and 0<Vr​<d, the number of losses and remaining kinetic energy are
n=⌊dx​⌋,eEn​​=r=x−d⌊dx​⌋,I0​I​=H(r−Vr​).
(12)
Here ⌊⋅⌋ is the floor function. Collection switches off for an interval of width Vr​ after each reset, then recovers; the pattern repeats every d. For d=4.9V, Vc​=1.2V and Vr​=1.0V, an applied V=11.4V gives n=2, r=0.4V and rejection, whereas V=12.2V gives n=2, r=1.2V and collection. With Vr​=0 the dips disappear in this idealization. With no collisions, there is only a single collection onset. Both limits expose the separate roles of loss and filtering.
To draw a smooth illustrative curve, extend this periodic rule mathematically to all x and convolve it with a chosen normal distribution of voltage jitter, of standard deviation σ:
Cσ​(x)=∑k∈Z​[Φ(σx−kd−Vr​​)−Φ(σx−(k+1)d​)].
(13)
Here Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Each summand is the smoothed indicator of the accepted interval kd+Vr​<x<(k+1)d. The result satisfies 0≤Cσ​≤1 and has period d. For the figure choose σ=0.18V and plot only well beyond the startup region. This is a chosen phenomenological curve, not a Boltzmann equation transport prediction or a fit to measured electric current. Its symmetric troughs center on Vc​+nd+Vr​/2, rather than the reset voltages Vc​+nd. Finite excitation rates, several loss channels, space charge, initial angular and energy spreads, and real electrode geometry can change the trough shape and phase; a single convolution does not solve these effects.
Franck-Hertz spacing fit and contact offset (Vn​=b+nd,Δ=ed)
Words: 311
Franck-Hertz energy-loss model OurBigBook.com
Fit the positions of successive, consistently defined troughs by linear regression, Vn​=b+nd+ϵn​. A constant intercept b absorbs the contact potential difference, collection phase and initial energy effects; d estimates the repeated loss expressed as a voltage. Subtracting successive positions cancels b. A line fit uses every position and leaves residuals that can reveal curvature or a misidentified trough. It still assumes that the phase is constant over the fitted range and that all troughs belong to the same loss channel.
For an original numerical example, choose n=1,2,3,4,5 and synthetic positions Vn​=(6.62,11.46,16.48,21.30,26.14)V. Their successive spacings are (4.84,5.02,4.82,4.84)V. The ordinary least squares formulas give
d=∑n​(n−nˉ)2∑n​(n−nˉ)(Vn​−Vˉ)​=4.888V,b=Vˉ−dnˉ=1.736V.
(14)
The fitted residuals are (−0.004,−0.052,0.080,0.012,−0.036)V, with zero sum and zero index-weighted sum. Their residual sum of squares is 0.01056V2. If independent, equal-variance, zero-mean errors describe these positions, the residual variance estimate is s2=0.01056/(5−2)=0.00352V2 and the slope standard error is s/10​=0.01876V. Thus the inferred model loss is 4.888eV with conditional standard error 0.01876eV. These errors summarize only the chosen position scatter; a voltage-scale error or a drifting collection phase is not included. Adjacent spacings share a position error and are therefore correlated even when position errors are independent.
The offset has a sharper identifiability limitation. Even if a constant-phase model were exact, an initial beam kinetic energy E0​ shifts the troughs by −E0​/e. For the symmetric illustrative curve, b=Vc​−E0​/e+Vr​/2. The transformation Vc​↦Vc​+α, E0​↦E0​+eα leaves every trough position unchanged. Measuring b alone therefore cannot determine the contact potential difference. Relabeling n↦n+m also changes b by −md without changing d. An independent offset calibration and a justified collection model are needed before turning a first trough into an excitation threshold. Varying Vr​ tests this model's predicted half-voltage shift of the trough centers; it is a diagnostic prediction of this chosen curve, not a universal correction for experimental minima.
