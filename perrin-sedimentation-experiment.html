Perrin sedimentation experiment

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Stochastic process Brownian motion
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
A colloidal grain in a liquid is large enough to see with a microscope, yet small enough for thermal motion to compete with its tendency to settle. In the Perrin sedimentation experiment, the equilibrium number density falls with height: a visible miniature atmosphere. Counting grains at different heights measures an energy scale; tracking their Brownian motion measures a transport rate. Connecting both to the same Avogadro constant makes the experiment a quantitative test of kinetic theory. The Wiener measure describes an idealized path law; these measurements instead test a physical model and its material parameters.
Jean Baptiste Perrin's 1909 memoir traces his sedimentation experiments to May 1908. It reports relative counts 100, 47, 22.6, 12 at heights 5, 35, 65, 95μm, and compares radius estimates from Stokes settling velocity, counted grain mass, and rows of touching grains.[ref]
The 1926 lecture describes resin precipitated from alcohol into water, washed and fractionated by centrifugation to obtain nearly monodisperse gamboge or mastic grains. Diameter and density were measured separately. A sealed slide-and-cover-glass chamber prevented evaporation; a vertical microscope resolved roughly 2μm layers through a roughly 0.1mm depth. Refocusing and photographing compared layer populations after equilibrium; changing temperature reversibly changed the distribution.[ref]
In Les Atomes (1913), Jean Baptiste Perrin restricts the eyepiece view to fewer than five or six grains and alternates observations between heights. Focus displacement requires a refractive index correction to recover actual layer separation. Horizontal positions were marked with a camera lucida, usually every 30s, and distances calibrated with a micrometer; the polygon joins sampled positions rather than recording the intervening trajectory.[ref][ref]
