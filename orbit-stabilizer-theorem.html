Orbit-stabilizer theorem (∣Gx∣=[G:Gx​])

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Group theory Group action
OurBigBook.com Words: 383 Articles: 1
For a group action of any group G on a set X, the cardinality of the orbit of a group action through x is the index of a subgroup of its stabilizer subgroup:
∣Gx∣=[G:Gx​],Gx​={g∈G:g⋅x=x}.
(34)
In particular, if G is a finite group,
∣G∣=∣Gx∣∣Gx​∣,∣Gx∣=∣Gx​∣∣G∣​.
(35)
For a fixed finite group, a larger stabilizer subgroup means a smaller group orbit: more transformations repeat the same image of x. The set X itself need not be finite.
The underlying bijection uses left cosets, without requiring Gx​ to be a normal subgroup:
Φ:{gGx​:g∈G}⟶Gx,gGx​⟼g⋅x.
(36)
To check it briefly, replacing g by gh with h∈Gx​ preserves g⋅x, so Φ is well-defined. Every member of the group orbit has this form. Finally, g⋅x=k⋅x implies k−1g∈Gx​, hence gGx​=kGx​, proving the bijection. This is the construction in MIT's group-action notes, lemma 23.5. For infinite groups, the bijection and the cardinality statement ∣Gx∣=[G:Gx​] still hold; division of infinite cardinalities does not supply the finite formula.
For the eight-element dihedral group D4​ acting on the square described under group action, moving a marked vertex of a polygon gives 8=4⋅2. Moving a marked side of a polygon also gives 8=4⋅2; moving one unoriented diagonal of a polygon gives 8=2⋅4; moving the centroid gives 8=1⋅8. These are four different group actions, each with one group orbit. In the first group action, the four left cosets of H=GA​={e,s} are
H={e,s},rH={r,rs},r2H={r2,r2s},r3H={r3,r3s}.
(37)
They correspond respectively to A,B,C,D. Counting all eight transformations would count each position of the marked vertex of a polygon twice.
For a second computed example, let the symmetric group S3​ act on the Cartesian product X={1,2,3}2 by σ⋅(i,j)=(σ(i),σ(j)). There are two orbits of a group action:
S3​⋅(1,1)={(1,1),(2,2),(3,3)},(S3​)(1,1)​={e,(23)},
(38)
and
S3​⋅(1,2)={(1,2),(1,3),(2,1),(2,3),(3,1),(3,2)},(S3​)(1,2)​={e}.
(39)
The first stabilizer subgroup can exchange 2 and 3 while fixing 1; the second must fix both 1 and 2, so also fixes 3. The orbit-stabilizer theorem gives 6=3⋅2 and 6=6⋅1, and the two group orbit sizes account for all nine ordered pairs. The general reason for exactly these two group orbits is equality-pattern orbits of ordered pairs.
