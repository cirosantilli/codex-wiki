Andrews's carbon dioxide experiment

 ... Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Pressure Equation of state Van der Waals equation
OurBigBook.com Words: 162
The 1869 The Bakerian Lecture: On the Continuity of the Gaseous and Liquid States of Matter by Thomas Andrews describes dried carbon dioxide trapped above mercury in a sealed capillary tube. A steel screw pressurized water in a strong copper tube; movable mercury transmitted the load. A second capillary tube held air as a pressure gauge. Flowing water around the visible capillary tubes controlled temperature; a thermometer read the bath. A cathetometer read column positions, converted to volume readings using weighed-mercury calibration.[ref]
Below the critical temperature, compression produced a visible liquid–gas boundary; above it, large density changes occurred without that boundary. Three trials gave 30.92∘C as his critical temperature. Purity checks detected residual air; mercury height corrections accounted for unequal hydrostatic pressures. He explicitly left air nonideality and pressure-induced tube-capacity corrections unresolved, so his air-gauge pressures were approximate. These readouts support a liquid-gas critical point; they do not measure a homogeneous unstable loop or establish universal quantitative accuracy of the later Van der Waals equation.[ref]
