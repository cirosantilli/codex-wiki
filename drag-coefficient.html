Drag coefficient (CD​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics Drag (physics)
OurBigBook.com Words: 388
The drag coefficient measures a drag force against a specified scale of force. Let U=vbody​−u∞​ be the body's velocity relative to the distant, undisturbed fluid, and let U=∣U∣>0 be its relative speed. For fluid density ρ, drag force magnitude FD​ and reference area A>0, define
CD​=21​ρU2AFD​​,FD​=21​ρU2ACD​.
(49)
The drag coefficient is a dimensionless quantity: the denominator has units of force. The reference area must be reported with it. For a sphere of radius a, the usual choice is the frontal area A=πa2. Choosing its entire surface area 4πa2 instead divides the reported drag coefficient by four for the same physical drag force. Comparing coefficients without comparing their reference areas can therefore be misleading. [ref]
For a rigid sphere in steady flow through an unbounded Newtonian fluid with constant dynamic viscosity μ, incompressible flow and the no-slip boundary condition, the creeping-flow limit requires the diameter-based particle Reynolds number Red​=2aρU/μ≪1. The Stokes drag law gives FD​=6πμaU. Substitution into the definition with A=πa2 gives the short derivation
CD​=21​ρU2πa26πμaU​=ρaU12μ​=Red​24​.
(50)
Thus the drag coefficient is neither necessarily constant nor bounded above by one. The formula containing U2 still describes linear drag here, because the drag coefficient varies as 1/U. The relation CD​=24/Red​ is the leading small-Reynolds number approximation, not a measured curve valid at every Reynolds number. [ref]
For a worked example, stipulate dynamic viscosity μ=0.10Pas, density ρ=1000kgm−3, radius a=10−4m and relative speed U=10−3ms−1. Within the stated Stokes drag law assumptions,
Red​=0.002,CD​=12000,FD​=6π×10−8N≈1.88495559×10−7N.
(51)
Doubling the relative speed with all other parameters fixed gives Red​=0.004 and CD​=6000: the predicted drag force doubles to 3.76991118×10−7N. Falsely holding the drag coefficient at 12000 would instead quadruple the drag force. Both particle Reynolds numbers remain small, so the comparison is within the creeping-flow approximation.
The useful idea is to separate the force scale from the dependence on geometry and flow conditions. A drag coefficient can help compare experiments, but changing Reynolds number can change the coefficient itself; NASA Glenn explicitly stresses matching the relevant flow conditions. The elegant cancellation above also exposes a common mistake: a drag coefficient definition does not by itself impose a quadratic drag law.
