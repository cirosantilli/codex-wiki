Inclusion-exclusion principle

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Combinatorics
OurBigBook.com Words: 581 Articles: 6
For finite sets A1​,…,An​, the cardinality of their union is
∣⋃i=1n​Ai​∣=∑∅=J⊆{1,…,n}​(−1)∣J∣+1​⋂j∈J​Aj​​.
(65)
Add the individual cardinalities, subtract pairwise intersection counts, add triple intersection counts, and continue alternating. This is useful whenever one object can satisfy several conditions: a search result can match several filters, or a permutation can have several forbidden fixed points.
Among the integers from 1 to 30, let A contain the multiples of 2 and B the multiples of 3. Then ∣A∣=15, ∣B∣=10, and ∣A∩B∣=5, because their common multiples are the multiples of 6. The naive sum 25 counts those five twice; the correct union size is 15+10−5=20. The regions contain ten in A alone, five in both, five in B alone, and ten in neither.
Add a third set C containing the multiples of 5. Its size is 6; the three pairwise intersections have sizes 5,3,2; and the triple intersection contains only 30. The inclusion-exclusion principle gives 15+10+6−5−3−2+1=22 integers divisible by at least one of 2, 3, and 5.
The alternating corrections have a local explanation. An object belonging to exactly r≥1 of the sets is counted (1r​)−(2r​)+⋯+(−1)r+1(rr​)=1 times, by the binomial theorem applied to (1−1)r. An object in none contributes zero. Thus overlapping descriptions are repaired without requiring the sets themselves to be disjoint. For probability, the same calculation applies to event probabilities in place of cardinalities, giving the inclusion-exclusion principle for probabilities.
