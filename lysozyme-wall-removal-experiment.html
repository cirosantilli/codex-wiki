Lysozyme wall-removal experiment

 Home Biology Biological cell Cell wall Protoplast
OurBigBook.com Words: 231
The 1953 paper The isolation of protoplasts from Bacillus megaterium by controlled treatment with lysozyme by Claes Weibull (pp. 688–690, Figures 2–3) tested Bacillus megaterium strain KM. Apparatus included a Klett-Summerson colorimeter with a red filter and a Spencer no. 8 phase-contrast microscope. Lysozyme in phosphate buffer produced empty residual bodies; adding 0.2M sucrose preserved granular spheres. Diluting their surroundings caused rapid osmotic lysis.
The same study compared untreated, boiled, lysozyme-exposed and sucrose-protected samples. Filled, stoppered tubes reduced damage from air exposure. After centrifugation, a Beckman DU spectrophotometer measured ultraviolet wave absorption of the separated liquid. Protected samples resembled untreated controls in releasing little ultraviolet-absorbing material; boiled and unprotected samples released much more. Later-growth cultures reduced spontaneous breakdown as a confounder.[ref]
Together, the shape and release readouts support removal of mechanical protection while a permeability barrier persists. They do not establish unchanged selective permeability to every solute, long-term viability, or universal susceptibility.
An instructive extension of this experimental logic is a matched comparison: lysozyme present or absent, crossed with osmotic support present or absent. Keep starting material, buffer, observation interval and handling comparable; include a blank to distinguish optical effects of reagents from changes in the biological cells. Then dilute already formed protoplasts as a separate intervention. This separates access and wall removal from failure after removal. These are proposed controls for interpreting the mechanism, not additional reported results from the historical paper. A fall in optical signal alone cannot establish rupture: shape changes also alter scattering of light.
