Spin-reversal cross-ratio for beta decay (ϵ=(R​−1)/(R​+1))

Consider a teaching extension with two opposite detectors, labelled + and − by fixed laboratory directions, and two preparation states s=+1,−1 with equal and opposite nuclear polarization. This is an original two-detector model; the historical Wu experiment used a fixed internal electron counter with reversal.[ref] Let Cs​>0 describe the exposure in each state and ηd​>0 the stable detection efficiency of detector d.
For matched opposite acceptances, write their common mean absolute cosine as k. The four expected positive counts are
N++​=C+​η+​(1+ϵ),N−+​=C+​η−​(1−ϵ),N+−​=C−​η+​(1−ϵ),N−−​=C−​η−​(1+ϵ),ϵ=ak,∣ϵ∣<1.
(5)
The same-state ratios each retain the unequal detection efficiencies:
N−+​N++​​=η−​η+​​1−ϵ1+ϵ​,N−−​N+−​​=η−​η+​​1+ϵ1−ϵ​.
(6)
Dividing these ratios gives the spin-reversal cross-ratio for beta decay:
R=N−+​N+−​N++​N−−​​=(1−ϵ1+ϵ​)2,ϵ=R​+1R​−1​.
(7)
Both the exposure ratio C+​/C−​ and the detection efficiency ratio η+​/η−​ cancel. The positive square root is essential: positive expected counts and ∣ϵ∣<1 make (1+ϵ)/(1−ϵ) positive.
For the chosen a=−0.3, opposite hemispheres give k=1/2, hence ϵ=−0.15. Choose C+​=10000, C−​=8000, η+​=0.8, and η−​=0.5, with their common acceptance factor absorbed into Cs​. The four expected counts in the displayed order are 6800, 5750, 7360, and 3400. Thus
N−+​N++​​=115136​,N−−​N+−​​=85184​,R=529289​,R​=2317​,ϵ=−203​.
(8)
The first raw detector ratio exceeds one despite negative ϵ: detector imbalance can overwhelm the physical directional preference. Reversal separates the stable imbalance from the term that changes sign.
