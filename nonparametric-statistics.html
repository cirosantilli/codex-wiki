Rank test

Words: 54
Nonparametric statistics OurBigBook.com
A rank test is a hypothesis test whose statistic is a function of the ranks of observations, possibly together with fixed sample labels. Under independent observations from a common continuous probability distribution, exchangeable rank allocations give its exact null law without specifying that distribution. The Wilcoxon rank sum test is an example. Invariance under common increasing transformations follows because ranks do not change.

Wilcoxon signed-rank test (W+​=∑Di​>0​rank(∣Di​∣))

Words: 257 Articles: 2
Nonparametric statistics OurBigBook.com
For independent paired observations, form differences Di​=Ui​−Vi​. Assume the differences are identically distributed, continuous and symmetric about a location δ. Under the zero-location null hypothesis, the signs are independent fair signs conditional on their absolute values. Rank the absolute differences and add ranks with positive signs. A negative-location alternative rejects for small W+​. Its exact conditional distribution is that of a sum of an independently selected subset of ranks, each of the 2n subsets equally likely. Thus its mean is n(n+1)/4 and its variance is n(n+1)(2n+1)/24. Symmetry is essential: a zero median alone does not justify this signed-rank null law. Zero differences are omitted in the usual version, and tied absolute differences receive average ranks.

Walsh average ((Xi​+Xj​)/2)

Words: 79
Wilcoxon signed-rank test OurBigBook.com
The Walsh averages of a sample are (Xi​+Xj​)/2 for 1≤i≤j≤n, including the diagonal pairs. If the absolute observations are distinct and nonzero, order them as ∣X(1)​∣<⋯<∣X(n)​∣. Among the r pairs whose larger absolute observation is X(r)​, every sum has the sign of X(r)​. Hence the number of positive Walsh averages is exactly ∑r​r1X(r)​>0​, the positive Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic. This proves equivalence of the corresponding tests; the Walsh averages themselves are generally dependent.

Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic (W+​=∑Xi​>0​rank(∣Xi​∣))

Words: 61
Wilcoxon signed-rank test OurBigBook.com
For a continuous sample, the statistic also equals ∑i​1{Xi​>0}​+∑i<j​1{Xi​+Xj​>0}​. In each pair the sign of the sum is the sign of the observation with larger absolute value, proving the identity by counting its smaller-rank partners. Under a continuous symmetric null, the pair kernel has projection F(x)−1/2 and projection variance 1/12. The U-statistic central limit theorem yields asymptotic normality of W+​, with variance asymptotic to n3/12.

Mann–Whitney U test (UX​=WX​−m(m+1)/2)

Words: 308 Articles: 3
Nonparametric statistics OurBigBook.com
For two independent samples of sizes m,n, rank their pooled observations and let WX​ be the sum of the ranks of the first sample. With continuous observations, the null hypothesis of a common distribution makes every allocation of m ranks to that sample equally probable. A one-sided alternative that the first sample is stochastically smaller rejects for small WX​, equivalently small UX​. In a common-shape location model this tests a negative location difference. Without the common-shape assumption it is not simply a test of medians. The shifted statistic UX​ counts cross-sample pairs for which the first observation exceeds the second. Ties use average ranks and a conditional permutation test or a tie-adjusted normal approximation.

Exact rank-sum tail one above the minimum (Pr{W≤m(m+1)/2+1}=2/(mm+n​))

Words: 72
Exact null distribution of a rank sum OurBigBook.com
For two independent continuous samples of positive sizes m,n under a common-distribution null, each m-subset of ranks 1,…,m+n is equally likely. The smallest rank sum is m(m+1)/2, attained only by {1,…,m}. The only subset with rank sum one larger is {1,…,m−1,m+1}. Hence the exact lower tail at that threshold is 2/(mm+n​). This follows directly by observing that any other change increases the sum by at least two.

Empirical likelihood places probability masses on observed or data-supported values and maximizes their product subject to normalization and any estimating constraints.

Empirical likelihood with mixed censoring

Words: 268 Articles: 2
Empirical likelihood OurBigBook.com
For independent event times with survivor function S(t)=P(T>t), an exact event at t contributes mass S(t−)−S(t), a right-censored observation at c contributes S(c), and an interval-censored observation l<T≤u contributes S(l)−S(u). Multiplying these factors gives the event-time likelihood when the observation mechanism is noninformative. Maximizing over nonincreasing right-continuous survivor functions gives a nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimator rather than requiring a parametric density.

Likelihood support reduction under censoring (L(w)=∏i​(∑j​Aij​wj​))

Words: 116
Empirical likelihood with mixed censoring OurBigBook.com
Partition the time axis into cells on which membership in each observed exact-event or censoring set is constant. Let wj​ be a cell's probability mass and Aij​ indicate whether cell j is permitted by observation i. The empirical likelihood has the displayed form, with nonnegative masses summing to one. Columns of A that agree can be combined. If column j is componentwise dominated by another column k, moving all mass from j to k cannot reduce any factor, so an unconstrained nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimator can omit column j. The remaining log-likelihood is a concave function of the masses. Extra constraints can prevent a domination move; for a fixed survivor function value, split cells at the constrained time and retain the associated linear mass constraint.

A Haar density estimator estimates the Haar scaling function coefficients by sample averages. It is exactly the histogram with cells of width 2−j. Its expectation is the Haar projection of the density. If the density has bounded derivative L and is bounded by B, its pointwise expected absolute error is at most L2−j+B2j/n​: the first term is the Haar projection error for a Lipschitz function, and the second follows from the variance of a cell count and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Flat-top kernel for density estimation (K(t)=1 near 0)

Words: 160 Articles: 1
Kernel for density estimation OurBigBook.com
Choose a real even smooth compactly supported Fourier transform η equal to one near zero and let K be its inverse Fourier transform. Repeated integration by parts makes K a Schwartz function. It is real, integrable and square-integrable, with unit integral. Moment differentiation of the Fourier transform gives ∫xjK(x)dx=ijη(j)(0)=0 for every positive integer j. Such a kernel for density estimation therefore satisfies arbitrary finite-order moment cancellation, and necessarily takes negative values. If the transform plateau contains the frequency support of a bandlimited function f, the convolution theorem proves K∗f=f.

Quadratic smoothing spline for interval averages (∑i​(Yi​−Li​g)2+λ∫ab​(g′)2)

Words: 118
Smoothing spline OurBigBook.com
Let Li​g be the average of g on successive knot intervals. Minimizing the displayed loss over C1[a,b] gives a quadratic spline with constant tails outside the first and last knots. To prove the reduction, choose the unique constant-tail quadratic spline q with the same interval averages as a candidate g, and put v=g−q. Since q′′ is constant on every knot interval, q′ is continuous and vanishes on both tails, integration by parts gives ∫q′v′=−∑i​q′′∣Ii​​∫Ii​​v=0. Consequently the data loss is unchanged and ∫(g′)2=∫(q′)2+∫(v′)2. The penalty decreases strictly unless v is constant; its zero interval averages then force v=0. The finite-dimensional quadratic optimization on this spline space also gives a unique minimizer because the interval-average map is injective.

Roughness-matrix formula for a natural cubic smoothing spline (Aλ​=(I+λK)−1)
