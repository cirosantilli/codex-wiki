Water-column wind-tunnel pressure measurement

 Home Physics Branches of physics Fluid mechanics Manometer U-tube differential manometer
OurBigBook.com Words: 304
NASA's Wind Tunnel Experiment Details, slide 27, describes a practical manometer measurement: partially fill a Tygon tube with water and connect its two ends to total pressure and static pressure ports in a wind tunnel. The total pressure connection pushes its liquid meniscus downward; the vertical length difference supplies the pressure difference. The slide predicts increasing separation as the tunnel speed increases. It supplies an apparatus and method, rather than a table of observed readings.[ref]
A control for this practical is to expose both connections to the same atmospheric pressure before the run: settled liquid meniscus heights should agree in a symmetric column. Read each liquid meniscus at eye level, reconnect sealed tubing to the identified ports, and wait for settling. Recheck the equal-pressure zero afterwards; reverse connections to check the sign. These checks establish neither mass density nor ruler accuracy.[ref]
The inference is a differential pressure from hydrostatic pressure, with stagnation pressure on one side and static pressure on the other in NASA's practical. Converting that difference to a flow speed additionally needs the hypotheses discussed under dynamic pressure and Pitot-static tube. A changing pressure can leave the column oscillating; leaks, blocked pressure tappings or trapped liquid in a gas connection can alter or delay the readout. The column should be read after settling, and temperature should be recorded when choosing the measuring liquid's mass density.
Two corrections clarify the limits. If both gas ports are at the same height and carry the same gas of constant mass density ρg​, keeping its hydrostatic pressure gives pL​−pR​=(ρ−ρg​)gΔh. Different port heights require their separate gas column heights. Also, a curved liquid meniscus can sustain a capillary pressure jump through surface tension. Write JL​,JR​ for liquid pressure minus gas pressure at the two surfaces. Neglecting gas weight, the balance becomes pL​−pR​=ρgΔh+JR​−JL​. Identical meniscus shapes in matching clean bores cancel those jumps; unequal bores or different wetting conditions need not. Thus the useful simplicity of ρgΔh rests on identifiable physical conditions.
