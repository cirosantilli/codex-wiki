Little's law (L=λeff​W)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Queueing theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 734 Articles: 1
For a stable queue with finite long-run averages, a positive finite admitted rate, and negligible initial and terminal contributions after division by observation time, Little's law states
L=λeff​W.
(29)
Here L is the time-average queue population, λeff​ is the admitted rate through the chosen system, and W is the customer-average customer sojourn time inside that same boundary. Without abandonment, admitted rate equals completed-service queue throughput in this stable regime. It may differ from the offered arrival rate because of blocking. When customers abandon, count their admissions and exits too, and average their actual time inside; using only service completions would mismatch the population being measured.
For the waiting area alone the matching identity is
Lq​=λeff​Wq​,
(30)
where Wq​ is mean queueing delay. For one server with no abandonment, service in arrival order, and finite mean service time ES,
W=Wq​+ES,L=Lq​+λeff​ES=Lq​+ρ,
(31)
with ρ the server utilization. Mixing total population L with waiting-only duration Wq​ would count different portions of each customer's visit. Customers who start service immediately contribute zero to Wq​, and must still be included in that customer average.
The strength of Little's law is that it does not require a Poisson process, an exponential distribution of service times, or service in arrival order. The sample-path area identity for Little's law explains why: counting customer-time across time slices or across customer visits gives the same area. A sufficient probabilistic setting is repeated independent and identically distributed empty-to-empty cycles with finite positive mean cycle duration, finite positive mean customer count, and finite mean population area; the renewal-reward theorem then supplies the long-run averages. Stability alone does not guarantee finite mean queueing delay: the Pollaczek-Khinchine mean formula shows why a finite second moment of service time matters for that mean in an M/G/1 queue.
For example, suppose a service desk receives six requests per hour but admits only four; measured mean customer sojourn time of admitted requests is half an hour. Its mean queue population is 4⋅(1/2)=2 customers. Multiplying the offered rate by that duration would incorrectly give three. Conversely, counting two customers on average and measuring admitted queue throughput of four per hour gives W=2/4=1/2 hour. This lets one estimate a hard-to-track duration from occupancy and completed-job counts.
John D. C. Little published a rigorous proof in 1961 under finite-mean, strict stationarity, and ergodicity assumptions on the arrival process. Those were assumptions of that proof, rather than a requirement that every useful form of the identity have random arrivals. [ref]. [ref].
