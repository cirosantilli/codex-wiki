For a nonempty set A⊆R, a supremum is a real number s such that a≤s for every a∈A, and every upper bound u of A satisfies s≤u. Equivalently, s is an upper bound and, for every ε>0, some a∈A satisfies a>s−ε. By completeness of the real numbers, it exists whenever A is nonempty and bounded above.
An increasing monotone sequence satisfies an+1​≥an​ for every n; a bounded sequence satisfies ∣an​∣≤M for some finite M and every n. Put s=sup{an​:n≥1}. Given ε>0, the defining property of the supremum supplies N with aN​>s−ε. For every n≥N, monotonicity gives
s−ε<aN​≤an​≤s.
(1)
This is exactly the definition of convergent sequence, so the bounded monotone sequence theorem gives an​→s​.

A limit point p of E⊆R is a point such that, for every ε>0, the punctured neighbourhood (p−ε,p+ε)∖{p} meets E. A closed set contains all of its limit points.
Every nonempty open interval contains a rational number; applying this to (p,p+ε) shows that every p∈R is a limit point of Q. Thus the limit points of Q form R, and Q is not closed.
If p∈Z, radius 1/2 excludes all other integers. If p∈/Z, its distances to the two neighbouring integers have a positive minimum, and a smaller radius excludes every integer. Thus Z has no limit points in R and is closed.

Suppose E is a closed set and p∈/E. Then p is not a limit point of E, so some punctured neighbourhood of p misses E. As p∈/E, the entire neighbourhood misses E. Thus R∖E is an open set.
Conversely, suppose every point p∈/E has a neighbourhood disjoint from E. No such point can be a limit point of E, so every limit point lies in E. This proves the equivalence between containing all limit points and having an complement that is an open set.

Only continuity at zero and boundedness on [0,1] were needed for this final estimate; the assumed continuity everywhere also guarantees the ordinary integrals exist.
