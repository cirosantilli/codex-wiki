The three elementary row operations on an m×n real matrix are:
  • Interchange two distinct rows: Ri​↔Rj​.
  • Multiply one row by a nonzero real number: Ri​←cRi​, with c=0.
  • Add a scalar multiple of one row to a different row: Ri​←Ri​+cRj​, with i=j.
Each operation changes whole rows, so it acts on every column by the same rule. Each can be undone by an elementary row operation.

Pij​Di​(c)Lij​(c)​=Im​−Eii​−Ejj​+Eij​+Eji​,=Im​+(c−1)Eii​,=Im​+cEij​,​Pij−1​Di​(c)−1Lij​(c)−1​=Pij​,=Di​(c−1)(c=0),=Lij​(−c)(i=j).​

A vector space over a field F is a set V with vector addition V×V→V and scalar multiplication F×V→V satisfying the following vector space axioms. Under vector addition, V is an abelian group: addition is associative and commutative, there is a zero vector 0V​, and each v has an additive inverse −v. For all a,b∈F and u,v∈V,
a(u+v)=au+av,(a+b)v=av+bv,(ab)v=a(bv),1F​v=v.
(8)
The operations are defined for every indicated pair and take their values in V.

Solution

A map T:V→W between vector spaces over the same field F is a linear map precisely when it preserves vector addition and scalar multiplication:
T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v),T(av)=aT(v)(u,v∈V, a∈F).
(9)
Equivalently, T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v) for all u,v∈V and a,b∈F.

Words: 94 Articles: 1
a OurBigBook.com
Solution
Words: 94
iii OurBigBook.com
Every v∈V has a unique basis expansion v=∑i=1n​ai​vi​. Define the linear functional
T(i=1∑n​ai​vi​)=i=1∑n​ai​μi​.​
(10)
Uniqueness of the coordinates makes this definition well defined. The coordinates of a sum or a scalar multiple are the corresponding sums or scalar multiples of the coordinates, so this formula defines a linear map. It has T(vi​)=μi​. Conversely, any linear map with those values must satisfy the same formula by linearity, proving uniqueness.
If the finite field F has q elements, each of the n values μi​ can be chosen independently in q ways. The number of linear maps V→F is therefore qn​, including the zero linear map.

Use basis extension to choose a basis (v,v2​,…,vn​) of the vector space V over the finite field Fp​=Zp​. The condition T(v)=0 fixes the first value of a linear functional; its remaining n−1 values are arbitrary. There are pn−1 such linear maps, exactly one of which is zero. Hence the required number is
pn−1−1.​
(11)
For n=1 this is zero, as it should be: vanishing at a basis vector then forces the entire linear functional to vanish.

The real spectral theorem states that a symmetric matrix has an orthonormal basis of real eigenvectors, and hence is a diagonalisable matrix. Here is a proof in this setting.
For n≥1, the quadratic form q(x)=xTAx attains a maximum on the unit sphere, by compactness and the extreme value theorem. Choose a unit maximizer u and put λ=uTAu. For any w⊥u, the Rayleigh quotient along u+tw has a maximum at t=0, so
0=dtd​∥u+tw∥2(u+tw)TA(u+tw)​​t=0​=2wTAu.
(26)
The last equality uses A=AT and uTw=0. Thus Au is orthogonal to every w⊥u, so Au=λu.
The orthogonal complement u⊥ is an invariant subspace, because for x⊥u,
uTAx=(Au)Tx=λuTx=0.
(27)
The restriction of A to u⊥ is again symmetric with respect to the induced inner product. By mathematical induction on dimension, it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors; adjoining u gives one for Rn. If Q has those basis vectors as columns, then QTAQ is a real diagonal matrix and Q−1=QT.

gz3g−1=(gzg−1)3=T(g)T(z)T(g)−1=g3z3g−3.

(50)
This proves the abelian image of a cubing homomorphism property. Since the image of a group homomorphism is a subgroup, T(G) is an abelian group​.

The set Sn​ is nonempty because n∈Sn​. By the well-ordering principle for the natural numbers, it has a least element p. In particular p>1 and p divides n.
If p were not a prime number, it would have a positive integer divisor d with 1<d<p. Since d divides p and p divides n, divisibility is transitive and d divides n. This would put d in Sn​, contradicting the minimality of p. Hence p is prime​.

Solution

i OurBigBook.com
Conjugating a cycle simply relabels its entries. With permutations composed from right to left,
ρ(a1​ a2​ ⋯ aℓ​)ρ−1=(ρ(a1​) ρ(a2​) ⋯ ρ(aℓ​)).
(63)
This follows by applying each side to ρ(aj​) and to points outside the displayed cycle. Consequently conjugation preserves the lengths in the disjoint cycle decomposition, including fixed points, and therefore preserves cycle type.
Conversely, suppose two permutations have the same cycle type. Pair their cycles of equal length, including their one-cycles. Define a bijection ρ by mapping the entries of each cycle of the first permutation, in their cyclic order, to the entries of the paired cycle of the second. Because the cycles partition {1,…,n}, these assignments combine into a permutation. The displayed identity then conjugates the first permutation to the second. Thus they are conjugate group elements exactly when their cycle types agree.

b (Every automorphism is inner except at degree six)

i

Words: 105 Articles: 1

Solution
Words: 105
i OurBigBook.com
Assume first n≥2. All transpositions form a single conjugacy class T1​. A group automorphism preserves order of a group element, so the image of a transposition is a nonidentity involution. By the order formula, its disjoint cycle decomposition contains only one-cycles and two-cycles, with at least one two-cycle. Thus it lies in some Tk​, where 1≤k≤⌊n/2⌋.
Moreover an automorphism maps the entire conjugacy class onto the conjugacy class of the image: for a fixed transposition t,
ϕ(gtg−1)=ϕ(g)ϕ(t)ϕ(g)−1,
(70)
and ϕ(g) runs over all of Sn​ by surjectivity. Part (a)(i) identifies that latter conjugacy class as Tk​. Hence ϕ(T1​)=Tk​​, with equality rather than just inclusion. If n=1, T1​ is empty and the equality holds trivially with k=1.

ii

Words: 368 Articles: 1
b OurBigBook.com
Solution
Words: 368
ii OurBigBook.com
For n≥2 with n=6, bijectivity of ϕ and the previous part imply
2kk!(n−2k)!n!​=∣Tk​∣=∣T1​∣=2n(n−1)​.
(71)
The supplied numerical fact forces k=1. Thus ϕ permutes the transpositions.
Suppose n≥3, and write sr​=ϕ((1r)) for r=2,…,n. These are distinct transpositions. For r=t, the product (1r)(1t) has order of a group element three, so sr​st​ also has order three. Two distinct transpositions have this property precisely when their two-element sets of moved points meet in one point: disjoint ones have a product of order two, whereas ones sharing one point have a three-cycle as product. The two-element support of a permutation associated with each sr​ therefore meets that of every other st​.
We now show that they have a common point. Choose two; the support of a permutation is {a,b} for one and {a,c} for the other, where a,b,c are distinct. Any further two-element set meeting both either contains a or is {b,c}. If {b,c} occurs, any two-element set meeting all three must itself be one of {a,b},{a,c},{b,c}: a set containing a must also contain b or c, and a set omitting a must contain both b,c. Hence a pairwise-intersecting family with no common point consists of exactly those three edges of a triangle.
For n≥5, there are n−1≥4 distinct sr​, ruling out that triangle. For n=3, the two sets already have a common point. For n=4, the triangle alternative would make all three sr​ fix the fourth point. They would then generate a proper subgroup of S4​, contrary to surjectivity of ϕ and part (a)(iv), which imply that the sr​ generate S4​. Thus in every case n≥3 there is a common point a, and we may write
sr​=(a br​)(2≤r≤n).
(72)
The br​ are distinct and different from a, so they exhaust the remaining n−1 points. Define a permutation σ by
σ(a)=1,σ(br​)=r.
(73)
Then
σ−1(1r)σ=(σ−1(1) σ−1(r))=(a br​)=ϕ((1r)).
(74)
The two group homomorphisms τ↦ϕ(τ) and τ↦σ−1τσ agree on a generating set of a group, so agree on every product of its elements, hence throughout Sn​:
ϕ(τ)=σ−1τσ(τ∈Sn​).​
(75)
Finally, S1​ is trivial and S2​ has a unique nonidentity element, so every automorphism of either is the identity and we can take σ=e. This completes all degrees n=6 and proves that every such automorphism is an inner automorphism.
