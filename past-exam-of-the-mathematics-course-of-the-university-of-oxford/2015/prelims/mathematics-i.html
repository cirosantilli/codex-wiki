An elementary row operation is one of three reversible changes: interchange two rows; multiply one row by a nonzero scalar; or add a scalar multiple of one row to a different row. Their inverses are, respectively, the same interchange, multiplication by the reciprocal, and addition of the negative scalar multiple.
Applied to an augmented matrix, these changes interchange linear equations, rescale an equation by a nonzero factor, or add a multiple of one equation to another. Every old solution satisfies the new equations. Applying the inverse elementary row operation gives the converse, so the solution set is unchanged at every step. Equivalently, all the operations together give
[A∣b]=E[A∣b],Ax=b⟺EAx=Eb⟺Ax=b,
(11)
where E is an invertible matrix, a product of elementary matrices.

The first possibility makes a,b commute, and ab then has order of a group element six: (ab)k=e forces ak=b−k∈⟨a⟩∩⟨b⟩={e}, so both 3 and 2 divide k. This contradicts our assumption. Therefore
bab−1=a−1.
(56)
Every element is uniquely ai or aib, with 0≤i<3, and the relation ba=a−1b determines all their products. Sending a to (123) and b to (12) gives a group isomorphism with the symmetric group S3​. This completes the classification of groups of order at most six.
Of these groups, exactly C1​,C2​,C3​,C4​,C2​×C2​,S3​ embed in S4​. Concrete subgroups are
{e},⟨(12)⟩,⟨(123)⟩,⟨(1234)⟩,{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)},{σ∈S4​:σ(4)=4}.
(57)
There is no C5​ subgroup, since 5 does not divide 24. There is no C6​ subgroup either: the order of a group element in a symmetric group is the least common multiple of the lengths in its disjoint cycle decomposition, and on four letters the only possibilities are 1,2,3,4.

Label the vertices of a regular tetrahedron 1,2,3,4, and include all its Euclidean isometries, both rotations and reflections. The following list describes every subgroup of cardinality at most six, allowing arbitrary relabelling of the vertices. The numbers count actual subgroups, rather than just their group isomorphism types.
  • One trivial subgroup contains only the identity element.
  • Six reflection subgroups C2​ are generated by a transposition, for example (12). Its mirror plane contains vertices 3,4 and the midpoint of the opposite edge 12.
  • Three half-turn subgroups C2​ are generated by a product of two disjoint transpositions, for example (12)(34). The rotation axis joins the midpoints of the opposite edges 12 and 34.
  • Four cyclic subgroups C3​ consist of the three rotations about an axis joining a vertex to the centre of its opposite face. For example, ⟨(123)⟩ fixes vertex 4.
  • Three cyclic subgroups C4​ are generated by an improper orthogonal transformation: a quarter-turn about an axis through opposite edge midpoints, composed with a reflection in the perpendicular plane through the centre. For ⟨(1234)⟩, the axis joins the midpoints of edges 13 and 24. Its square is the half-turn (13)(24).
  • One rotational Klein four-group consists of the identity element and the three half-turns about opposite-edge midpoint axes:
    {e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)}.
    (58)
  • Three further Klein four-groups arise from a pair of opposite edges. For edges 12,34, the subgroup is
    {e,(12),(34),(12)(34)}.
    (59)
    It contains two reflections in perpendicular planes and their product, the half-turn about the line where those planes meet. It preserves each of the two opposite edges as a set.
  • Four subgroups S3​ are the stabilizer subgroups of individual vertices. Each contains three rotations about the vertex-to-opposite-face axis and three reflections in planes containing that axis. For the fixed vertex 4, this is every permutation of 1,2,3.
Here is why this is exhaustive. Part b leaves only the trivial group, C2​,C3​,C4​,C2​×C2​,S3​. The disjoint cycle decomposition gives exactly the two listed kinds of involutions, the three-cycles, and the four-cycles, accounting for all the cyclic subgroups. In a Klein four-group, the nonidentity elements commute. If it contains (12), any other nonidentity element must be a commuting involution, hence (34) or (12)(34); either choice yields the displayed opposite-edge subgroup. If it has no transposition, its three nonidentity elements must be all three double transpositions, yielding the rotational subgroup.
Finally, an S3​ subgroup has a unique cyclic subgroup of cardinality three. Its two three-cycles fix the same unique vertex. Every element of the S3​ subgroup preserves this vertex, since conjugation preserves that unique cyclic subgroup. Thus it is the full stabilizer subgroup of that vertex. The counts in the list are consequently exhaustive, giving
1+6+3+4+3+1+3+4=25 subgroups of order at most 6.​
(60)
The two kinds of C2​ and the two kinds of Klein four-groups have different geometric behavior despite having the same abstract group isomorphism type within each pair. This is the distinction recorded by the small subgroups of tetrahedral symmetry.

A left group action of G on a set S is a function G×S→S, written (g,s)↦g⋅s, satisfying
e⋅s=s,(gh)⋅s=g⋅(h⋅s)​
(61)
for all g,h∈G, s∈S. The orbit of a group action through s and the stabilizer subgroup of s are, respectively,
G⋅s={g⋅s:g∈G},Gs​={g∈G:g⋅s=s}.​
(62)
The identity element belongs to Gs​. If g,h∈Gs​, then (gh)⋅s=g⋅(h⋅s)=g⋅s=s, so gh∈Gs​. If g∈Gs​, applying g−1 to g⋅s=s gives s=g−1⋅s, so g−1∈Gs​. These facts prove that the stabilizer subgroup is a subgroup of G.

The orbit-stabilizer theorem identifies the orbit of a group action of s with the set of left cosets of Gs​. Explicitly, there is a bijection
G/Gs​⟶G⋅s,gGs​⟼g⋅s.​
(63)
Here G/Gs​ denotes a set of cosets; it is not asserted to be a quotient group. To prove the identification, observe that
gGs​=hGs​⟺h−1g∈Gs​⟺(h−1g)⋅s=s⟺g⋅s=h⋅s.
(64)
This equivalence proves both well-definedness and injectivity. Every element of the orbit of a group action has the form g⋅s, proving surjectivity.
In particular, for a finite group, the cardinalities satisfy
∣G⋅s∣=[G:Gs​]=∣Gs​∣∣G∣​,∣G∣=∣G⋅s∣∣Gs​∣.​
(65)
The last equality follows from Lagrange's theorem applied to the stabilizer subgroup.

The orbits of a group action form a partition of a set S: every element lies in its own orbit of a group action, and two intersecting orbits of a group action coincide because each point can be moved back and then forward using inverse elements. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, each orbit of a group action has cardinality dividing 8, so its size is 1,2,4 or 8.
If every orbit of a group action had size 2,4 or 8, their sizes would add to an even number, contrary to ∣S∣=17. Therefore there is a one-element orbit of a group action, say {s′}. Every g∈G sends s′ into this same singleton, so
g⋅s′=s′for every g∈G.​
(66)
Thus s′ is a fixed point of a group action. In fact the number of fixed points of a group action is odd, since all other orbits of a group action have even cardinality.

For a free group action, every stabilizer subgroup is {e}. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, every orbit of a group action has cardinality ∣G∣. Hence the two free group actions have the same number
r=∣G∣∣S∣​
(67)
of orbits of a group action. Choose one representative si​ from each ⋅ orbit of a group action and one representative ti​ from each ∗ orbit of a group action, and match them using 1≤i≤r.
Every s∈S has a unique expression s=g⋅si​. The orbit of a group action determines i, and the free group action property determines g: if g⋅si​=h⋅si​, then (h−1g)⋅si​=si​, forcing g=h. Therefore the rule
f(g⋅si​)=g∗ti​​
(68)
defines a function f:S→S without ambiguity. For h∈G,
f(h⋅(g⋅si​))=f((hg)⋅si​)=(hg)∗ti​=h∗(g∗ti​)=h∗f(g⋅si​),
(69)
which proves the required identity for every s.
Indeed, f is a bijection: its inverse is g∗ti​↦g⋅si​, well defined because the second is also a free group action. If S is empty, the unique empty function already satisfies the identity. Thus the result holds in every case, and proves the stronger statement that free finite actions with equal cardinality are isomorphic.
