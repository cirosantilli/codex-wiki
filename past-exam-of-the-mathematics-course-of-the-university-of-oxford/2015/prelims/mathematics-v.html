For an oriented surface Σ with piecewise smooth closed boundary ∂Σ, and a vector field F that is continuously differentiable on an open neighborhood of Σ, Stokes theorem states
∬Σ​(∇×F)⋅ndS=∮∂Σ​F⋅dr.​
(1)
Here n is the chosen unit normal vector. The orientation of ∂Σ follows the right-hand rule: with the right thumb pointing along n, the fingers curl in the positive direction. Equivalently, walking along the boundary with one's head along n leaves the surface on the left. Each boundary component receives this induced orientation.

Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
First, the Fourier coefficients of the Fourier cosine series decay as O(n−2), whereas those of the Fourier sine series are of order n−1. In particular, the absolute convergence bound for the Fourier cosine series gives a uniform tail estimate
sup0≤x≤L​∣g(x)−CN​(x)∣≤2(e+1)∑n>N​1+n2π21​=O(N−1).
(37)
Thus its tail is absolutely controlled using fewer Fourier harmonics. The Fourier sine series does not have an absolutely summable sequence of Fourier coefficients.
Second, the even extension is continuous, so its Fourier cosine series converges uniformly on the closed interval and approximates the nonzero endpoint limits correctly. The odd periodic extension has jumps, producing the Gibbs phenomenon in its Fourier sine series near zero and L. Every Fourier sine series partial sum is zero at both endpoints, so by approaching L from inside (0,L) one obtains
sup0<x<L​∣g(x)−SN​(x)∣≥e.
(38)
Hence the Fourier sine series approximation cannot converge uniformly even on the whole open interval, despite pointwise convergence at each interior point. The Fourier cosine series approximation has faster coefficient decay and avoids the persistent errors near the endpoints.

The space R3 is simply connected, and the electric field has zero curl. Hence it is a conservative vector field. More explicitly, Stokes theorem gives zero circulation around any closed loop, since such a loop bounds a spanning surface in R3. Therefore the line integral between two fixed endpoints is independent of the chosen path.

The electric potential is determined up to an additive constant; the later condition at infinity fixes that constant for the finite charge distribution in part (b).

By the assumed spherical symmetry of the electric potential, the electric field is radial: E=Er​(r)er​ for r>0. On a sphere of radius r, Gauss's law therefore reads
4πr2Er​(r)=ϵ0​Q(r)​,Q(r)=4π∫0r​ρ(s)s2ds.
(71)
Put R=a(1+δ). For r<a, the enclosed electric charge is 4πρ0​r3/3. In the spherical shell, the factor s2 in the volume element cancels s−2 in the charge density, so
Q(r)​=34πρ0​a3​+δ4πρ0​a2​∫ar​h′(s/a)ds=4πρ0​a3[31​+δh(r/a)−h(1)​].​
