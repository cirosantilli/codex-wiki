For a finite set S={s1​,…,sk​} of vectors in a real vector space, its span is the set of all linear combinations:
Sp(S)={i=1∑k​ai​si​:ai​∈R}.​
(1)
The span of the empty set is {0}. The set S is linearly independent if ∑i​ai​si​=0 implies ai​=0 for every i. It is a spanning set for V if Sp(S)=V. A basis is both a linearly independent set and a spanning set.
For basis extension, start with the given linearly independent set. If it does not span V, choose v∈/Sp(S) and adjoin it. The enlarged set remains linearly independent: a relation av+∑i​ai​si​=0 with a=0 would express v as a linear combination of S, a contradiction; if a=0, independence of S forces all other coefficients to be zero.
Repeat whenever the current span is proper. Each step increases the set's size by one, while the allowed bound says its size cannot exceed dimV. Thus the process terminates with a linearly independent set spanning V, hence a basis containing the original set. This also covers an initially empty set and the zero-dimensional vector space.

Choose a basis u1​,…,ur​ of U, extend it to a basis u1​,…,ur​,w1​,…,ws​ of W, and extend again to a basis of V by adding v1​,…,vt​. Define
W′=span{u1​,…,ur​,v1​,…,vt​}.​
(2)
Every member of the full basis lies in W or W′, so the sum of vector subspaces is W+W′=V. If x∈W∩W′, express it in both displayed bases. Uniqueness of the coordinates in the full basis forces its wj​ and vj​ coefficients to vanish. Thus x∈U. The reverse inclusion is built into the construction, proving W∩W′=U. This is a subspace supplement with a prescribed intersection.
For the matrix example, let
J=(0−1​10​),I=(10​01​).
(3)
The skew-symmetric matrices are exactly U=span{J}, and the traceless matrices form W. A suitable answer is
W′=span{I,J}={(a−b​ba​):a,b∈R}.​
(4)
The matrix trace of aI+bJ is 2a, so W∩W′=span{J}=U. Every 2×2 real matrix has the decomposition
A=(A−2trA​I)+2trA​I,
(5)
whose first term lies in W and second in W′. Therefore W+W′=M2​(R), as required.

For a linear map T:V→V, its kernel of a linear map and image of a linear map are
kerT={v∈V:T(v)=0},imT={T(v):v∈V}.
(6)
Its nullity of a linear map is n(T)=dimkerT, and its rank of a linear map is r(T)=dimimT.
Choose a basis u1​,…,uk​ of kerT and extend it to a basis u1​,…,uk​,v1​,…,vm​ of V. The vectors T(v1​),…,T(vm​) span imT, because applying T to an arbitrary linear combination of the full basis eliminates the ui​ terms. They are also linearly independent: if ∑j​bj​T(vj​)=0, then ∑j​bj​vj​∈kerT, so ∑j​bj​vj​=∑i​ai​ui​ for some coefficients. Independence of the extended basis forces all ai​,bj​ to vanish.
Consequently r(T)=m, n(T)=k and dimV=k+m. This proves the rank-nullity theorem:
dimV=r(T)+n(T).​
(7)

Restrict S to the vector subspace W=imT. This restriction has kernel of a linear map W∩kerS and image of a linear map S(W)=im(ST). The rank-nullity theorem on W therefore gives
r(ST)=r(T)−dim(imT∩kerS).
(8)
Apply the rank-nullity theorem to T and ST on V to obtain the exact nullity of a composition identity:
n(ST)=n(T)+dim(imT∩kerS).
(9)
Since the intersection is a vector subspace of kerS, its dimension is at most n(S). Hence
n(ST)≤n(T)+n(S).​
(10)

The image of a linear map im(ST)=S(imT) lies inside imS. Therefore r(ST)≤r(S). Also S restricted to imT cannot have rank of a linear map greater than its domain's dimension, so r(ST)≤dimimT=r(T). Thus
r(ST)≤min{r(S),r(T)}.​
(11)
Equivalently, apply the rank-nullity theorem to that restriction: r(ST)=r(T)−dim(imT∩kerS)≤r(T). This proof keeps track of the intermediate vector subspace, rather than assuming an invertible factor.

An elementary row operation is one of three operations: interchange two rows, multiply one row by a nonzero scalar, or add a scalar multiple of one row to a different row. Each operation is reversed by another operation of the same kind.
Performing such an operation on an m×n matrix A is equivalent to left multiplication by the m×m elementary matrix obtained by applying that operation to the identity matrix. Each elementary matrix is invertible, with inverse corresponding to the reverse operation. Thus a sequence of operations gives
B=Ek​Ek−1​⋯E1​A,B=PA,P=Ek​⋯E1​ invertible.​
(12)
For square matrices, if A were invertible, then B=PA would have inverse A−1P−1. Hence if B is singular, A must be singular as well. In fact the two are invertible together, because A=P−1B gives the converse.

A real number λ is an eigenvalue of A if there is a nonzero vector v with Av=λv; such a v is an eigenvector for λ. The nonzero condition is essential.
For distinct eigenvalues λ1​,…,λr​, choose corresponding nonzero eigenvectors v1​,…,vr​. Prove their linear independence by induction on r. The claim for one vector is immediate. Given a relation ∑i=1r​ai​vi​=0, apply A−λr​I to get
∑i=1r−1​ai​(λi​−λr​)vi​=0.
(16)
By the induction hypothesis, every coefficient here is zero. Distinctness gives ai​=0 for i<r, and the original relation then gives ar​=0 since vr​=0. Thus the eigenvectors are linearly independent.
With n distinct real eigenvalues, the n real eigenvectors form a basis of Rn. Put them into the columns of P. Then P is invertible and
AP=Pdiag(λ1​,…,λn​),P−1AP=diag(λ1​,…,λn​).​
(17)
This proves diagonalizability directly.

Suppose AT=A. For eigenvectors vi​,vj​ with distinct real eigenvalues, symmetry gives
λi​viT​vj​=(Avi​)Tvj​=viT​ATvj​=viT​Avj​=λj​viT​vj​.
(18)
Thus (λi​−λj​)viT​vj​=0, and the eigenvectors are orthogonal. Divide each by its nonzero Euclidean norm to obtain an orthonormal basis. The matrix P having these normalized columns satisfies PTP=I, so it is an orthogonal matrix and P−1=PT. As in part (a),
PTAP=diag(λ1​,…,λn​).​
(19)
This proves the needed orthogonal diagonalization from symmetry and distinct eigenvalues, without invoking an unproved spectral theorem.

Among the ∣G∣+1 powers e,g,g2,…,g∣G∣ in the finite group G, the pigeonhole principle forces two to be equal: gi=gj with 0≤i<j≤∣G∣. Multiply by g−i to obtain
gj−i=e,j−i>0.​
(22)
This proves the existence of a positive power equal to the identity element, without presupposing the order of a group element.

The order of a group element o(g) is the least positive integer n for which gn=e, whose existence follows from part (i). If gk=e, use the Euclidean division to write k=qn+r, where 0≤r<n. Then
e=gk=(gn)qgr=gr.
(23)
Minimality of n forces r=0. Thus
o(g)∣k.​
(24)
The argument also works for negative k, using integer powers and the same remainder convention.

Lagrange's theorem states that for a subgroup H of a finite group G,
∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣,
(25)
where [G:H] is the index of a subgroup, the number of its left cosets. In particular, ∣H∣ divides ∣G∣.
The cyclic subgroup ⟨g⟩ consists of the distinct elements e,g,…,gn−1, where n=o(g). Every power reduces to one of these by the Euclidean division, and a repetition among them would contradict minimality of n. Thus ∣⟨g⟩∣=o(g). Applying Lagrange's theorem gives
o(g)∣∣G∣.​
(26)

i OurBigBook.com
Let n=o(g). A group homomorphism preserves powers and the identity element, so
ϕ(g)n=ϕ(gn)=ϕ(e)=eH​.
(27)
The preceding divisibility property gives
o(ϕ(g))∣o(g).​
(28)
Now assume ϕ is surjective and choose h∈H of order m. Take a preimage g of h, and write o(g)=mr, as the divisibility result permits. The element gr has order exactly m: its mth power is e, while (gr)s=e implies mr∣rs, hence m∣s. Therefore G contains an element of order m, namely gr.
The preimage g itself need not have order m; taking the suitable power is the essential step. The constructed element need not map to the original h. This proves that element orders in a finite quotient occur in the original group.

A group homomorphism from an additive cyclic group Zn​ is determined by the image a of 1, subject exactly to an=e in multiplicative notation, or na=0 in an additive target.
For Z18​→Z15​, the relation is 18a≡0(mod15), equivalently 3a≡0(mod15). The possibilities are a=0,5,10, and each defines a homomorphism between cyclic groups. Thus there are three such maps, an instance of the number of homomorphisms between finite cyclic groups.
For Z9​→S4​, the image of 1 must be a permutation whose order of a group element divides 9. A permutation on four symbols can have order 1,2,3 or 4, so the allowed images are the identity and the three-cycles. There are (34​)(3−1)!=8 three-cycles: choose their three symbols and one of the two cyclic orientations. Hence there are nine maps.
Finally, each transposition in S5​ has order 2, so its image in Z5​ must have order dividing both 2 and 5, and is therefore the identity. Every permutation is a product of transpositions, so every element must map to the identity. There is one map. In the order asked, the counts are
3,9,1.​
(29)

A subgroup N of G is a normal subgroup if conjugation by every element preserves it:
gNg−1=Nfor every g∈G.​
(30)
Equivalently, gN=Ng for every g∈G. Here equality means equality of sets; it does not require each individual element of N to commute with every element of G.

ii OurBigBook.com
The center of a group consists of its elements commuting with every element. First check it is a subgroup. The identity element is central. If a,b∈Z(G) and x∈G, then b−1 commutes with x and
(ab−1)x=a(xb−1)=x(ab−1).
(31)
Thus ab−1∈Z(G), proving closure under the subgroup criterion. Moreover, if z∈Z(G), then xzx−1=z for every x∈G. Hence conjugation fixes the center elementwise, so
xZ(G)x−1=Z(G)(x∈G).​
(32)
Therefore the center of a group is a normal subgroup.

If g∈Z(G), it commutes with each x∈G, so x−1gx=g. Its conjugacy class is therefore C(g)={g}.
Conversely, if C(g)={g}, then x−1gx=g for every x. Multiplication by x on the left gives gx=xg for every x, which is exactly membership in the center of a group. Thus
g∈Z(G)⟺C(g)={g}.​
(33)

Conjugation of a permutation relabels each symbol by the inverse conjugating permutation. Specifically,
τ−1(a1​a2​⋯an​)τ=(τ−1(a1​) τ−1(a2​) ⋯ τ−1(an​)).​
(34)
To verify the direction of the inverse, apply the left side to τ−1(aj​): τ first takes it to aj​, the original cycle takes it to aj+1​, and τ−1 takes it to τ−1(aj+1​), with cyclic indexing. Any symbols fixed by the original permutation are similarly relabelled and remain fixed. More generally, conjugate every cycle of a disjoint-cycle decomposition in this way; disjointness is preserved because τ−1 is a bijection.

A left group action of G on a set S is a function ϕ:G×S→S satisfying
ϕ(e,s)=s,ϕ(gh,s)=ϕ(g,ϕ(h,s))​
(39)
for all s∈S and g,h∈G. Writing g⋅s=ϕ(g,s), these are e⋅s=s and (gh)⋅s=g⋅(h⋅s). No commutativity is assumed, so the order of composition matters.

The group action law gives
ϕg​∘ϕg−1​=ϕe​=idS​,ϕg−1​∘ϕg​=idS​.
(40)
Thus ϕg​ is a bijection with inverse ϕg−1​, hence a permutation of S. Also, for every s,
ϕgh​(s)=ϕ(g,ϕ(h,s))=(ϕg​∘ϕh​)(s).
(41)
Therefore the assignment Φ(g)=ϕg​ takes values in the symmetric group Sym(S) and satisfies
Φ(gh)=Φ(g)Φ(h).​
(42)
It is a group homomorphism, with the group product on the right interpreted as composition of permutations.

Define g⋅(g1​H)=(gg1​)H on the set of left cosets. It is well-defined: if g1​H=g2​H, then g2​=g1​h for some h∈H, so (gg2​)H=(gg1​h)H=(gg1​)H. The identity fixes each coset, and associativity in G gives
g⋅(h⋅(g1​H))=(ghg1​)H=(gh)⋅(g1​H).
(43)
Hence this is a left group action, and part (a) supplies its group homomorphism Φ:G→Sym(S).
An element of the kernel of a group homomorphism Φ fixes every coset, in particular H itself. Thus g∈kerΦ implies gH=H, which implies g∈H. Therefore
kerΦ≤H.​
(44)
The subgroup symbol is justified because a kernel of a group homomorphism is a subgroup, and the argument proves containment.

By Lagrange's theorem, the index of a subgroup is [G:H]=75/25=3. Thus the set of left cosets has three elements, and the preceding group action yields a group homomorphism Φ:G→S3​ after labelling them.
Its image is a subgroup of S3​, so its order divides 6. By the first isomorphism theorem, ∣imΦ∣=∣G∣/∣kerΦ∣ also divides 75. Hence its order divides gcd(6,75)=3, giving possibilities 1 and 3. The action on cosets is transitive: g2​g1−1​ sends g1​H to g2​H. It cannot be the trivial action on a three-element set, so the image does not have order 1. Therefore
∣imΦ∣=3.​
(45)
It follows that ∣kerΦ∣=75/3=25. Part (b)(i) gives kerΦ≤H, and ∣H∣=25; equal finite orders force equality. A kernel of a group homomorphism is a normal subgroup, so
H=kerΦ⊴G.​
(46)
This is an instance of the fact that a subgroup of index the smallest prime divisor is normal.
