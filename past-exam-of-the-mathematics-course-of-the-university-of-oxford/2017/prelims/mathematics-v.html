Pair each point x of the square's boundary with −x. The vector field obeys f(−x)=f(x), whereas the outward unit normal vector changes sign: n(−x)=−n(x). Corresponding arc length elements are equal. Thus the two contributions to the flux cancel, proving without evaluating any boundary integral that
∫C​f⋅nds=0.​
(7)
The same cancellation appears in the divergence, which is an odd function of x over a region symmetric under x↦−x. To justify using the planar divergence theorem despite the lack of differentiability at the origin, remove a disk of radius ε<1. The vector field is continuously differentiable on the remaining region, its divergence has zero double integral by this symmetry, and the magnitude of the inner flux is at most 2πε2, since ∥f∥≤ε there. Letting ε→0 recovers the result. This is flux around a removable point singularity.

The specific heat capacity c is the energy needed per unit mass per unit increase in temperature. With c constant, a small element of volume dV has mass ρdV and thermal energy ρcTdV, measured relative to the reference at which T=0. Summing over Ω gives

Suppose T(1) and T(2) are two sufficiently regular steady state solutions with the same prescribed temperature on the boundary. Their difference w=T(1)−T(2) satisfies
∇⋅(k∇w)=0in R,w=0on ∂R.
(13)
The identical PDE source terms cancel. Apply the product rule and the divergence theorem to wk∇w; this is the weighted form of Green's first identity:
0​=∭R​w∇⋅(k∇w)dV=∬∂R​wk∇w⋅ndA−∭R​k∥∇w∥2dV=−∭R​k∥∇w∥2dV.​
(14)
Since k>0, the nonnegative integrand must vanish, so ∇w=0. Hence w is constant on each connected component of R, and its zero Dirichlet boundary condition makes each constant zero. Therefore T(1)=T(2)​: the solution, if it exists, is unique. This proves Dirichlet uniqueness for variable-conductivity heat conduction; it does not require a constant thermal conductivity.

For any closed surface enclosing a region V, Gauss's law for gravity states that the outward flux of the gravitational field is
∬∂V​g⋅ndA=−4πG∭V​ρdV=−4πGMV​.​
(17)
Here G is the gravitational constant and MV​ is the enclosed mass; the negative sign expresses attractive gravity.
Use the gravitational potential sign conventions specified by this paper: g=∇φ. Thus φ is the negative of the usual Newtonian gravitational potential Φ defined by g=−∇Φ. For example, setting the value at infinity to zero gives φ=GM/r for a point mass. The divergence theorem now gives
∭V​∇2φdV=−4πG∭V​ρdV.
(18)
Since V is arbitrary, continuity of the integrands implies the local Poisson's equation
∇2φ=−4πGρ.​
(19)
Conversely, integrating this Poisson's equation over any V and applying the divergence theorem recovers Gauss's law for gravity. At a discontinuity of the mass density, the local equation is interpreted piecewise, or globally in the sense of distributions.

Write u(x,t)=U(ξ,η), where ξ=x−t and η=x+t. The chain rule gives
∂x​=∂ξ​+∂η​,∂t​=−∂ξ​+∂η​.
(41)
For a twice continuously differentiable classical solution, mixed partial derivatives commute, so the wave equation becomes
0=utt​−uxx​=−4Uξη​.
(42)
Hence Uξ​ depends only on ξ. Integrating first in ξ yields the countertravelling decomposition of the one-dimensional wave equation:
u(x,t)=F(x−t)+G(x+t).​
(43)
On a rectangular domain in (ξ,η) this follows immediately; on the full line for t≥0, the connected characteristic slices give the same representation. The two terms travel at wave speed one in opposite directions.
Impose the initial conditions. At t=0,
F(x)+G(x)=f(x),−F′(x)+G′(x)=g(x).
(44)
Differentiate the first identity and combine:
F′(x)=2f′(x)−g(x)​,G′(x)=2f′(x)+g(x)​.
(45)
If H(x)=∫x∗​x​g(s)ds, then F=(f−H)/2+C and G=(f+H)/2−C. Substitution cancels C and the arbitrary base point x∗​, giving D'Alembert's formula:
u(x,t)=2f(x−t)+f(x+t)​+21​∫x−tx+t​g(s)ds.​
(46)
For example, f∈C2 and g∈C1 make this a classical solution of the wave equation satisfying both initial conditions. The same formula defines a weak solution for less regular data, including the continuous piecewise smooth data of part (b).

Let A be the constant cross-sectional area, and suppose that the temperature is uniform on each cross-section. Consider a slice [a,b]. Its stored thermal energy is ρcA∫ab​T(x,t)dx. Since the lateral surface is insulated and there is no internal heat generation, conservation of energy gives
ρcAdtd​∫ab​T(x,t)dx=Aq(a,t)−Aq(b,t),
(54)
where q is the heat flux in the positive x direction. By Fourier's law, q=−kTx​, so
ρc∫ab​Tt​dx=kTx​(b,t)−kTx​(a,t)=k∫ab​Txx​dx.
(55)
The slice is arbitrary; therefore the local heat equation is
Tt​=κTxx​,κ=ρck​>0.​
(56)
The constant κ is the thermal diffusivity. The cancellation of A uses a rod of uniform cross-section; the one-dimensional model assumes no transverse temperature variation.
