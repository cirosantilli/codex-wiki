Subtracting the first row from the other two in the determinant gives
f(a,b,c)=det(b1​−a1​c1​−a1​​b2​−a2​c2​−a2​​).
(4)
This vanishes exactly when the two displacements b−a and c−a are linearly dependent. If a=b, that means c−a=t(b−a) for some real number t. Thus all three points lie on the line
x=a+s(b−a),s∈R.
(5)
Conversely, putting three points on any one line makes their displacements linearly dependent, so the determinant vanishes.
The equivalence remains true without distinctness. If exactly two points coincide, the two distinct locations determine a line containing all three, and two rows of the original determinant coincide. If all three points coincide, any line through that point works and the determinant is again zero. Existence persists, although uniqueness of the line can fail.

The required lines are the x-axis and every line through the origin in the plane x=0. Explicitly,
L={(t,0,0):t∈R}orL={(0,tcosϕ,tsinϕ):t∈R},0≤ϕ<π.​
(25)
The x-axis meets the ellipsoid at its two poles and is a normal line at each. A line of the second kind meets it at the antipodal points (0,±cosϕ,±sinϕ), whose normal vectors are parallel to that line.
To prove completeness, suppose a common normal line passes through distinct points P=r(θ,ϕ) and Q=r(θ′,ϕ′). If either is a pole, b forces the line to be the x-axis. Otherwise b says that the line meets the x-axis at (−cosθ/2​,0,0) and at (−cosθ′/2​,0,0). A line other than the x-axis has at most one such intersection, so cosθ=cosθ′.
The points P,Q consequently have equal x-coordinates. Since they are distinct, their joining line has zero x-component in its direction. Its normal vector at P therefore has 2​cosθ=0. Hence P,Q lie in x=0, and the normal line there passes through the origin. This gives exactly the listed family.

The euclidean algorithm computes the greatest common divisor of two integers by repeated division with remainder. Start with r0​=∣A∣, r1​=∣B∣, interchanging them if necessary, and repeatedly write
rj−1​=qj​rj​+rj+1​,0≤rj+1​<rj​,
(55)
until a zero remainder occurs. The positive remainders strictly decrease, so the process terminates. At each step the common divisors of rj−1​,rj​ are exactly the common divisors of rj​,rj+1​, because rj+1​=rj−1​−qj​rj​. Hence
gcd(A,B)=the last nonzero remainder.​
(56)
If one input is zero, the answer is the absolute value of the other; the usual convention sets gcd(0,0)=0. Back-substitution also expresses the greatest common divisor as an integer linear combination of the inputs, as in Bézout's identity.
