The Steinitz exchange lemma states that if u1​,…,ur​ are linearly independent and w1​,…,ws​ span a vector space V, then r≤s and some r of the wj​ can be replaced by u1​,…,ur​ while preserving the spanning set property.
Here is the exchange argument. Suppose that after k replacements, a spanning set is
u1​,…,uk​,wk+1​,…,ws​,
(2)
where the remaining wj​ have been relabelled if necessary. Express uk+1​ as a linear combination of this family. At least one remaining wj​ has a nonzero coefficient; otherwise uk+1​ belongs to the span of u1​,…,uk​, contradicting linear independence. Relabel this entry wk+1​. Solving for wk+1​ shows that it lies in the span of
u1​,…,uk+1​,wk+2​,…,ws​.
(3)
The new family therefore still spans V. Starting at k=0 repeats this step until all r independent vectors have been inserted. If r>s, after s steps the next us+1​ would lie in the span of u1​,…,us​, a contradiction. Thus r≤s, and the construction proves the replacement assertion as well.

A transposition in the symmetric group Sn​ exchanges two distinct symbols and fixes all others. A permutation cycle (a1​ a2​ ⋯ ak​) sends aj​ to aj+1​, sends ak​ to a1​, and fixes the other symbols. Its entries are distinct. For k=1 this is the identity permutation.
A group element has order of a group element k if k is the least positive integer for which its kth power is the identity element. For the displayed permutation cycle, its jth power sends ai​ forward j places, with subscripts read modulo k. Hence the kth power fixes every symbol, whereas for 0<j<k the image of a1​ is different from a1​. Thus its order of a group element is exactly k, including k=1.

For each symbol a, iterate the permutation σ. Since the symbol set is finite, some iterate repeats. Applying an inverse power of σ shows that the first return is to a itself, giving
a,σ(a),…,σk−1(a),σk(a)=a,
(42)
with the displayed symbols distinct. This is a permutation cycle. If two such lists share a symbol, applying powers of σ shows that they contain exactly the same symbols. Hence these group orbits form a partition of a set {1,…,n}. On each group orbit, σ acts as the corresponding permutation cycle, and these disjoint permutation cycles multiply to σ.
The disjoint cycle decomposition is unique up to reordering the cycles and cyclically rotating the entries within each cycle, with fixed points optionally omitted. Indeed, each cycle must consist of one of the uniquely determined group orbits, and its successive entries must follow the action of σ.

By Lagrange's theorem, every nonidentity group element has order 5, 7, or 35. If some element has order 35, its seventh and fifth powers have orders 5 and 7, respectively, and we are done.
Otherwise every nonidentity element has order 5 or 7. Distinct subgroups of a given prime number order intersect only in the identity: a shared nonidentity element generates both. If there were no element of order 5, the 34 nonidentity elements would therefore partition into sets of six, the nonidentity elements of the cyclic subgroups of order seven. This would force 6∣34, a contradiction. If there were no element of order 7, the analogous partition into sets of four would force 4∣34, again a contradiction. Thus elements of both orders exist.

For any integer n≥1, let R=R2π/n​ in the stabilizer subgroup from i. Since Rθ​Rϕ​=Rθ+ϕ​ and Rθ​=I exactly when θ∈2πZ, this rotation matrix has order exactly n. Therefore
⟨R2π/n​⟩≅Cn​​

is a subgroup of G of order n, including the trivial case n=1.

J=diag(−1,1,−1)∈G.
(66)
Direct multiplication gives J2=I and JRJ=R−1. Every word in R,J therefore reduces to Rk or RkJ, 0≤k<n.
