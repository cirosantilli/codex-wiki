The scalar product, also called the dot product, and the vector product, also called the cross product, are
a⋅b=a1​b1​+a2​b2​+a3​b3​,a∧b=​a2​b3​−a3​b2​a3​b1​−a1​b3​a1​b2​−a2​b1​​​.​

a⋅b=∣a∣∣b∣cosϑ,∣a∧b∣=∣a∣∣b∣sinϑ.​
(2)
The cross product is orthogonal to both vectors, with its orientation determined by the right-hand rule. If either vector is zero, both products vanish, although the angle is undefined.

A real square matrix A is an orthogonal matrix when
ATA=I.​
(3)
Here AT is the transpose and I the identity matrix; equivalently, its columns form an orthonormal basis. If this holds, the squared Euclidean norm obeys
∣Av∣2=vTATAv=vTv=∣v∣2,
(4)
so the linear transformation preserves length.
Conversely, suppose ∣Av∣=∣v∣ for every vector v. Put B=ATA−I, a symmetric matrix. Then vTBv=0 for every vector. Taking v=ei​, the standard basis vectors, gives Bii​=0. Taking v=ei​+ej​ for i=j gives 2Bij​=0. Thus B=0, proving that the linear transformation preserves length if and only if A is an orthogonal matrix.

Choose the first orthonormal basis vector to be e1​=a. If b is not parallel to a, choose e2​ along b−(a⋅b)a; otherwise choose any unit vector orthogonal to a. Complete to an orthonormal basis with e3​. The matrix with rows eiT​ is an orthogonal matrix, and expressing the three unit vectors in this basis gives
a′=(1,0,0)T,b′=(x,y,0)T,c′=(z,wcosθ,wsinθ)T.
(5)
The last two components of c′ have polar coordinates of the displayed form; we may choose w≥0. If they vanish, θ is arbitrary.
An orthogonal transformation preserves dot products. Taking dot products with a′, and then using the unit Euclidean norms, gives
x=cosγ,z=cosβ,∣y∣=sinγ,∣w∣=sinβ.​
(6)
The remaining dot product gives
cosα=xz+ywcosθ.
(7)

∣(a∧b)⋅c∣2​=y2w2−(ywcosθ)2=(1−x2)(1−z2)−(cosα−xz)2=1−cos2α−cos2β−cos2γ+2cosαcosβcosγ.​

A nondegenerate hyperbola centred at the origin has a quadratic equation
Ax2+2Bxy+Cy2+F=0.
(16)
There are no linear terms because the origin is its centre. Its asymptotes are the zero set of the homogeneous quadratic form Ax2+2Bxy+Cy2. Requiring the directions y=x and y=−x gives A+2B+C=A−2B+C=0, hence B=0 and C=−A. Since the conic section is nondegenerate, A and F are nonzero. Thus all such hyperbolas with prescribed asymptotes are exactly
Hc​: x2−y2=c,c∈R∖{0}.​
(17)
For c>0 these rectangular hyperbolas open along the x-axis, and for c<0 along the y-axis. Conversely, each displayed hyperbola has the required centre and asymptotes. Two distinct members have different values of x2−y2, so they cannot intersect.
For the second pair of asymptotes, the same argument gives H2​:4x2−y2=d, d=0. The intersections of concentric hyperbolas therefore satisfy
x2=3d−c​,y2=3d−4c​.
(18)
If both right sides are positive, there are four independent sign choices. If precisely one is zero and the other is positive, there are two points. If either is negative there are none; both cannot vanish because c=0. Each possibility occurs: take (c,d)=(1,5) for four, (1,4) for two, and (1,1) for none. Hence
∣H1​∩H2​∣∈{0,2,4}.​
(19)

At a regular point of the parametrized surface, the partial derivatives rs​ and rt​ are linearly independent tangent vectors. Their cross product gives a nonzero normal vector:
n=rs​×rt​.​

Measure x1​,x2​ as horizontal displacements from the respective downward equilibrium positions; the absolute horizontal coordinates are x1​ and L+x2​. Each vertical displacement is of second order in its small angle. Thus the spring is horizontal to first order, its extension is x2​−x1​, and Hooke's law gives horizontal forces k(x2​−x1​) and −k(x2​−x1​). Combining these with the linearized simple pendulum restoring force yields
x¨1​=−ℓg​x1​+mk​(x2​−x1​),x¨2​=−ℓg​x2​−mk​(x2​−x1​).​
(34)
For the normal modes of two spring-coupled equal pendulums, add and subtract these ordinary differential equations. The normal mode coordinates q+​=x1​+x2​ and q−​=x1​−x2​ satisfy
q¨​+​+ℓg​q+​=0,q¨​−​+(ℓg​+m2k​)q−​=0.
(35)
Hence the two natural angular frequencies and displacement patterns are
ω+​=ℓg​​,(x1​,x2​)∝(1,1);ω−​=ℓg​+m2k​​,(x1​,x2​)∝(1,−1).​
(36)
Ordinary frequencies are ω+​/(2π) and ω−​/(2π). In the first normal mode, both particles swing together with equal amplitudes and the spring is unextended to first order. In the second normal mode, the particles swing in opposite directions with equal amplitudes, so the spring adds a restoring force and increases the angular frequency.

Use g as the gravitational acceleration, so the weights are mg and 2​mg, consistently with the requested equations. The printed introductory phrase calling g a force magnitude omits the factor m.
For the particle on a cone coupled to a hanging mass, write z=−r and use the cylindrical coordinates unit vectors er​,eθ​,ez​. The ideal string segment from the cone particle to the hole has length 2​r and direction (−er​+ez​)/2​. If h is the downward length of the hanging segment and s0​ the fixed total length, then
h=s0​−2​r=s0​+2​z,z2​=−h=−s0​−2​z.
(37)
The light ideal string and smooth hole give the same tension T on both sides. Applying the vertical component of Newton's second law to the hanging particle gives
2​mz¨2​=T−2​mg,T=2​mg−2mz¨.​
(38)
Write the normal reaction on the cone particle as N(er​+ez​)/2​, allowing N to be signed according to the side of the ideal constraining surface. The acceleration in cylindrical coordinates and Newton's second law give
m(r¨−rθ˙2)m(rθ¨+2r˙θ˙)mz¨​=2​N−T​,=0,=2​N+T​−mg.​
(39)
There is no azimuthal force. Multiplying the middle equation by r/m proves
dtd​(r2θ˙)=0,r2θ˙=av,​
(40)
where the positive angular direction is chosen along the initial velocity. This is conservation of angular momentum about the vertical axis.
Subtract the radial equation from the vertical equation to eliminate the normal reaction. Since r¨=−z¨, this gives
m(2z¨+rθ˙2)=2​T−mg.
(41)
Substitute the tension and the conserved angular momentum, using r=−z:
2(1+2​)z¨=g−rθ˙2=g+z3a2v2​.
(42)
Multiplication by z˙ now gives an exact time derivative:
dtd​((1+2​)z˙2+2z2a2v2​−gz)=0.
(43)
Initially z=−a and z˙=0, because the initial velocity is horizontal. Therefore
(1+2​)z˙2+2z2a2v2​−gz=2v2​+ga.​
(44)
This is also total mechanical energy divided by m, with an irrelevant constant removed: the two particles contribute (1+2​)mz˙2 to the radial and vertical kinetic energy, while their combined gravitational potential energy is −mgz plus a constant.

Start with [a0​,b0​]=[a,b]. At stage n, form the midpoint cn​=(an​+bn​)/2. If f(cn​)=0, stop; for a sequence defined for all later indices, repeat cn​ forever. Otherwise retain the half-interval with opposite endpoint signs: set [an+1​,bn+1​]=[an​,cn​] if f(an​)f(cn​)<0, and [an+1​,bn+1​]=[cn​,bn​] otherwise. This is the bisection method.
If it never stops, the closed intervals are nested, retain f(an​)f(bn​)<0, and have lengths
bn​−an​=2nb−a​⟶0.
(60)
By the nested interval theorem, there is a unique real ξ in their intersection. In particular, an​→ξ, bn​→ξ, and the sequence of midpoints satisfies
∣cn​−ξ∣≤2n+1b−a​⟶0.
(61)
Since f is continuous, f(an​)f(bn​)→f(ξ)2. All these products are negative, so their limit of a sequence is nonpositive. Therefore f(ξ)2≤0, forcing f(ξ)=0. We have proved that the sequence is a convergent sequence whose limit of a sequence is a zero of a function:
cn​⟶ξ,f(ξ)=0,∣cn​−ξ∣≤2n+1b−a​.​
(62)
The error bound applies at every stage before exact termination; after termination the error is zero. The intermediate value theorem also guarantees a zero of a function in each retained interval, but the nested-interval argument identifies the particular one to which the sequence of midpoints converges.

For an unconstrained differentiable function of two real variables, an interior local minimum must solve the simultaneous stationary point equations
fx​(x,y)=0,fy​(x,y)=0,or∇f(x,y)=0.
(63)
Apply Newton's method in optimization to this gradient equation. The Jacobian matrix of the gradient is the Hessian matrix
H(x,y)=(fxx​fyx​​fxy​fyy​​).
(64)
At pn​=(xn​,yn​)T, solve the linear system H(pn​)sn​=−∇f(pn​), then set pn+1​=pn​+sn​. When the Hessian matrix is invertible, this is
pn+1​=pn​−H(pn​)−1∇f(pn​).​
(65)
This follows by setting the first order Taylor expansion ∇f(pn​+sn​)≃∇f(pn​)+H(pn​)sn​ equal to zero. Solving the linear system avoids explicitly computing a matrix inverse. A stationary point need not be a local minimum: for example, a positive-definite matrix H(p) suffices to classify a stationary point as a strict local minimum, whereas an indefinite Hessian matrix identifies a saddle point of a scalar function.
