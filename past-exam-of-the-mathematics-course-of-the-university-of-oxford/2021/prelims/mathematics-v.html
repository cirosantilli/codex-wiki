If two solutions exist, their difference w satisfies ∇2w−w=0 inside B and w=0 on the boundary. Suppose w has a positive value. Continuity on the closed ball gives a positive maximum at an interior point, where the Hessian matrix is negative semidefinite and hence ∇2w≤0. But the equation gives ∇2w=w>0, a contradiction. A negative minimum similarly contradicts ∇2w=w<0.
Thus w=0​, proving uniqueness by the maximum principle for the modified Helmholtz equation. Here a classical solution is understood to be C2 inside and continuous up to the boundary; mere first differentiability would not define the displayed Laplacian classically. With enough boundary regularity, the alternative Green's first identity proof is 0=∫B​w(∇2w−w)=−∫B​(∣∇w∣2+w2).

The graph repeats every two units: it rises linearly from the right-hand limit −1 at an odd integer to value one at the next even integer, then falls linearly to value zero at the next odd integer. The defined value at each odd integer is zero, while its right-hand limit is minus one.
The Dirichlet convergence theorem for Fourier series gives the midpoint of the two one-sided limits at a jump. Hence, on the requested interval, the Fourier sum differs from f precisely at
x∈{−3,−1,1,3},Fourier sum=−21​=f(x)=0.​
(21)

For uniqueness, subtract two solutions and call their difference w. It has zero boundary and initial data, with wt​=wxx​+awx​+bw. Its squared L2 norm obeys the energy estimate
21​dtd​∫−11​w2dx=−∫−11​wx2​dx+2a​[w2]−11​+∫−11​bw2dx≤0.
(33)
The boundary term vanishes and b≤0. A nonnegative quantity starting at zero must stay zero, proving uniqueness in the usual classical or corresponding energy class.
The printed evenness claim for a=0 is false for an arbitrary coefficient b(x,t)≤0. Take T0​>0 and b(x,t)=−2−x. The strong maximum principle makes the solution positive in the interior for t>0. If it were even, Tt​ and Txx​ would both be even. Subtracting the equation at x and −x would give 0=[b(x,t)−b(−x,t)]T(x,t)=−2xT(x,t), impossible for x=0.
With the additional assumption b(−x,t)=b(x,t), the intended argument is valid. For a=0, reflection T(x,t)=T(−x,t) solves the same equation with the same initial and boundary data. Uniqueness then proves T(−x,t)=T(x,t)​. If T0​=0, initial and boundary values disagree at the two time-zero corners; the initial condition is understood in the interior or in the L2 norm limit.
