Set u=X(x)Y(y) in Laplace's equation. Separation of variables gives
X′′+λX=0,Y′′−λY=0,X′(0)=X′(π)=0.

The Sturm-Liouville problem in x has the constant mode X0​=1 with λ0​=0, and the eigenfunctions Xn​=cos(nx) with λn​=n2, n≥1. Indeed, integration by parts makes λ nonnegative; for λ>0 the first Neumann boundary condition removes the sine term and the second requires λ​ to be an integer. For λ=0, both derivative conditions make X constant.

Since the bottom value is zero, a convenient factor depending on y for the nonconstant modes is sinh(n(y+1)), which vanishes at y=−1. Its value at y=1 is sinh(2n). The zero mode is linear in y, so matching both constant boundary values gives

Thus
u(x,y)=3π2​(1+y)+n=1∑∞​n2sinhn2(−1)n+1​(sinh(ny)+tanh(n)cosh(ny))cos(nx)​.
(69)
Each product of hyperbolic sine or hyperbolic cosine with cos(nx) has uxx​+uyy​=0. The zero mode is also a harmonic function. On −1≤y≤1, the factor sinh(n(y+1))/sinh(2n) lies between zero and one. Since ∑n−2 converges, the Weierstrass M-test gives uniform convergence on the closed rectangle. The series therefore gives zero at the bottom and the continuous Fourier cosine series of π2−x2 at the top, including its endpoints. On compact subsets with y<1, the factors decay exponentially with n, justifying termwise partial derivatives. In particular ux​=0 on both open vertical edges because every differentiated cosine factor vanishes there. The solution has finite Dirichlet energy: Fourier orthogonality gives the nth nonconstant mode's energy as (π/2)ngn2​coth(2n)=O(n−3), whose sum is finite. Here coth is the hyperbolic cotangent. Thus a proves uniqueness in that class.
There is a corner compatibility condition for mixed boundary data in the printed wording. The top data have g′(π)=−2π, but the paper writes ux​(π,y)=0 even at y=1. The series solves the interior equation and all the open-edge boundary conditions; the literal derivative condition at (π,1) is incompatible with the top data. A function differentiable in x along the top edge would necessarily have ux​(π,1)=g′(π)=−2π. Hence it cannot simultaneously satisfy the printed zero derivative at that corner, and there is no solution with all those literal corner requirements. The standard interpretation of this mixed boundary condition imposes the Neumann boundary condition on the open sides and the Dirichlet boundary condition continuously on the horizontal edges. Differentiating the series term by term on the top edge would be invalid at this corner; the uniform convergence of the undifferentiated series does not license that operation.
