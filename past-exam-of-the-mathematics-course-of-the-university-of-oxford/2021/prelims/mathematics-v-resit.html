One sufficient version of the divergence theorem is the following. Let R be a bounded region in R3 with a closed, piecewise smooth surface as its boundary, and let F be a continuously differentiable function with values in R3 on an open neighbourhood of the closure of R. With n the outward unit normal vector,
∭R​∇⋅FdV=∬∂R​F⋅ndS​.
(9)
The boundary surface integral is the total outward flux integral. If the boundary has several components, each has the outward orientation relative to the material of R; inner components therefore point into the holes. Edges and isolated vertices may be omitted when assigning the unit normal vector, since they have zero surface area.

The theorem on pointwise convergence of a piecewise smooth Fourier series gives the average of the two one-sided values of the corresponding periodic extension. Under the usual piecewise smooth interpretation of the paper's derivative assumption, the even extension is continuous at 0 and at the joined endpoints ±π. Thus
Cf​(x)=f(x)(0≤x≤π)​.
(33)
For the odd extension, the values on either side of zero are −f(0) and f(0); those on either side of π are f(π) and −f(π). Therefore
Sf​(x)={f(x),0,​0<x<π,x=0 or x=π.​​

The truncated Fourier cosine series gives the better approximation on the closed interval, in the senses of uniform convergence and the asymptotic L2 norm error. To make the comparison precise, let CN​ and SN​ retain the frequencies 1≤n≤N, with the constant term also included in CN​.
The cosine Fourier coefficients are O(n−2). In fact, absolute convergence gives the uniform tail bound
sup0≤x≤π​∣f(x)−CN​(x)∣≤π4​∑n>N​n21​≤πN4​⟶0.
(42)
For the Fourier sine series, every Fourier partial sum satisfies SN​(0)=SN​(π)=0. Thus
0≤x≤πsup​∣f(x)−SN​(x)∣≥1+π​,
(43)
so there is no uniform convergence to f on the closed interval. Its odd extension has jump discontinuities, producing the Gibbs phenomenon near the endpoints; its Fourier coefficients decrease only like 1/n.
The advantage persists in the L2 norm, which ignores individual endpoint values. By Parseval's identity and Fourier orthogonality,
∥f−CN​∥22​=2π​∑n>N​an2​=O(N−3),∥f−SN​∥22​=2π​∑n>N​bn2​=O(N−1).
(44)
The nonzero odd cosine coefficients and both parity classes of sine coefficients also give matching lower bounds of these orders by comparison with the corresponding p-series. Hence their L2 norm errors decrease respectively like N−3/2 and N−1/2. This is a comparison of approximation quality in specified norms, not a claim that one finite sum has smaller error at every individual interior point.

To obtain exactly the paper's form, use the hyperbolic-function identity
sinh(2n)sinh(n(y+1))​=2sinhncoshnsinh(ny)coshn+cosh(ny)sinhn​=2sinhnsinh(ny)+tanhncosh(ny)​.
(68)
Thus
u(x,y)=3π2​(1+y)+n=1∑∞​n2sinhn2(−1)n+1​(sinh(ny)+tanh(n)cosh(ny))cos(nx)​.
(69)
Each product of hyperbolic sine or hyperbolic cosine with cos(nx) has uxx​+uyy​=0. The zero mode is also a harmonic function. On −1≤y≤1, the factor sinh(n(y+1))/sinh(2n) lies between zero and one. Since ∑n−2 converges, the Weierstrass M-test gives uniform convergence on the closed rectangle. The series therefore gives zero at the bottom and the continuous Fourier cosine series of π2−x2 at the top, including its endpoints. On compact subsets with y<1, the factors decay exponentially with n, justifying termwise partial derivatives. In particular ux​=0 on both open vertical edges because every differentiated cosine factor vanishes there. The solution has finite Dirichlet energy: Fourier orthogonality gives the nth nonconstant mode's energy as (π/2)ngn2​coth(2n)=O(n−3), whose sum is finite. Here coth is the hyperbolic cotangent. Thus a proves uniqueness in that class.
There is a corner compatibility condition for mixed boundary data in the printed wording. The top data have g′(π)=−2π, but the paper writes ux​(π,y)=0 even at y=1. The series solves the interior equation and all the open-edge boundary conditions; the literal derivative condition at (π,1) is incompatible with the top data. A function differentiable in x along the top edge would necessarily have ux​(π,1)=g′(π)=−2π. Hence it cannot simultaneously satisfy the printed zero derivative at that corner, and there is no solution with all those literal corner requirements. The standard interpretation of this mixed boundary condition imposes the Neumann boundary condition on the open sides and the Dirichlet boundary condition continuously on the horizontal edges. Differentiating the series term by term on the top edge would be invalid at this corner; the uniform convergence of the undifferentiated series does not license that operation.
