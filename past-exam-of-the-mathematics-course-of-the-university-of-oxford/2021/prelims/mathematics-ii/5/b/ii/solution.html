We give the explicit point x=1/4​. Repeatedly choose the left remaining third and then the right remaining third. After 2m choices, the resulting closed interval is [Lm​,Rm​], where
Lm​=∑j=1m​32j2​=41​(1−9−m),Rm​=Lm​+9−m=41​+43​9−m.
(41)
These formulas follow from a finite geometric series. They show Lm​<1/4<Rm​, and that both endpoints tend to 1/4. The point 1/4 is also strictly inside each intermediate left third: its distance from Lm​ is one quarter of the current length. It therefore never enters any assigned closed middle third or its endpoints, so 1/4∈C.
Endpoint values in the Cantor function construction obey the same left/right choices, with halving in place of thirding. Thus
f(Lm​)=∑j=1m​22j1​=31​(1−4−m),f(Rm​)=f(Lm​)+4−m=31​+32​4−m.
(42)
All these endpoints are in the domain of the partial function: Lm​ is the right endpoint of a middle third assigned at round 2m, and Rm​ is the left endpoint of a middle third assigned at round 2m−1.
Given ε>0, choose m so that (2/3)4−m<ε, and put δ=9−m/4. If t∈/C and ∣t−1/4∣<δ, then Lm​<t<Rm​. The assumed monotonicity gives
31​−31​4−m≤f(Lm​)≤f(t)≤f(Rm​)≤31​+32​4−m.
(43)
Consequently ∣f(t)−1/3∣<ε. This proves directly, on the stated partial domain,
t→1/4t∈/C​lim​f(t)=31​.​
(44)
There are defined points arbitrarily close to 1/4, for example Lm​ and Rm​, so the limit of a function is not vacuous. No continuous extension has been assumed. The residual C here omits the endpoints that the question assigns values to; it is the usual Cantor set with those assigned endpoints removed.
