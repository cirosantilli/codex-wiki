Buffer capacity measurement

A proposed modern measurement extends the weak acid dissociation measurement by estimating the local slope of an acid-base titration. Transfer a measured sample with a pipette into a stirred vessel; clamp a combination glass electrode/reference electrode clear of the stirring magnet and place a temperature probe nearby. A calibrated burette delivers a chemical base of independently standardized concentration cT​, such as sodium hydroxide. Record the initial sample volume V0​, cumulative delivered volume v, stable pH, and temperature after each small addition. Use a separate fresh portion for an acidward series. These are proposed measurement conditions, not reported experimental results.
For the instrument calibration, use fresh standard buffer solutions bracketing the measured pH range, with values appropriate to the actual temperature. The Hanna manual specifies separate rinse and calibration portions, gentle stirring, and approximately 4cm immersion of the probe tip. It calls for stable readings; the sensing bulb and reference electrode junction must remain immersed. Rinse between standards and samples and avoid carrying rinse water into the measured portion.[ref]
The readout pairs each added amount of substance with the measured pH. With negligible added volume, neighboring readings give the finite difference estimate

assigned to the interval's midpoint. High buffer capacity appears as a small change of pH for a measured reagent dose. Choose increments small enough to resolve changing buffer capacity, but large enough that the change of pH exceeds measurement noise. Repeat with a fresh portion and different increment sizes; remeasure a standard afterward to check drift. A water blank can reveal contamination, but its pH must not be subtracted from the sample pH. These controls are proposed tests of the measurement and model assumptions.
If additions appreciably increase volume, even the analytical concentration changes: C(v)=C0​V0​/(V0​+v). Merely dividing the added moles by the current volume does not restore the constant-volume derivative. One alternative is explicitly to report a changing-volume convention. To compare with the constant-volume theory, fit the changing-volume chemical equilibrium model instead of treating the observed slope as β. In the same ideal monoprotic acid system, define S0​=[A−]0​+[OH−]0​−[H+]0​. For baseward additions, the electric charge balance at each reading is
V0​+vS0​V0​+cT​v​=Ka​+HC(v)Ka​​+c∘(HKw​​−H).

Use a minus sign before cT​v for the corresponding acidward series. From the model parameters, evaluate the constant-volume buffer capacity at the chosen analytical concentration and pH. The fit also tests whether one acid dissociation constant describes the observations.
Limitations include incomplete mixing, slow glass electrode response, uncertain reagent concentration, and changes in temperature or ionic strength. Dissolved atmospheric carbon dioxide introduces an additional chemical acid system; comparable exposure and freshly prepared samples help distinguish it from the intended monoprotic acid response. Additional reacting chemical species and nonideal activity coefficients invalidate the simple formula. Especially when pH increments are tiny, measurement noise can dominate the inferred derivative; reporting the dose, pH interval, volume convention, and repeatability is part of reporting a meaningful buffer capacity.
