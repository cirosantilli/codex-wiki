Derivation of Wien's displacement law (xλ​=5(1−e−xλ​))

 ... Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Radiative transfer Planck function Wien's displacement law
OurBigBook.com Words: 171
At fixed T>0, substitute x=hc/(λkB​T)>0 into the Planck function:
Bλ​(T)=h4c32kB5​T5​ex−1x5​.
(29)
Here h is the Planck constant, kB​ the Boltzmann constant, and c the speed of light. Since dx/dλ=−x/λ=0, an interior maximum in wavelength is also a stationary point in x. A logarithmic derivative gives
dxd​logex−1x5​=x5​−1−e−x1​=0,x=5(1−e−x).
(30)
The required positive root is xλ​=4.9651142317…. The rearranged equation also has x=0, but that endpoint is not a peak. For g(x)=5(1−e−x)−x, g′(x)=5e−x−1 changes sign once, at x=log5. Thus g first increases and then decreases to negative values, crossing zero exactly once for x>0. Since x5/(ex−1) tends to zero at both endpoints, this stationary point is the unique global maximum. Solving the definition of x for wavelength gives
λmax​=kB​Txλ​hc​,b=kB​xλ​hc​.
(31)
The inverse-temperature dependence follows before any numerical root-finding. More generally Bλ​(T)=T5F(λT) for one fixed function F, so spectra at all temperatures have the same rescaled shape. This is stronger than a relation between two peak positions. Replacing the denominator by ex in the Wien approximation would instead give x=5 and a peak wavelength about 0.70% too short.
