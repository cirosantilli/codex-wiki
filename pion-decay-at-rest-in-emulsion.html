Pion decay at rest in emulsion (π+→μ++νμ​)

 Home Photography Photographic emulsion Nuclear emulsion Nuclear emulsion track experiment
OurBigBook.com Words: 423
Cecil Frank Powell's 1950 lecture illustrates the successive decays of pions and muons through their tracks and explains why the nearly fixed secondary muon range, allowing for range straggling, supports a single neutral decay partner. He describes its identification as a neutrino tentatively. The neutral particle itself leaves no recorded track. He contrasts this decay with the nuclear reactions caused by stopped negative pions, connecting the latter with the nuclear force.[ref]
Here is a modern special relativity derivation for the dominant leptonic pion decay, assuming a free pion at rest, a massless muon neutrino, and no emitted photon. Write mπ​ and mμ​ for the pion and muon masses. By conservation of momentum the daughter momenta are opposite with equal magnitude p. By conservation of energy and the energy–momentum relation,
mπ​c2=Eμ​+pc,Eμ2​=p2c2+mμ2​c4.
(3)
Substitute Eμ​=mπ​c2−pc, square, and cancel p2c2. This gives
p=2mπ​(mπ2​−mμ2​)c​,Eμ​=2mπ​(mπ2​+mμ2​)c2​,Tμ​=2mπ​(mπ​−mμ​)2c2​.
(4)
The resulting p is fixed, although the muon's direction is not set by the incoming stopping track. Thus the kink angle relative to that earlier track is not the angle between the two daughters: in the pion rest frame, the daughters are back to back. A neutral-partner arrow in a diagram represents inferred momentum, not an observed string of grains.
For an original calculation, adopt rounded input values mπ​c2=139.57MeV and mμ​c2=105.66MeV. They give pc=29.79MeV, Tμ​=4.119MeV and Eν​=29.79MeV. Their sum satisfies Tμ​+Eν​=(mπ​−mμ​)c2=33.91MeV. These are rounded calculation inputs and outputs, not a fit to the 1947 plates.
At fixed material and particle identity, a fixed initial kinetic energy suggests a narrow particle range distribution broadened by range straggling and measurement uncertainty. The synthetic example convolves a single chosen range with a normal distribution; a second example superposes two such chosen ranges. It explains why repeated comparable stopping distances can be informative, without pretending to extract a historical spectrum.
The decay equations do not identify the neutral partner as a neutrino on their own: a massless photon would satisfy the same energy and momentum equations. Nor does any isolated kink establish the pion's identity. The inference gains force from calibrated particle range, track grain density, repeated decay patterns and independent particle properties. Historical particle names should not obscure the distinction: a muon is a lepton, whereas a pion is a meson.
