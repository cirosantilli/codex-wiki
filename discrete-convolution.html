Discrete convolution ((a∗b)n​=∑m∈Z​am​bn−m​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Fourier analysis Convolution
OurBigBook.com Words: 450 Articles: 4
For sequences a,b indexed by the integers, discrete convolution adds products whose indices sum to the output index:
(a∗b)n​=∑m∈Z​am​bn−m​.
(82)
For finite sequences, treat every unspecified entry as zero; the sum then has finitely many nonzero terms. For infinite sequences, a convergence hypothesis is needed, for example absolute summability of both sequences.
Take a=(1,2,1) at indices 0,1,2 and b=(1,1) at indices 0,1. Their convolution is (1,3,3,1) at indices 0,1,2,3:
c0​=1⋅1=1,c1​=1⋅1+2⋅1=3,c2​=2⋅1+1⋅1=3,c3​=1⋅1=1.
(83)
Equivalently, cn​=an​+an−1​: add an unshifted and a one-step shifted copy of a. This explains the output by superposition, with every index included.
The same products appear in polynomial multiplication and generating functions:
(1+2z+z2)(1+z)=1+3z+3z2+z3.
(84)
A term am​zm times bk​zk contributes to coefficient m+k, precisely the index condition in discrete convolution. In probability, a counts the outcomes of two independent fair coin tosses by their number of heads, while b counts the outcomes of one more toss. Dividing the output by eight gives the probability mass function (1,3,3,1)/8 of the binomial distribution with n=3 and p=1/2. No new arithmetic is needed to pass from coefficient multiplication to counting independent sums.
The full finite convolution is different from circular convolution, where indices wrap modulo a chosen length. Wrapping this four-term output to length three adds its last entry to its first and gives (2,3,3). When using a discrete Fourier transform to compute the full convolution, zero padding to length at least the sum of the input lengths minus one prevents this wraparound. [ref]
