Brouwer fixed-point theorem (F(x∗​)=x∗​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Topological analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 7
The Brouwer fixed-point theorem says that every continuous map F:K→K has a fixed point when K is a nonempty compact set and a convex set in a finite-dimensional vector space over the real numbers. Equivalently, take K⊆Rn for a finite n, with the usual Euclidean space structure: there is some x∗​∈K such that F(x∗​)=x∗​. The conclusion guarantees at least one fixed point, without asserting uniqueness or telling us how quickly to find one. [ref]
The standard pictures are a closed ball, including a closed unit ball, and a simplex. A filled disk qualifies; its bounding circle alone does not. A probability simplex also qualifies even though its dimension is smaller than that of its ambient Euclidean space. The hypotheses have concrete meanings: being a convex set keeps every line segment between allowed points inside K, and compactness prevents escape or loss of a limiting point. In Euclidean space, the Heine-Borel theorem identifies compact sets with closed sets that are bounded sets. Crucially, every output must belong to the same K as the input.
For a fully computable example, let D={(u,v):u2+v2≤1} be the filled unit disk. Apply a counterclockwise quarter-turn rotation, shrink by 0.4, and add a translation:
R90​(u,v)=(−v,u),F(u,v)=0.4R90​(u,v)+(0.3,0.1).
(1)
This affine map is a continuous map. Because the rotation preserves the Euclidean norm, the triangle inequality gives, for every x∈D,
∥F(x)∥≤0.4∥x∥+∥(0.3,0.1)∥≤0.4+0.1​≈0.716228<1.
(2)
Thus the whole image lies strictly inside D. More precisely, it is a disk of radius 0.4 centred at (0.3,0.1). This containment check is what makes the Brouwer fixed-point theorem applicable; a formula being a continuous map by itself would not suffice.
The fixed point can now be found by a system of linear equations:
u=−52​v+103​,v=52​u+101​,x∗​=(5813​,5811​)≈(0.224138,0.189655).
(3)
Indeed, substitution gives F(x∗​)=(−22/290+87/290,26/290+29/290)=x∗​. Its Euclidean norm is 290​/58≈0.293610, safely inside D. The figure shows arrows from sample inputs to their outputs; an arrow disappears at the fixed point because the displacement F(x)−x vanishes there.
Repeated iterations of a map happen to succeed for this example. Starting at x0​=(1,0) and setting xk+1​=F(xk​) gives x1​=(0.3,0.5), x2​=(0.1,0.22), and x3​=(0.212,0.14). Subtracting the fixed point equation yields xk+1​−x∗​=0.4R90​(xk​−x∗​), so the Euclidean distance to x∗​ shrinks by exactly 0.4 each step. This extra fact follows from the contraction mapping property and the Banach fixed-point theorem. It is additional information beyond the Brouwer fixed-point theorem.
The distinction becomes visible with two simpler continuous maps of the same disk. The pure rotation G(u,v)=(−v,u) has exactly one fixed point, (0,0), since u=−v and v=u force both to vanish. Yet iterations of a map starting at (1,0) here cycle through (0,1), (−1,0), and (0,−1) forever. Their Euclidean distance from (0,0) stays one, so there is no convergence in a metric space. The reflection H(u,v)=(u,−v) has an entire line segment of fixed points, namely {(u,0):−1≤u≤1}. Starting off that line segment produces an alternating pair. Existence, uniqueness, and convergence of iterations of a map are separate questions.
Removing hypotheses changes the answer. Restrict the same quarter-turn rotation to the circle S1={(u,v):u2+v2=1}. It remains a continuous map into that compact set, but its only possible fixed point, (0,0), has been removed. The circle is not a convex set. Conversely, on the unbounded convex set R, the translation t↦t+1 has no fixed point; on the open interval (0,1), t↦(t+1)/2 also has none. Neither domain is a compact set. The finite-dimensional closed ball statement should likewise not be extended to arbitrary infinite-dimensional vector spaces, where a closed ball need not be a compact set.
The one-dimensional version explains the connection to the intermediate value theorem. For a continuous map f:[a,b]→[a,b], put h(t)=f(t)−t. Then h(a)≥0 and h(b)≤0, so the intermediate value theorem gives h(c)=0 for some c∈[a,b]. In higher dimensions, the Brouwer fixed-point theorem supplies a corresponding existence principle when this scalar sign argument is unavailable. Its practical value is ensuring that a proposed equation x=F(x) has a solution once a suitable domain is found; it supplies no general promise of a fast algorithm.
A useful application is positive matrix eigenvector from simplex normalization: rescale the output of a strictly positive matrix to make its entries sum to one. For example, take
A=(21​12​),x=(t,1−t),0≤t≤1.
(4)
On this probability simplex, Ax=(1+t,2−t) has entry sum 3, so the normalized continuous map is T(x)=((1+t)/3,(2−t)/3). Both entries are positive and sum to one, keeping the image inside the probability simplex. Its fixed point equation is t=(1+t)/3, hence t=1/2. Consequently x∗​=(1/2,1/2) satisfies Ax∗​=(3/2,3/2)=3x∗​: a strictly positive eigenvector with eigenvalue 3. In larger dimensions, the Brouwer fixed-point theorem guarantees the corresponding positive eigenvector without requiring us to solve a system of linear equations in advance. The fixed point expresses preservation of relative weights, rather than preservation of their unnormalized size.
Proofs can be approached through the no-retraction theorem or Sperner's lemma. In the latter approach, increasingly fine triangulations locate approximate fixed points; compactness supplies a convergent subsequence, and continuous maps turn its limit into an exact fixed point. [ref]
A primary historical reference is L. E. J. Brouwer's 1911 paper Über Abbildung von Mannigfaltigkeiten, whose digitized original is hosted by Zenodo under DOI 10.1007/BF01456812. This gives a direct route to the early literature behind the Brouwer fixed-point theorem. [ref]
