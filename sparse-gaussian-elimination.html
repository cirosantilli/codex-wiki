Sparse Gaussian elimination

 ... Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Numerical analysis Numerical linear algebra Gaussian elimination
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 10
Sparse Gaussian elimination solves a linear system by Gaussian elimination while exploiting its sparse matrix structure to avoid storing zeros and performing unnecessary arithmetic operations. The catch is that zeros in the input need not remain zero: eliminating a shared unknown can couple two equations that previously had no direct coupling. These new positions are fill-in, and their number can depend strongly on the order of elimination.
For a real symmetric positive-definite matrix, the usual direct method is a Cholesky decomposition. Choose an ordering, use symbolic factorization to allocate the predicted factor entries, compute their values, then solve two triangular systems. Compressed sparse storage makes the reduced storage useful. General LU decomposition may also need numerical partial pivoting, so an ordering chosen for sparsity can change during factorization.[ref]
For a fixed ordering, let P be the permutation matrix that puts the unknowns in that order. Permute both rows and columns:
B=PAPT=CCT,Cy=Pb,CTz=y,x=PTz.
(42)
Here C is the lower triangular matrix in the Cholesky decomposition of B; z=Px. Forward substitution and back substitution give the original solution. The simultaneous permutation preserves the symmetric positive-definite matrix property because vTBv=(PTv)TA(PTv)>0 for every nonzero vector v. A row permutation alone generally loses the symmetric matrix structure.
The local mechanism is a Schur complement. If the active pivot element is akk​, then for remaining indices i,j,
aijnew​=aij​−akk​aik​akj​​.
(43)
Even when aij​=0, two nonzero couplings through k make the second term nonzero. Thus the elimination graph removes k and joins its remaining neighbors into a clique. The fill-path criterion for symmetric elimination extends this local rule across several steps. The elimination tree organizes the resulting dependencies; the minimum degree algorithm and nested dissection choose orderings to control them.
This connection between graphs and linear systems was already explicit in Donald J. Rose, R. Endre Tarjan and George S. Lueker's 1976 study of fill-in and perfect elimination orderings.[ref]
