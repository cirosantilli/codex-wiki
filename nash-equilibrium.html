Nash equilibrium (σ∗)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Game theory Bimatrix game
OurBigBook.com Words: 885 Articles: 13
A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile in which every player's strategy is a best response to the others. For a finite game and mixed strategies, its precise condition is
Ui​(σi∗​,σ−i∗​)≥Ui​(τi​,σ−i∗​)for every player i and every τi​∈Δ(Si​).
(27)
The opponents' strategies σ−i∗​ are held fixed while testing each unilateral deviation. Equality is allowed: a player may have other equally good best responses. Because expected payoff is a linear function of the player's own mixture, it suffices to test every pure strategy. Equivalently, every choice in the support of a mixed strategy must attain the largest pure-choice expected payoff, and no unused choice may exceed it.
The two worked examples illustrate different uses of this condition. Matching pennies has no pure strategy Nash equilibrium, but its unique mixed strategy Nash equilibrium removes each player's vulnerability to predictable play. The asymmetric coordination example has three Nash equilibria, and its mixed one fails Pareto efficiency. A Nash equilibrium tests individual incentives, whereas Pareto efficiency compares feasible outcomes that may require several players to change together. In the prisoner's dilemma, for example, mutual defection is a Nash equilibrium even though mutual cooperation gives both players a larger payoff because R>P.
Existence in a finite game is guaranteed by Nash's theorem. The theorem does not select one Nash equilibrium, promise Pareto efficiency, or assert convergence of a learning process. Nash's [ref] used the Kakutani fixed-point theorem; his [ref] gave a proof using the Brouwer fixed-point theorem. The connection to a fixed point captures the idea of choices that remain mutually optimal when considered together.
