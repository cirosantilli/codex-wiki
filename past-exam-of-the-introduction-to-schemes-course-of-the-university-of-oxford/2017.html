Apply the surjectivity of f(Y) to the identity morphism 1Y​. It gives a morphism of schemes g:Y→X satisfying f∘g=1Y​. To obtain the other identity, compare g∘f and 1X​ in X(X). Their images under f(X) agree, since
f∘(g∘f)=(f∘g)∘f=f=f∘1X​.
(1)
The injectivity of f(X) therefore gives g∘f=1X​. Thus
f is an isomorphism of schemes, with inverse g.​
(2)
Only surjectivity for the test scheme Y and injectivity for the test scheme X were needed. This is the isomorphism-detection argument behind the Yoneda lemma for the functor of points.

No. Let k=F2​ be the field with two elements, put A=k[ε]/(ε2), and take the closed immersion
f:Speck↪SpecA​
(3)
induced by the ring homomorphism q:A↠k with q(ε)=0.
For every field K, a ring homomorphism α:A→K must send ε to zero: α(ε)2=0, and a field has no nonzero nilpotent elements. Consequently α factors uniquely through q. Passing from ring homomorphisms to morphisms of schemes between affine schemes gives a bijection
f(SpecK):HomSch​(SpecK,Speck) ∼ ​HomSch​(SpecK,SpecA).
(4)
This concerns all morphisms of schemes, without requiring them to be over k: if K does not have characteristic two, both sets are empty; otherwise each contains exactly one element.
Nevertheless, f is not an isomorphism of schemes. Its map on global sections is q, whose kernel of a ring homomorphism contains the nonzero element ε, so q is not a ring isomorphism. The target is the nonreduced scheme defined by the dual numbers, whereas the source is a reduced scheme. This illustrates why field-valued points do not detect nilpotents.

Yes. First, the hypothesis implies that f(T) is injective for every scheme T, even when T is not an affine scheme. Indeed, suppose a,b:T→X satisfy f∘a=f∘b. Choose an affine open cover T=⋃λ​Vλ​. On each Vλ​, injectivity of f(Vλ​) gives a∣Vλ​​=b∣Vλ​​. By gluing morphisms of schemes, a=b.
Now choose an affine open cover Y=⋃i​Ui​, and denote its inclusion maps by ji​:Ui​↪Y. Surjectivity of f(Ui​) gives morphisms of schemes gi​:Ui​→X with f∘gi​=ji​. On Ui​∩Uj​, the two restrictions have the same composite with f. By the injectivity just established for arbitrary test schemes,
gi​∣Ui​∩Uj​​=gj​∣Ui​∩Uj​​.
(5)
In particular, the overlaps need not be affine schemes. Gluing morphisms of schemes supplies g:Y→X with g∣Ui​​=gi​, and f∘g=1Y​ because this holds on every Ui​.
Finally,
f∘(g∘f)=f=f∘1X​.
(6)
Injectivity of f(X), already proved without an affineness assumption, gives g∘f=1X​. Therefore
f is an isomorphism of schemes.​
(7)
This proves the relevant instance of affine test schemes determine scheme morphisms: the restricted functor of points still retains the information needed to construct the inverse.

The localization of a ring Rr​ is generated over R by the single element 1/r, since every fraction can be written as
rna​=ϕ(a)(1/r)n.
(8)
The presentation of a localization at one element is
Rr​≅R[t]/(rt−1),t⟼1/r.​
(9)
To verify the presentation even when R has zero divisors, observe that r is invertible in R[t]/(rt−1) with inverse t. Hence the map from R to this quotient ring extends uniquely to Rr​. This extension and the displayed map are inverse ring homomorphisms: they agree on R and send the designated inverse of r to the designated inverse of r.
Thus Rr​ is a finitely generated algebra over a ring, and the criterion for a morphism of finite type between affine schemes applies to u:SpecRr​→SpecR. There is just one affine scheme in the required cover of the source, with one algebra generator. Therefore u is of finite type. If r is nilpotent, the same presentation is the zero ring, and the source is the empty scheme; the conclusion still holds.

Put C={z}​, where the closure is taken in the underlying topological space of X. It is nonempty because it contains z.
Suppose C=A∪B for two closed subsets of C in the subspace topology. Since C is a closed subset of X, both A and B are also closed subsets of X. The point z lies in one of them, say A. By the defining minimality of the closure, every closed subset of X containing z contains C. Hence C⊆A, so A=C.
Thus C cannot be expressed as the union of two proper closed subsets, proving that C is an irreducible closed subset. This proves closure of a point is irreducible in every topological space; the scheme assumption is unnecessary. Equivalently, z is a generic point of its own closure.

Yes: every scheme is a Kolmogorov space. Choose an affine open subscheme V=SpecA containing x. If y∈/V, take U=V.
Otherwise, the distinct points x,y∈V correspond to distinct prime ideals px​,py​⊆A. There is an element in one of these prime ideals and not the other. After interchanging the names of the two points if necessary, choose
a∈px​∖py​.
(10)
The principal open subscheme
U=D(a)={p∈SpecA:a∈/p}​
(11)
contains y and does not contain x. It is an open subset of V, hence an open subset of X because V is open in X. This establishes (∗) in every case, proving schemes are Kolmogorov spaces.

a OurBigBook.com
Write Pr=Pkr​ over its ground field; the argument also works over any Noetherian affine base. Since ι is a closed immersion, X is a noetherian scheme and a quasi-compact scheme. Let
L=ι∗OPr​(1),F(n)=F⊗OX​​L⊗n.
(12)
Here the pullback of a sheaf of modules makes L an invertible sheaf, the restriction of the twisting sheaf on projective space. It is an ample line bundle.
By global generation by sufficiently positive twists, choose n≥0 such that F(n) is a globally generated sheaf. Finitely many global sections suffice: at each point, finite generation of the stalk of the coherent sheaf F(n) lets us select finitely many generating germs of global sections. Those sections generate on a neighbourhood, because their cokernel sheaf is a coherent sheaf with zero stalk at that point. A finite subcover, using that X is a quasi-compact scheme, supplies finitely many global sections generating everywhere. This is finite global generation on a quasi-compact scheme, and gives a surjective sheaf morphism
OX⊕m​↠F(n).
(13)
Tensor with (L∨)⊗n, where L∨ is the dual of a sheaf. Here tensoring with an invertible sheaf is exact: locally the tensor product of sheaves is tensoring with a free module of rank one. Using the permitted identity L⊗L∨≅OX​, we obtain
G=((L∨)⊗n)⊕m=OX​(−n)⊕m,G↠F⟶0.​
(14)
A finite direct sum of sheaves of invertible sheaves is a locally free sheaf of finite rank, and it is a coherent sheaf on the noetherian scheme X. This constructs the required G. No regularity or smoothness assumption on X is needed.

b OurBigBook.com
Use homogeneous coordinates x0​,…,xr​ on projective space and let
Ui​=X∩D+​(xi​),0≤i≤r.
(15)
These r+1 open subschemes cover X. Each is a closed subscheme of the affine scheme D+​(xi​), hence is an affine scheme.
Every nonempty finite intersection is affine as well. Indeed, inside D+​(xi0​​) the intersection with D+​(xi1​​),…,D+​(xip​​) is the principal open subscheme obtained by inverting
∏j=1p​xi0​​xij​​​.
(16)
Intersecting it with the closed subscheme X gives Ui0​​∩⋯∩Uip​​, again an affine scheme.
For any quasi-coherent sheaf F, vanishing of quasi-coherent cohomology on an affine scheme makes every such intersection acyclic for F. The acyclic cover theorem therefore identifies the sheaf cohomology of F with the Čech cohomology of this cover. Its alternating Čech cochain complex has terms
Cˇp(U,F)=∏0≤i0​<⋯<ip​≤r​Γ(Ui0​​∩⋯∩Uip​​,F),
(17)
which vanish for p>r: one cannot choose p+1 distinct indices from r+1 indices. Consequently
Hk(X,F)=0(k>r),N=max{1,r}.​
(18)
The choice N=max{1,r} meets the strict requirement N>0 even when r=0. It is independent of F, and applies to all quasi-coherent sheaves, including those which are not coherent sheaves. This is the cohomological dimension bound from an affine cover.

Fix a coherent sheaf F and an integer k>M. Set K0​=F. Repeatedly apply a to construct short exact sequences of sheaves
0⟶Kj+1​⟶Gj​⟶Kj​⟶0,
(19)
where each Gj​ is a coherent sheaf and a locally free sheaf. Each kernel sheaf Kj+1​ is a coherent sheaf because X is a noetherian scheme. Thus the construction can be repeated as many times as needed; it does not require the kernel sheaves themselves to be locally free sheaves.
For every integer q>M, the long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology contains
Hq(X,Gj​)⟶Hq(X,Kj​) δj​ ​Hq+1(X,Kj+1​)⟶Hq+1(X,Gj​).
(20)
Both outer groups vanish by the hypothesis, since q and q+1 exceed M. Hence δj​ is an isomorphism. Iterating this dimension shifting in sheaf cohomology gives
Hk(X,F)≅Hk+1(X,K1​)≅⋯≅Hk+s(X,Ks​).
(21)
Choose s≥0 with k+s>N, where N is the uniform bound from b. The coherent sheaf Ks​ is a quasi-coherent sheaf, so b makes the last group zero. Therefore
Hk(X,F)=0for every coherent sheaf F and every k>M.​
(22)
The argument uses a finite initial segment of a locally free resolution. There is no need to assume that every coherent sheaf has a finite locally free resolution: the uniform bound from b is what terminates the argument.
