Chemical bond

 Home Chemistry
OurBigBook.com Words: 17k Articles: 165
A chemical bond associates chemical atoms or groups of chemical atoms into a sufficiently stable structure to treat it as a distinct entity. The concept covers connections within molecules and in extended solids; see the IUPAC Gold Book, chemical bond. It is useful because composition alone does not specify how the chemical atoms are connected, and those connections help determine a chemical substance's properties and possible chemical reactions.
In a covalent bond, electrons are shared between chemical atoms. Two hydrogen chemical atoms can form a molecular hydrogen molecule, with its electrons interacting with both atomic nuclei. In the usual description of this diatomic molecule, the potential energy as a function of nuclear separation has a minimum: moving the nuclei too close or too far raises the energy. See 7.2 Covalent Bonding.
Separating bonded chemical atoms requires energy; forming the corresponding chemical bonds releases energy. Whether an overall chemical reaction releases energy depends on both the chemical bonds broken and those formed. The lines in the stoichiometry diagram indicate connections schematically, rather than literal rods or a sequence of microscopic events.

Bond-dissociation energy (D)

Words: 1k Articles: 5
Chemical bond OurBigBook.com
A bond-dissociation energy specifies the energy needed for a stated dissociation into separated fragments. An energy from the lowest vibrational state, an energy from the bottom of a potential energy well, and a finite-temperature molar dissociation enthalpy require different conventions; do not silently mix them in an electronegativity formula.
Which fragments, in which quantum states, and at which temperature? For a neutral gaseous molecule with a specified two-electron covalent bond, the usual thermochemical convention is homolysis,
A−B(g)⟶A∙(g)+B∙(g).
(1)
Each fragment receives one of the two bonding electrons; the dots denote unpaired electrons. The final fragments are isolated from each other, with their own relaxed molecular geometry and specified electronic quantum states. The energy cost includes this relaxation, rather than merely stretching the initial chemical bond while freezing everything else. For a neutral parent, heterolysis instead produces A+(g)+B−(g), or the opposite assignment of electric charges; those are different final states and different energies. A value for gaseous neutral fragments cannot be transferred to ions, a chemical solution, or a solid without accounting for those changed conditions.[ref]
The distinction is useful beyond terminology. Pauling electronegativity compares consistently defined bond-dissociation energies; photodissociation can reveal a specified ground-state threshold; a thermal chemical reaction balance requires compatible enthalpies. A threshold is also different from an activation energy: it compares initial and final quantum states, whereas a barrier can obstruct the path between them.

Bond potential-well depth (De​)

Words: 111
Bond-dissociation energy OurBigBook.com
For a diatomic molecule, let V(r) be the electronic potential energy at bond length r, with the atomic nuclei treated as fixed while finding the electronic energy. Choose zero at infinitely separated chemical atoms in the specified electronic quantum states. If the minimum occurs at re​, the bond potential-well depth is
De​=V(∞)−V(re​)=−V(re​).
(2)
The bottom of this curve describes an equilibrium bond length, not an attainable motionless vibrational quantum state. Changing the zero of potential energy shifts both endpoints equally and leaves De​ unchanged. A quantum harmonic oscillator approximates the curve near its minimum but rises without limit, so that local approximation alone cannot supply a dissociation asymptote. A Morse potential supplies both a minimum and a finite separated-fragment limit.[ref]

Ground-state dissociation energy (D0​)

Words: 146
Bond-dissociation energy OurBigBook.com
The ground-state dissociation energy starts at the lowest vibrational quantum state, with the lowest allowed rotational quantum state and the fragment states explicitly specified. For a diatomic molecule whose fragments are ground-state chemical atoms, omitting any additional rotational correction,
D0​=De​−EZP​,
(3)
where EZP​ is the vibrational zero-point energy measured upward from the well minimum. The initial molecule already has this energy, so less additional energy is needed to reach the same final limit.[ref]
For fragments that are themselves molecules, include their zero-point energies too. Define De​ as the electronic energy difference between separately relaxed products and reactant; then, with consistent rotational conventions,
D0​=De​+∑fragments​EZP​−EZP,parent​.
(4)
Thus “subtract the parent's zero-point energy” is sufficient for atomic products but generally incomplete for molecular products. An excited final quantum state raises its channel threshold by its excitation energy; that threshold does not become the ground-product D0​ just because a detector selects it.

Bond-dissociation enthalpy (Δdiss​H∘(T))

Words: 179
Bond-dissociation energy OurBigBook.com
A bond-dissociation enthalpy is the molar enthalpy change for the specified homolysis at temperature T, with reactant and products in specified standard states. Its usual units are kJmol−1. The superscript ∘ specifies standard states; it does not fix T to room temperature.[ref]
Write δhi​(T)=Hm,i∘​(T)−Hm,i∘​(0) for each species' molar thermal enthalpy increment. With D0​ an energy per parent molecule,
Δdiss​H∘(T)=NA​D0​+δhA​(T)+δhB​(T)−δhAB​(T).
(5)
Here NA​ is the Avogadro constant. The thermal increments include translational kinetic energy, molecular rotation, thermally excited molecular vibration and electronic states when populated, and the pV contribution to enthalpy. They are additional to the zero-point energies already included in D0​. If values of standard enthalpy of formation at the same T are available, Hess's law gives the same quantity as Δf​H∘(A)+Δf​H∘(B)−Δf​H∘(AB).
Units alone do not identify the convention. Multiplying either De​ or D0​ by the Avogadro constant gives molar energy, but does not add thermal enthalpy. One electronvolt per dissociation corresponds to 96.485332kJmol−1. A spectroscopic wavenumber ν represents energy hcν, using the Planck constant h and speed of light c; 1cm−1 corresponds to 0.011962657kJmol−1.[ref]

Stipulated bond dissociation model

Words: 214
Bond-dissociation energy OurBigBook.com
Take an invented diatomic molecule with a Morse potential, setting the separated-atom energy to zero:
V(r)=De​[(1−e−a(r−re​))2−1],De​=2.0000eV,A=ℏωe​=0.2000eV.
(6)
Here a is an inverse length, re​ the equilibrium bond length, and ωe​=a2De​/μ​, with reduced mass μ. These stipulated parameters describe a simple model, not a measured chemical substance. For the one-dimensional Morse oscillator, energy eigenvalues above the minimum are
ϵv​=A(v+21​)−4De​A2​(v+21​)2,v=0,1,…,v+21​<A2De​​.
(7)
Consequently,
EZP​=2A​−16De​A2​=0.09875eV,D0​=2.0000−0.09875=1.90125eV.
(8)
The harmonic estimate A/2=0.1000eV is slightly larger than this model's exact zero-point energy. The physical bound-level restriction matters: the quadratic expression is not an infinite ladder of vibrational quantum states.
For a separate thermal illustration using this same stipulated D0​, assume an ideal gas, fully active molecular rotation of the parent, negligible thermal molecular vibration, and negligible electronic excitation for all species. Each monatomic product contributes δh=5RT/2; the diatomic molecule contributes 7RT/2. Thus the net increment is 3RT/2, with molar gas constant R. At T=300K,
NA​De​≈192.971kJmol−1,NA​D0​≈183.443kJmol−1,Δdiss​H∘≈187.184kJmol−1.
(9)
This last result follows from the stated thermal model, not a universal correction for every chemical bond. Frozen rotations, accessible excited states, different fragment composition, or a condensed phase change the thermal increments.

Sequential bond-dissociation energies (D1​,D2​,…)

Words: 219
Bond-dissociation energy OurBigBook.com
Equivalent chemical bonds in an intact molecule need not have equal successive bond-dissociation enthalpies. Once one chemical bond is removed, the next reactant has a different electronic quantum state and may relax to a different molecular geometry. The successive steps compare different species. This distinction also explains why a tabulated average chemical bond strength is not necessarily the cost of the first cleavage.[ref]
For an invented species AB2​, stipulate the following gas-phase steps, with all enthalpies at the same temperature and the same standard states:
AB2​(g)AB(g)​⟶AB(g)+B(g),⟶A(g)+B(g),​D1​D2​​=300kJmol−1,=400kJmol−1.​
(10)
The symbols stand for hypothetical species with consistently chosen electronic states. They are not values for water or another measured molecule. By Hess's law, the total enthalpy of atomization for AB2​(g)→A(g)+2B(g) is 700kJmol−1; the arithmetic mean per broken chemical bond is 350kJmol−1. Neither individual step equals that mean. Nor must the second step be cheaper: here it is deliberately more expensive. An exact sequence sums exactly; substituting averages taken from other molecules generally gives only an estimate.

Homolysis

Words: 27
Chemical bond OurBigBook.com
Homolysis splits a covalent bond with one bonding electron assigned to each fragment. A neutral closed-shell molecule thereby produces fragments with unpaired electrons, each a radical. The usual bond-dissociation enthalpy uses this product convention.

Heterolysis

Words: 30
Chemical bond OurBigBook.com
Heterolysis assigns both bonding electrons to one fragment. For an initially neutral molecule, the resulting cation and anion define a different dissociation channel from homolysis; their isolated gas-phase energy differs from that of solvated ions.

Photodissociation

Words: 461 Articles: 5
Chemical bond OurBigBook.com
Photodissociation separates a molecule into fragments after photon absorption. For an isolated parent with a single absorbed photon, conservation of energy gives
hν+Einitial,int​=D0​+Eproducts,int​+Erecoil​.
(11)
Internal excitation energies here are relative to the parent and product ground states used to define D0​; Erecoil​ is the total product translational kinetic energy in the parent's centre of mass frame. Measuring one product's kinetic energy is insufficient by itself. For two fragments of masses mA​,mB​, conservation of momentum gives Erecoil​=EA​(mA​+mB​)/mB​ when A is detected. Unknown product excitation can conceal part of the available energy.

The first-order rate coefficient for a specified photodissociation channel. It integrates spectral actinic flux, absorption cross section and channel quantum yield. Absorption without dissociation does not contribute to that channel's rate. Its units are inverse time.

Words: 42

Photofragment-ion imaging

Words: 287 Articles: 1
Photodissociation OurBigBook.com
Photofragment-ion imaging detects fragment velocities by ionizing neutral products and mapping their motion onto a position-sensitive detector using an electric field. In a velocity-mapping implementation, the image radius measures transverse speed under fixed lens settings. Selecting an arrival-time slice helps extract the central plane of the three-dimensional distribution. Species identification and electronic quantum state selection are essential for turning a radius into a particular dissociation threshold.

Nitrogen dissociation threshold from fragment images

Words: 227
Photofragment-ion imaging OurBigBook.com
A molecular nitrogen experiment used a 0.5mm pulsed nozzle and two skimmers. A 5cm tube raised the rotational temperature. Opposed vacuum-ultraviolet laser pulses excited selected molecular levels and, about 10ns later, selectively ionized excited nitrogen fragments. A microchannel plate detector, phosphor screen and camera recorded radii.[ref]
The measured squared radii were fitted against molecular excitation energies, referenced to the parent ground state:
Ej​=D0,channel​+κRj2​.
(12)
The intercept at zero radius gave the excited-product threshold; the fitted slope absorbed the detector scale. A wavemeter monitored the excitation source; scanning the probe covered the recoil Doppler shift. Subtracting the atomic excitation energies converted the intercept to ground-product D0​.[ref]
An apertured grounded plate and detector timing rejected faster competing channels. Selecting fragment electronic states distinguished nearby thresholds. Agreement of De​ across isotopes after zero-point energy correction tested consistency. The method still requires correctly assigned levels, adequate near-threshold signal and stable imaging; a signal drop alone can reflect another channel or a barrier.[ref]
This measurement determines a state-resolved ground-state dissociation energy. Obtaining a finite-temperature bond-dissociation enthalpy requires the thermal increments above; obtaining a bond potential-well depth requires the zero-point energy correction. The three quantities describe different starting conditions for the same specified separation.

A bond length describes the separation of the atomic nuclei of two bonded chemical atoms. Distinguish an equilibrium geometry from an instantaneous or experimentally averaged separation. Methods of determining molecular geometry can use different averaging conventions, so reported bond lengths need their measurement context. See the IUPAC Gold Book: bond length. Changing a bond length is one possible reaction coordinate.

Hydrogen bond (X−H⋯Y)

Words: 1k Articles: 11
Chemical bond OurBigBook.com
A hydrogen bond is an attractive association involving a hydrogen attached to another chemical atom, and an accepting atom or group. In the IUPAC 2011 definition, the atom X in X−H is more electronegative than hydrogen, and evidence of bond formation is required. The familiar strong examples use O–H or N–H groups, but restricting the concept to only nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine misses weaker cases. A short distance alone is not the entire definition.[ref]
In the depicted water pair, one O–H group is the hydrogen bond donor; the other oxygen supplies the hydrogen bond acceptor. The solid O–H line denotes a covalent bond within a water molecule; the dotted H···O line denotes the hydrogen bond between them. One water molecule can play both roles in a larger arrangement because it has O–H groups and lone pairs.
This makes hydrogen bonds useful for understanding organization beyond a single molecular formula. They connect water molecules and contribute to protein folding and recognition between molecules. The same types of chemical atoms can support different associations depending on which donor and acceptor sites are exposed and how they are oriented. Alberts and colleagues discuss these noncovalent interactions in Molecular Biology of the Cell, the chemical components of a cell.
Hydrogen bonds are often directional, with an approximately straight donor–H–acceptor arrangement favourable in familiar cases; strength and preferred geometry depend on the system. The IUPAC definition uses converging experimental or theoretical evidence rather than one universal distance threshold. The author-hosted 2009 task-group draft, §§3.1–3.3 explains the definition and typical criteria leading to the recommendation.

Hydrogen bond donor (X−H)

Words: 327 Articles: 1
Hydrogen bond OurBigBook.com
A hydrogen bond donor is the X−H group supplying the attached hydrogen in a hydrogen bond X−H⋯Y. The donor atom X, or the whole molecule containing it, may also be called the donor. The terminology does not imply transfer of a free proton: in the ordinary water pair, the O–H covalent bond remains. Familiar donors include O–H groups in water and N–H groups in Ammonia. See Andrew Glasfeld, Intermolecular Attraction, pp. 12–13: donor and acceptor roles.
The attached hydrogen is essential to this role. At the nitrogen site, an unprotonated amine R3​N can be a hydrogen bond acceptor, but has no N–H hydrogen bond donor. Protonation gives R3​NH+: the new N–H group can donate, while that nitrogen no longer has the accepting lone pair. This is a change of chemical form, not a permanent label attached to a chemical element. Here R denotes a group attached through carbon; the statement concerns the nitrogen site and does not rule out weaker C–H associations elsewhere. See McMurry, Organic Chemistry, 24.3: amine protonation and Glasfeld: protonated amines as donors.
An amide with an N–H covalent bond can donate even though its nitrogen is ordinarily not an accepting site. For example, in R−C(=O)−NH−R′, N–H is a hydrogen bond donor and the carbonyl group's oxygen is a hydrogen bond acceptor. Replacing the remaining N–H by another group attached through carbon removes this N–H donor without removing the accepting oxygen. The analogous division of roles in a peptide bond helps explain protein structure. See Glasfeld: amide roles and biological interactions. The useful question is therefore which site supplies the hydrogen, rather than whether the whole substance is simply “hydrogen bonding”.

Hydrogen bond acceptor

Words: 413 Articles: 1
Hydrogen bond OurBigBook.com
A hydrogen bond acceptor supplies the electron-rich atom or group Y interacting with the attached hydrogen of a hydrogen bond donor in X−H⋯Y. An oxygen lone pair in water is the familiar example. Acceptance describes the association, not necessarily acquisition of a proton. A single molecule, such as water or Ammonia, can offer both hydrogen bond donor and hydrogen bond acceptor sites. See Glasfeld: water and nitrogen-containing accepting sites.
A lone pair drawn in a Lewis structure is a useful clue, not a universal test. At an ordinary neutral amide nitrogen, the nominal lone pair participates in chemical resonance with the carbonyl group, so this nitrogen is ordinarily excluded from lists of hydrogen bond acceptors; the carbonyl group's oxygen remains an accepting site. Protonation changes the test again: NH4+​ has four N–H covalent bonds and no nitrogen lone pair, whereas Ammonia has three and one. These distinctions follow the actual chemical form, and accepting ability also depends on the partner and whether it can approach the site. See McMurry: amide resonance and protonation and Glasfeld: limitations of nitrogen accepting sites.
Conversely, an accepting group need not supply an atom-centred lone pair. The following phenol–benzene experiment probes an O–H donor interacting with the ring's distributed π electron density.
Saggu, Levinson and Boxer (2011) used infrared spectroscopy to compare 200mmolL−1 phenol in different solvents. The O–H stretching peak was at 3611cm−1 in CCl4​ and 3555.9cm−1 in benzene. To distinguish association from a general solvent effect, they diluted the ring-containing chemical solutions with CCl4​: two peaks appeared, with the free peak nearly fixed while the associated peak depended on the partner. Replacing the O–H hydrogen with deuterium reproduced the ordering of shifts for different partners. Thus the perturbed O–H group identifies the hydrogen bond donor; the ring supplies the hydrogen bond acceptor. A peak shift alone does not specify a unique molecular geometry. [ref]
The roles make recognition between molecules intelligible: exposed hydrogen bond donors need compatible hydrogen bond acceptors in reachable positions. A count of sites is a starting description, not a prediction of binding strength. In water, forming a contact between two partners can also replace their existing hydrogen bonds to water; the Gibbs free energy of association concerns the whole change. See Glasfeld, pp. 15–16: competition with solvent interactions.

Valence electron

Words: 147
Chemical bond OurBigBook.com
A valence electron belongs to the electronic arrangement used to describe a chemical atom's chemical bonds and ordinary chemical reactions. For the neutral hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen chemical atoms used here, the valence electrons occupy the outermost occupied electron shell. Their respective electron configurations are 1s1, 1s22s22p2, 1s22s22p3, and 1s22s22p4, giving one, four, five, and six valence electrons. See Chemistry 2e, 6.4: electron configurations and valence electrons.
Lewis structures show these valence electrons while omitting the inner electrons. Consequently, eight drawn valence electrons in water do not mean eight total electrons: neutral water has ten, with two inner oxygen electrons omitted. Neutral Ammonia likewise has ten total electrons but eight valence electrons; neutral carbon dioxide has twenty-two total electrons but sixteen valence electrons.
This distinction makes the small counts useful: connections and possible lone pairs can be organized without representing every electron or solving the entire many-electron wavefunction. The outermost-electron shell shortcut applies to the examples above, rather than serving as a universal definition for all chemical elements; the electronic arrangement relevant to chemical bonds can also involve other electron subshells.
