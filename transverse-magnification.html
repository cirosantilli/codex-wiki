Transverse magnification (m=hi​/ho​)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Optics Image formation Magnification
OurBigBook.com Words: 240
Transverse magnification is the ratio of corresponding signed lengths perpendicular to the optical axis. For an ideal image of a small arrow of nonzero height,
m=ho​hi​​.
(35)
Use the same upward-positive height convention in both planes. Thus an upright 0.50cm arrow imaged as −0.25cm has m=−1/2: inverted and half as tall. The sign describes orientation; real image versus virtual image describes ray intersections. They are separate properties, even when a particular thin lens setup correlates them.[ref]
For a thin lens with object coordinate −u and image coordinate v, the undeviated centre light ray is the straight line through (−u,ho​) and (0,0), so its height at x=v is hi​=−ho​v/u. This is the signed similar triangles argument. Combining it with the thin lens equation gives
m=−uv​=−u−ff​(u=f).
(36)
For f>0, u>2f gives −1<m<0; u=2f gives m=−1; f<u<2f gives m<−1; and 0<u<f gives m>1. At u=f, the image is conventionally “at infinity”: the divergent expressions for v and m describe the limiting behaviour, not a finite image of infinite height. Different object points produce parallel bundles in different directions, which is why angular magnification remains meaningful.
The real image experiment tests m in two independent ways: signed ratios of measured corresponding intervals, and −v/u from measured longitudinal distances. As a further prediction, moving from u=30cm to u=15cm for f=10cm swaps u,v, changing m=−1/2 to m=−2. The two image sizes differ by a factor of four, while both images remain inverted. This reversal follows from the symmetric thin lens equation and connects to the Bessel lens displacement method.
