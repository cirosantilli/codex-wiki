Post-receptoral processing compares cone signals, organizes those comparisons across space and time, and uses them to represent surfaces and objects. Three receptor classes do not imply three neurons labelled with three color names. A complete answer must distinguish the information available at the receptors from its neural transformation and its perceptual interpretation.
The principle of univariance supplies the starting constraint. For one cone class, an absorption-dependent response does not identify wavelength independently of intensity. Comparing differently sensitive classes makes chromatic information available. Relative to an adapted background, cone contrasts can be written cj​=(Qj​−Qj0​)/Qj0​, where Qj0​>0 is the background catch. This representation separates a change relative to the prevailing input from the raw number of absorbed photons. It is a useful coordinate system, not a claim that every neuron implements this exact formula.
Retinal circuitry immediately transforms the signals. Light hyperpolarizes cone cells and changes their transmitter release. ON and OFF bipolar pathways respond differently to changes in cone output, while horizontal and amacrine circuitry contribute spatial and temporal interactions. Retinal ganglion cells transmit spike trains rather than simply forwarding a cone's voltage. Their center–surround organization compares regions, so chromatic and spatial structure are already intertwined. Near the central retina, the midget pathway can oppose an L- or M-dominated center to a surround containing other cone input. The same circuitry can carry information useful for fine spatial structure and for chromatic contrast; these are not necessarily separate wires.
A distinct S-cone route illustrates the anatomical specificity. Dennis M. Dacey and colleagues combined physiological characterization with identification of ganglion-cell morphology in primate retina, finding that the S-ON opponent response belonged to a small bistratified ganglion-cell type rather than simply another midget cell.[ref] This supports parallel circuits with different cone combinations. Calling the pathway “blue-on” is shorthand for its stimulus response, not proof that the cell itself produces a unique blue experience.
At a simplified computational level, an achromatic-like combination and two chromatic combinations can be represented as
y0​=cL​+cM​,y1​=cL​−cM​,y2​=cS​−21​(cL​+cM​).
(1)
This invertible three-channel opponent transform reorganizes three coordinates rather than creating additional independent spectral information. Their matrix has determinant −2, so the transform is invertible: cL​=(y0​+y1​)/2, cM​=(y0​−y1​)/2 and cS​=y2​+y0​/2. Actual weights depend on cell, location, adaptation and stimulus conditions. This algebra is an illustrative opponent transform, not a fitted retinal wiring diagram or a complete perceptual color space.
The lateral geniculate nucleus preserves several parallel retinal pathways while also participating in cortical interactions. A. M. Derrington and colleagues studied macaque geniculate cells with gratings whose luminance and chromatic modulation could be varied independently on a calibrated color television display. Finding directions that changed cone stimulation without driving a cell constrained its linear cone weights.[ref] For a nonzero linear weight vector, the zero-response contrasts obey r=wTc=0, defining a chromatic null plane. A null response therefore tests a relation between inputs, rather than merely assigning the cell a preferred color name. The resulting opponent directions need not coincide with human unique hues.
Cortical processing adds more complex spatial comparisons. Bevil R. Conway mapped cone inputs to color-responsive neurons in alert macaque V1 using cone-isolating spots and eye-position-corrected reverse correlation. Some cells combined chromatic opponency with opposite tuning in adjacent spatial subregions.[ref] Such double-opponent cells can respond strongly to a chromatic boundary while responding less to a spatially uniform change. This offers a mechanism useful for separating surface differences from shared illumination changes. It does not establish that one V1 population alone solves color constancy under arbitrary scenes.
Further processing must relate color to form, illumination, material and the current task. The same cone contrasts can be interpreted differently in a shadow, highlight or translucent object. Surface color judgments, discrimination thresholds and color naming therefore constrain different computations. Feedback and attention can influence which information is selected, while learning can alter how a signal is interpreted or normalized. A lesion or imaging response can constrain contributions to those tasks, but an area responsive to color is not automatically an isolated “color center.”
The strongest picture is consequently a hierarchy of interacting computations: cone-specific signals, opponent and spatial filtering, combinations tuned to richer image structure, and object-level judgments. Trichromatic sampling limits spectral information; later processing determines how that information supports boundaries, stable surface appearance and behavior.

Sensitivity adjustments occur at several stages and over several timescales. They reveal adaptive gain control and flexible reference points, but no single aftereffect uniquely identifies one anatomical site. Changes in threshold, apparent hue, an achromatic setting and a receptor current must be distinguished before explaining them with one adaptation mechanism.
At the receptor level, a finite photocurrent range has to represent environments with very different mean light levels. Julie L. Schnapf and colleagues measured membrane currents from individual macaque cone outer segments. Responses to a maintained light step had an initial component followed by a smaller steady response, and a steady background reduced sensitivity to an added weak flash. This desensitization was distinguishable from loss of pigment through bleaching.[ref] Direct recording establishes that some gain adjustment occurs before any color name, decision or cortical object representation. The preparation is macaque tissue, and a measured cone current is not identical to a human appearance judgment.
An idealized normalization model illustrates the benefit. If a mechanism's incremental gain is proportional to 1/(I+I0​), maintaining a fixed response increment requires ΔI∝I+I0​. At high backgrounds this gives approximately constant fractional contrast ΔI/I, a Weber-like regime. It should not be confused with a universal law across all intensities. Under a known-background Poisson counting model with mean N, the noise standard deviation is N​; a small increment has signal-to-noise ratio approximately ΔN/N​. A fixed signal-to-noise criterion then gives fractional threshold proportional to N−1/2 instead. The two predictions derive from different assumptions about what limits performance.
Mean adaptation and contrast adaptation also differ. A diagonal cone-gain model replaces Qj​ by gj​Qj​, with gains related to recent stimulation. It can reduce the effect of a persistent chromatic shift in the illuminant. If the signal changes approximately as Qj′​=kj​Qj​, choosing gj′​=gj​/kj​ cancels that change. This is exact only for the assumed channelwise scaling; real spectral illuminant changes need not transform every surface's catches by the same three factors.
To test adjustments beyond mean cone excitation, experiments can modulate color around an unchanged time average. John Krauskopf and colleagues found direction-selective threshold elevation after temporal chromatic or luminance modulation, supporting separable adaptable mechanisms for particular directions near threshold.[ref] Michael A. Webster and colleagues then measured appearance after adaptation to modulation along different color-space directions. A color monitor, a modulated adapting field and asymmetric appearance matches allowed testing effects beyond detection thresholds. Appearance shifted away from the adapting direction, including directions intermediate to the simplest cardinal color directions.[ref] Thus a three-independent-channel account sufficient for some threshold results was not sufficient for all suprathreshold appearance changes. Constant mean catches constrain a simple mean-receptor explanation, but do not prove that all receptor nonlinearities have been removed.
Longer experience changes reference points too. Jay Neitz and colleagues altered chromatic experience with colored filters over days and measured unique yellow settings. Shifts persisted for one to two weeks after filter use ended.[ref] Persistence after removing the immediate adapting stimulus demonstrates more than an instantaneous receptor response. It supports experience-dependent normalization without showing that a color category or verbal label has been rewritten. Any interocular transfer must also be measured explicitly; duration alone does not identify cortex.
A natural perturbation extends the range to months. Peter B. Delahunt and colleagues followed achromatic settings before and after removal of cataractous lenses. Removal greatly changed short-wavelength input; settings continued to readjust over roughly three months, whereas chromatic discrimination recovered much faster.[ref] An achromatic setting compensates for a perceived tint, so its physical shift should not be mistaken for a same-direction tint in appearance. The findings indicate distinct adjustment timescales. They are observational evidence with a changing optical input, not a randomized isolation of one neural site or a general treatment recommendation.
These studies support a multilevel experimental strategy. Measure receptor responses, detection thresholds and appearance separately; manipulate mean and contrast independently; test spatial specificity, interocular transfer and recovery. Control exposure, eye position, stimulus calibration, response criterion and learning. A loss of sensitivity can reflect adaptation that preserves dynamic range rather than damage, and stable appearance can coexist with changed receptor inputs. Conversely, perfect compensation is not guaranteed.
Adaptation is a family of adjustments serving different constraints. Its most informative use is to distinguish which signal statistics, stages and reference points change, rather than treating every aftereffect as evidence for one fatigued color channel.

Photoreceptors determine which optical differences are available for vision, with what noise and spatial–temporal precision. They strongly constrain light, color and material perception without uniquely determining the eventual percept. Later processing can exploit context and action, but cannot recover arbitrary information already lost in receptor sampling.
For cone class j, a simplified expected quantum catch over one sampling interval is
Qj​=∫N(λ)aj​(λ)dλ,
(2)
where N is the incident photon-number spectrum at the receptor, including ocular filtering, and aj​ effective absorption probability. An energy spectrum requires conversion by photon energy hc/λ before this counting interpretation. The integral is many-to-one: equal catches do not imply equal spectra. The principle of univariance means that an individual receptor does not independently report photon wavelength and number.
Multiple receptor classes provide comparisons, but ordinary cone-mediated trichromacy still samples a spectral distribution with only three class responses at a specified location and time. In a discretized model, Q=An with three rows and more than three spectral bins. The matrix has a nonzero kernel. Around a strictly positive spectrum, a sufficiently small perturbation in that kernel preserves nonnegative intensities while leaving all three catches unchanged. This derives the possibility of metamers; it does not mean all real-world spectral differences are unobservable under every condition. Altering illumination, observer or spatial–temporal context can break a particular match.
Sensitivity also has a photon budget. In an ideal Poisson model, mean catch N has variance N, so relative counting uncertainty is 1/N​. Gathering more photons reduces this uncertainty but requires a larger effective collecting area or more integration time. Temporal averaging can obscure rapid change, while spatial pooling can sacrifice fine detail. Receptor and downstream noise add limitations beyond shot noise. Rods support low-light detection with different sensitivity and pooling from cone cells; if only one receptor class provides usable signals, wavelength–intensity ambiguity cannot be resolved by comparisons within that class. Mesopic vision, where rods and cone cells can both contribute, is not captured by declaring all dim-light vision achromatic.
The receptor array is also uneven. Christine A. Curcio and colleagues mapped rods and cone cells in whole-mounted human retinas using microscopic counts. Cone density was highest centrally and fell strongly with eccentricity, while the rod distribution differed and the central foveal region was rod-free.[ref] Thus acuity and sensitivity should not be treated as uniform over the visual field. Optical blur and downstream sampling constrain performance alongside receptor spacing. The S-cone mosaic is sparser than the combined L/M mosaic, so equal spatial detail cannot simply be assumed for all chromatic signals.
A regular one-dimensional model of photoreceptor sampling makes one limitation explicit. Sampling a sinusoid at spacing d gives identical samples for frequencies f and f+k/d, because the added phase at sample n is 2πkn. Restricting a real signal to frequencies below 1/(2d) avoids this basic aliasing ambiguity. Real retinal mosaics are two-dimensional, irregular and optically filtered, so this is a benchmark rather than a literal universal retinal cutoff. Fine material texture can consequently disappear or alias even when coarser color and shape remain visible.
Individual variation changes the sampling system itself. Heidi Hofer and colleagues' adaptive-optics measurements showed large L:M cone-ratio variation among observers passing normal color tests.[ref] Gabriele Jordan and colleagues found behavioral evidence consistent with an extra chromatic dimension in one of a selected set of carriers with potential extra pigment diversity, rather than in every carrier.[ref] These findings separate receptor availability from downstream use: receptor counts and pigment variants constrain possibilities without directly specifying appearance or functional dimensionality.
Material perception adds an inverse problem. For a matte surface under a single simplified illuminant, the reflected photon spectrum is proportional to E(λ)R(λ), so catches mix illumination and surface reflectance. The same catches can therefore arise from different surfaces, even before considering three-channel spectral compression. Specular reflection, subsurface scattering, translucency and fluorescence introduce further dependencies on direction, geometry or wavelength conversion. Three cone values cannot independently label every physical material property.
Nevertheless, humans can exploit cues across space, movement and time. Highlights change with viewpoint; texture and shading constrain shape; surrounding surfaces provide illumination evidence. Such information can reduce ambiguity under useful assumptions, while metamerism and sampling limits remain. Receptor properties define an information bottleneck and a set of trade-offs. Successful material recognition depends on combining what survives that bottleneck with contextual constraints, not on receptors directly measuring “wood,” “metal” or intrinsic surface color.

L, M and S describe the relative spectral sensitivities of cone classes. Calling them red, green and blue confuses receptor sampling with the color appearance constructed from comparisons and context. The shorthand also wrongly suggests three narrow, nonoverlapping detectors or the primaries of a display.
First, each class has a broad sensitivity curve. A long-wavelength-sensitive cone responds to many wavelengths, and its sensitivity maximum is not a unique red stimulus. L- and M-cone sensitivities overlap extensively, while S cone cells are relatively more sensitive at shorter wavelengths. Light at one wavelength can therefore excite more than one class. Andrew Stockman and colleagues derived L- and M-cone sensitivity estimates using color-matching data and measurements from observers of known pigment genotype. Their analysis emphasizes broad overlap and the need for isolation procedures to measure one class without contamination from others.[ref] Exact curves depend on pigment variant and optical filtering, so the letters denote relative classes rather than three invariant color labels.
Second, the principle of univariance prevents one cone from identifying hue. For a monochromatic stimulus, let mean absorption be Q=Ia(λ). Whenever a(λ1​) and a(λ2​) are positive, intensities satisfying I2​=I1​a(λ1​)/a(λ2​) give the same Q. The single receptor cannot distinguish those two inputs on absorption count alone. Calling that receptor a red detector would assign a color judgment to a signal that cannot even uniquely distinguish the two wavelengths. It remains true that spectral sensitivity constrains the comparison; univariance does not mean all receptor types are identical.
Third, perceptual color requires combinations. Comparing L and M signals and comparing S with L+M supplies chromatic information; summing appropriately weighted signals contributes to luminance-related processing. An L-cone increment can be part of several stimulus changes depending on what happens to M and S and on the adapted background. Neither an L response nor a raw L:M ratio by itself specifies a context-independent color word. The retinal and cortical opponent circuitry demonstrated by Dennis M. Dacey and Bevil R. Conway provides mechanisms for comparisons, rather than a relabelling of three cone outputs as three sensations.[ref][ref]
Fourth, “blue” or “red” need not identify a single wavelength. Spectrally different lights can be metamers, and a purple appearance can arise from a mixture rather than a monochromatic wavelength. Conversely, the same physical spectrum can look different against another surround or after adaptation. Cone labels cannot replace the distinction between emitted or reflected spectrum, receptor catch, opponent representation and reported hue.
Fifth, the display analogy is backwards. An RGB screen emits mixtures from three selected primary spectra. The cone cells receive and integrate the resulting spectrum; their response curves are not identical to those primary spectra. If primary intensities are pk​ and cone sensitivities to each primary form matrix B, then Q=Bp. Off-diagonal terms are generally nonzero because each primary can excite several cone classes. If B is nonsingular, an intended catch change can be computed through B−1 within the display's feasible gamut. It does not follow that every requested catch vector is realizable with nonnegative bounded primary intensities. Calibration and observer differences therefore matter in cone-isolating experiments.
Finally, color terms themselves vary across languages and tasks, whereas receptor class is a biological classification. Receptor genes, color matches, discrimination and linguistic categorization are related but distinct measurements. The labels L, M and S prevent an unwarranted assumption that one anatomical cell class directly instantiates one learned word or unique subjective hue.
The useful statement is that trichromatic vision starts with three broad, overlapping receptor classes and extracts chromatic information by comparison. “Red, green and blue cone cells” conceals both the information loss at each receptor and the transformations needed to see color.

The research challenge is to retain causal control while measuring appearance under the coupled spectral, spatial and temporal variations of actual scenes. Simply replacing a flat patch with a photograph does not guarantee that either the stimulus or the task is ecologically informative.
A display's RGB values do not specify the spectrum reaching a participant's cone cells. Primary spectra, viewing geometry, calibration, luminance range and the observer's lens and pigment sensitivities matter. A photograph can clip highlights, alter white balance and compress dynamic range; a display cannot necessarily reproduce the original spectrum, brightness or angular light field. A researcher studying spectral constancy should measure radiance and illuminants or specify the limits of a display simulation, rather than treating an uncalibrated image as physical ground truth.
Real materials also vary in more than diffuse reflectance. Shape, roughness, specularity, translucency and surrounding illumination jointly determine an image. Changing one rendering parameter can unintentionally change highlight area, contrast and apparent shape. Conversely, holding an image statistic constant can produce an unnatural stimulus. Experiments need independent manipulation of candidate causes and a clear statement of which physical scene properties are represented by the apparatus.
James M. Kraft and David H. Brainard demonstrated a productive compromise with a chamber of actual surfaces. They independently controlled illumination and surface reflectances and used a projection colorimeter to let observers adjust a test patch to appear achromatic. Manipulations could hold candidate image statistics approximately fixed while changing the illumination. The results rejected explanations based solely on local adaptation, the image mean or the most intense region.[ref] The achievement was not realism alone: it was enough realism combined with manipulations that made competing mechanisms disagree. These results do not show that local adaptation never contributes.
For material appearance, Roland W. Fleming and colleagues used rendered objects illuminated by natural illumination distributions and systematically altered those distributions. Reflectance matching was more reliable under realistic illumination statistics than under some altered statistics.[ref] This makes illumination an experimental variable, not merely a nuisance background. It also warns that a model tested only under a small set of studio lights can exploit cues unavailable or misleading elsewhere.
The dependent variable needs equal care. Selecting the same paint, matching apparent color, adjusting an achromatic point, detecting an illuminant change and naming a material do not measure the same outcome. A person may correctly infer that two patches are the same material while still noticing an appearance difference. Instructions, response format, adaptation time and available context can alter the task. Eye and head movements change retinal exposure, so fixation constraints should be chosen deliberately and their cost to natural viewing acknowledged.
A strong program therefore combines calibrated simplified stimuli, controlled real scenes, validated rendering and tests on previously unseen objects and illumination. It should sample more than one observer and record relevant visual variation, report both accuracy and uncertainty, and test model generalization across scenes rather than only across random frames of the same scene. Proposed interventions can vary geometry while preserving spectral input, or alter illumination while suppressing a particular cue. Their outcomes must be measured, not assumed. The goal is interpretable generalization: experimental control sufficient to distinguish mechanisms and scene diversity sufficient to test whether those mechanisms remain useful.

The visual system must infer relatively stable object properties from images jointly determined by illumination, shape, material and viewpoint. The problem is intrinsically ambiguous; robust performance exploits several constraints and tolerates uncertainty rather than perfectly recovering a unique physical scene from every image.
Even a diffuse model contains a surface-illuminant ambiguity. Ignoring a fixed geometric factor, N(λ)=E(λ)R(λ). For any positive function g that leaves the resulting reflectance physically admissible, define E′=Eg and R′=R/g. Then E′R′=ER, so even an observer measuring the complete outgoing spectrum could not distinguish those two explanations from that surface alone. Three cone catches introduce further spectral metamerism. Thus perfect color constancy cannot be obtained by a universal local inversion of three numbers.
For directional optical reflection, an image sample instead depends on an integral such as
Lo​(λ,ωo​)=∫Ω+​fr​(λ,ωi​,ωo​)Li​(λ,ωi​)cosθi​dωi​.
(3)
The expression sums contributions from incident directions, weighted by the bidirectional reflectance distribution function and projected area. It assumes a local, nonfluorescent optical reflection model and omits emission. A glossy surface therefore reflects aspects of its environment, and moving the observer changes the sampled outgoing direction even if the material is unchanged. Subsurface transport and transmission require further terms or models.
The system can constrain illumination by comparing surfaces, spatial boundaries and changes over time, while using shape and scene layout to distinguish a shadow from a pigment boundary. Cone-gain adjustment can approximately compensate shared spectral changes but cannot solve every mixture of differently colored lights, interreflections and specular components. A spatial average is unreliable if a scene is genuinely dominated by one colored material. A bright region might be a lamp, a highlight or a high-reflectance object, so “assume the brightest point is white” is not a universal solution.
Material judgments require distinguishing diffuse shading from highlights and transparency or translucency cues. Isamu Motoyoshi and colleagues showed that manipulating image statistics, including luminance-distribution skewness, could influence apparent gloss and lightness in their surface-image conditions.[ref] Such regularities offer efficient cues. They cannot uniquely specify a material: a histogram-preserving spatial permutation changes spatial organization while retaining the luminance histogram and all its moments. That mathematical ambiguity motivates experiments separating histogram cues from geometric consistency; it does not, by itself, predict every appearance judgment after scrambling.
Juno Kim and colleagues directly tested the relation between highlights and diffuse shading. Manipulating their positional and orientation congruence reduced perceived gloss, including when simple proximity to broad luminance maxima was controlled.[ref] The visual system therefore uses relationships among image structures, not merely the presence of a few bright pixels. The conclusion concerns the tested images and judgments, rather than a claim that one cue explains every shiny or transparent material.
Temporal information and active viewing help further. A highlight can move differently from a surface marking; multiple views constrain geometry, while familiar object structure and expected illumination supply priors. These strategies can fail under unusual lighting or deliberately misleading images. The system must also decide which differences matter: recognizing a material category, judging gloss and estimating spectral reflectance are related but separable goals.
Robustness comes from combining adaptation, spatial and temporal relations, shape information and learned scene constraints. It does not remove the physical ambiguities, and performance should be judged against a specified task rather than an impossible demand for exact recovery in every scene.

Language is not necessary for the basic capacity to discriminate or organize colors, but it can influence color judgments, attention and memory in particular tasks. The evidence supports neither the claim that words create all color experience nor the claim that language is irrelevant whenever the stimuli are visible.
The starting distinctions are essential. A receptor sensitivity, a color-matching relation, a discrimination threshold, a category boundary and a color name are different measurements. Molecular differences can change available cone signals, while linguistic experience can change how available information is grouped or used. Finding different words for two samples does not show that one group cannot discriminate them. Finding the same matching relation does not establish identical subjective appearance or identical naming.
The biological basis imposes constraints independent of a person's vocabulary. Three cone classes provide overlapping spectral measurements, and later circuits compare those measurements. Altered receptor signals or adaptation can change performance without requiring a new word. Conversely, changing a response strategy can change performance while leaving receptor input intact. The central empirical question is therefore which stage and task language influences, not whether one can name a color without knowing its name.
Prelinguistic evidence rules out a strict requirement for color words. Alice E. Skelton and colleagues used infants' recognition-memory responses to sample relations among colors across the hue circle. Infants' categorical distinctions showed organization related to common divisions in adult color lexicons and to early visual mechanisms.[ref] Infants did not need to produce the relevant color terms to show those distinctions. The inference is not that newborns possess an immutable adult lexicon: the infants already had visual experience, and looking-time measures require controls for discriminability, novelty and attention. The title's biological interpretation should not be strengthened into proof that experience plays no role.
Cross-language studies also challenge a rigid universal partition. Debi Roberson and colleagues compared English speakers with Berinmo speakers using naming, recognition and category-learning tasks. Effects of linguistic category boundaries appeared for the categories used by each group, and response-bias controls weakened a simple claim that universally focal colors always enjoy a memory advantage.[ref] The article's historical title is retained as a citation, not adopted as a description of people. The comparison shows that category use and memory cannot be reduced to one vocabulary-independent set of preferred labels. Because language group covaries with cultural and visual experience, it does not isolate vocabulary as the sole cause.
A more direct manipulation was used by Jonathan Winawer and colleagues. Russian has conventional basic terms distinguishing lighter and darker blues that English usually groups under “blue.” Participants saw a target square and two alternatives together and selected the matching alternative. Russian speakers showed a speed advantage for comparisons crossing their linguistic boundary. A concurrent verbal task removed that advantage, while a spatial interference task did not produce the same pattern.[ref] The simultaneous display reduces the long-term color-memory explanation, and the interference manipulation implicates access to language-related processes during performance. It does not establish a new receptor limit, a complete inability to discriminate within a category, or a necessary change in private color qualia. Concurrent tasks can affect more than one processing stage.
These results motivate separating sensitivity from response facilitation. A faster match can reflect attentional selection, category-assisted comparison or decision formation. To claim changed sensory resolution, an experiment should measure psychometric functions over controlled stimulus separations, fit location, slope and lapse parameters, and test whether any effect persists with nonverbal responses and reversed response mappings. Equal physical distance in a standard color space does not automatically ensure equal discriminability for every participant; individual thresholds and adaptation should be measured where they matter.
A stronger causal program can combine within-person interference, language-learning interventions and longitudinal or bilingual comparisons, while matching exposure and task difficulty. Testing transfer to new samples distinguishes memorized stimulus labels from a changed category rule. Comparing immediate discrimination, delayed recognition, naming and appearance matches identifies whether an effect generalizes across measurement levels. These are proposed strategies rather than invented experimental outcomes. No test based only on verbal descriptions can directly establish equality or inequality of another observer's subjective experience.
The seemingly opposing findings can therefore coexist. Biology and early experience can produce color organization before fluent naming, while learned labels subsequently guide the use of that organization. Color vision does not depend on language for its existence; linguistic resources can nevertheless alter how people attend to, classify, remember and rapidly compare colors under specified conditions.
