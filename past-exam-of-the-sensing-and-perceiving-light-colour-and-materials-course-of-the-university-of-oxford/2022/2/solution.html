Sensitivity adjustments occur at several stages and over several timescales. They reveal adaptive gain control and flexible reference points, but no single aftereffect uniquely identifies one anatomical site. Changes in threshold, apparent hue, an achromatic setting and a receptor current must be distinguished before explaining them with one adaptation mechanism.
At the receptor level, a finite photocurrent range has to represent environments with very different mean light levels. Julie L. Schnapf and colleagues measured membrane currents from individual macaque cone outer segments. Responses to a maintained light step had an initial component followed by a smaller steady response, and a steady background reduced sensitivity to an added weak flash. This desensitization was distinguishable from loss of pigment through bleaching.[ref] Direct recording establishes that some gain adjustment occurs before any color name, decision or cortical object representation. The preparation is macaque tissue, and a measured cone current is not identical to a human appearance judgment.
An idealized normalization model illustrates the benefit. If a mechanism's incremental gain is proportional to 1/(I+I0​), maintaining a fixed response increment requires ΔI∝I+I0​. At high backgrounds this gives approximately constant fractional contrast ΔI/I, a Weber-like regime. It should not be confused with a universal law across all intensities. Under a known-background Poisson counting model with mean N, the noise standard deviation is N​; a small increment has signal-to-noise ratio approximately ΔN/N​. A fixed signal-to-noise criterion then gives fractional threshold proportional to N−1/2 instead. The two predictions derive from different assumptions about what limits performance.
Mean adaptation and contrast adaptation also differ. A diagonal cone-gain model replaces Qj​ by gj​Qj​, with gains related to recent stimulation. It can reduce the effect of a persistent chromatic shift in the illuminant. If the signal changes approximately as Qj′​=kj​Qj​, choosing gj′​=gj​/kj​ cancels that change. This is exact only for the assumed channelwise scaling; real spectral illuminant changes need not transform every surface's catches by the same three factors.
To test adjustments beyond mean cone excitation, experiments can modulate color around an unchanged time average. John Krauskopf and colleagues found direction-selective threshold elevation after temporal chromatic or luminance modulation, supporting separable adaptable mechanisms for particular directions near threshold.[ref] Michael A. Webster and colleagues then measured appearance after adaptation to modulation along different color-space directions. A color monitor, a modulated adapting field and asymmetric appearance matches allowed testing effects beyond detection thresholds. Appearance shifted away from the adapting direction, including directions intermediate to the simplest cardinal color directions.[ref] Thus a three-independent-channel account sufficient for some threshold results was not sufficient for all suprathreshold appearance changes. Constant mean catches constrain a simple mean-receptor explanation, but do not prove that all receptor nonlinearities have been removed.
Longer experience changes reference points too. Jay Neitz and colleagues altered chromatic experience with colored filters over days and measured unique yellow settings. Shifts persisted for one to two weeks after filter use ended.[ref] Persistence after removing the immediate adapting stimulus demonstrates more than an instantaneous receptor response. It supports experience-dependent normalization without showing that a color category or verbal label has been rewritten. Any interocular transfer must also be measured explicitly; duration alone does not identify cortex.
A natural perturbation extends the range to months. Peter B. Delahunt and colleagues followed achromatic settings before and after removal of cataractous lenses. Removal greatly changed short-wavelength input; settings continued to readjust over roughly three months, whereas chromatic discrimination recovered much faster.[ref] An achromatic setting compensates for a perceived tint, so its physical shift should not be mistaken for a same-direction tint in appearance. The findings indicate distinct adjustment timescales. They are observational evidence with a changing optical input, not a randomized isolation of one neural site or a general treatment recommendation.
These studies support a multilevel experimental strategy. Measure receptor responses, detection thresholds and appearance separately; manipulate mean and contrast independently; test spatial specificity, interocular transfer and recovery. Control exposure, eye position, stimulus calibration, response criterion and learning. A loss of sensitivity can reflect adaptation that preserves dynamic range rather than damage, and stable appearance can coexist with changed receptor inputs. Conversely, perfect compensation is not guaranteed.
Adaptation is a family of adjustments serving different constraints. Its most informative use is to distinguish which signal statistics, stages and reference points change, rather than treating every aftereffect as evidence for one fatigued color channel.
