Finite-population radioactive decay (N(t)∼Binomial(N0​,e−λt))

 Home Physics Nuclear physics Radioactive decay
OurBigBook.com Words: 221
Take N0​ independently decaying atomic nuclei, each with lifetime Ti​ drawn from an exponential distribution of rate λ. With no replenishment, the survivor count is
N(t)=∑i=1N0​​1{Ti​>t}​,p(t)=e−λt.
(15)
Each indicator variable has success probability p(t), so the binomial distribution gives
E[N(t)]=N0​p(t),Var(N(t))=N0​p(t)(1−p(t)).​
(16)
The smooth population law is the expected value N(t)=E[N(t)], satisfying N′=−λN. One realization instead loses whole atomic nuclei at discrete times. An observed count above the smooth curve need not contradict the mathematical model.
For an original chosen example, let N0​=1000 and half-life 3h. Then λ=log2/(3h). After 9h, the survival probability is 1/8, giving
E[N(9h)]=125,SD(N(9h))=109.375​≃10.46.​
(17)
The standard deviation describes variation between repeated populations governed by these assumptions. It is not an uncertainty interval for a noiseless deterministic curve.
The half-life and the mean lifetime answer different questions:
t1/2​=λlog2​,E[Ti​]=λ1​.
(18)
The first is the median of one lifetime, or the time when half the population survives on average. The second is the lifetime's expected value. In this example they are 3h and approximately 4.33h.
