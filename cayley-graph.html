Cayley graph

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Geometric group theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 12
A Cayley graph turns multiplication in a group into travel: the chosen generators of a group are the allowed one-step moves, and a word over an alphabet becomes a route. This lets us study a finitely generated group using graph distances, closed balls, and geodesics, rather than only equations between products.
Let G be a group with a finite generating set of a group S satisfying S=S−1 and 1∈/S. Such a symmetric subset of a group includes the inverse element of every allowed move. The Cayley graph CayS​(G) has vertex set G and joins g to gs for each s∈S. We choose right multiplication for these moves. Reversing the move uses s−1, so for graph distance we regard g→gs and gs→g as the two orientations of one undirected edge. An involution therefore gives one edge, not two parallel edges. Each edge has length one, regardless of its length in a drawing.[ref]
A sequence s1​,…,sn​ labels the walk
g,gs1​,gs1​s2​,…,gs1​⋯sn​.
(13)
It ends at h exactly when g−1h=s1​⋯sn​. Thus the Cayley graph is a connected graph precisely because S generates G, and its graph distance on vertices is the word metric. Left multiplication by a sends the edge from g to gs to the edge from ag to (ag)s, preserving its generator label: this is a group action by graph automorphisms and isometries. Viewing groups as symmetries of geometric objects is the motivation of Clay and Margalit's introduction.[ref]
The useful geometric object includes its allowed moves, not just its drawing. Cayley graphs of the integers show how changing the moves adds shortcuts; word metric balls in the integer lattice show how the same group can acquire visibly different closed balls. Meier's book develops this passage from group operations to graphs as its central motivation.[ref]
