Kastler-Brossel optical pumping experiments

 Home Physics Atomic physics Atomic measurement methods Optical pumping
OurBigBook.com Words: 265
Alfred Kastler's 1966 lecture recalls his 1950 proposal and distinguishes the early 23Na atomic beam work from later sealed-vapour experiments. Its Fig. 5 shows subsequent sodium radio wave resonances, including multiple-photon transitions.[ref]
The 1952 demonstration by Jean Brossel, Alfred Kastler and Jacques Winter used a sodium atomic beam, lamp light with circular polarization and fluorescence readout. Jean Brossel was a central experimental contributor; Alfred Kastler received the 1966 Nobel Prize in Physics alone.[ref][ref]
The lecture also describes a 199Hg experiment: a pumping beam with circular polarization prepares the vapour, while a perpendicular probe converts transverse magnetic dipole moment Larmor precession into modulated absorption at a photodetector. Interrupting pump and RF together exposes decaying free Larmor precession. Its envelope measures transverse relaxation under that model; it is distinct from the sodium atomic beam fluorescence signal.[ref]
MIT's later rubidium experiment pumps natural 85Rb/87Rb with a discharge lamp filtered to D1 (5S1/2​→5P1/2​, near 795 nm). A polarizer and quarter-wave plate precede a heated rubidium/neon cell. Helmholtz coils set a weak static magnetic field. Separate transverse RF mixing increases absorption, measured by photodiode transmission.[ref]
Compare resonant and detuned RF with the optical beam fixed, then change the calibrated static magnetic field: a true magnetic sublevel resonance should move with its Zeeman splitting. Lamp drift, stray magnetic fields, imperfect circular polarization, optical attenuation and RF power broadening can change the observed signal. Consequently, a transmission dip alone does not determine every sublevel population. These are measurement-design considerations, not additional reported historical observations.
