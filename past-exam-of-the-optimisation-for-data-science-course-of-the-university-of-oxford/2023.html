Put z=x−α∇f(x) and τ=αλ. The proximal operator for the L1 norm separates into independent scalar minimization problems:
pi​=argminu∈R​{τ∣u∣+21​(u−zi​)2}.
(1)
The subgradient optimality condition is 0∈pi​−zi​+τ∂∣pi​∣. By the subgradient of the absolute value, a positive solution must satisfy pi​=zi​−τ>0, and a negative solution must satisfy pi​=zi​+τ<0. At zero, the subgradient optimality condition holds exactly when ∣zi​∣≤τ. The squared term makes the objective function a strictly convex function, so these cases give its unique minimiser.
Thus the proximal operator is the soft-thresholding operator
Sτ​(t)=⎩⎨⎧​t−τ,0,t+τ,​t>τ,∣t∣≤τ,t<−τ,​pi​=Sτ​(zi​).
(2)
Consequently the requested proximal gradient mapping, rather than just the proximal operator, is
[Gα​(x)]i​=αxi​−Sαλ​(xi​−α∂i​f(x))​,i=1,…,n.​
(3)
The corresponding proximal gradient method update is xi+​=Sαλ​(xi​−α∂i​f(x)). Small coordinates are set exactly to zero. When λ=0, the soft-thresholding operator is the identity and the proximal gradient mapping reduces to ∇f(x).

Again write z=x−α∇f(x) and τ=αλ, and let zℓ​ denote its ℓth block of k coordinates. The block mixed norm in this part makes the proximal operator separate over blocks. For a single block, its minimiser solves
minu∈Rk​{τ∥u∥+21​∥u−zℓ​∥2}.
(4)
At a nonzero u, the gradient of the Euclidean norm is u/∥u∥. The subgradient optimality condition therefore becomes
zℓ​=u+τ∥u∥u​.
(5)
Thus u points in the direction of zℓ​ and ∥zℓ​∥=∥u∥+τ. This yields u=(1−τ/∥zℓ​∥)zℓ​ when ∥zℓ​∥>τ.
At zero, the subdifferential of the Euclidean norm is the closed Euclidean unit ball: the subgradient inequality is qTw≤∥w∥ for every w, which is equivalent to ∥q∥≤1 by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the choice w=q. Hence zero solves the subgradient optimality condition exactly when ∥zℓ​∥≤τ. This also covers zℓ​=0 and avoids division by zero.
The resulting radial soft thresholding is
Tτ​(zℓ​)=⎩⎨⎧​0,(1−∥zℓ​∥τ​)zℓ​,​∥zℓ​∥≤τ,∥zℓ​∥>τ.​
(6)
If yℓ​ is the corresponding block of x, the required proximal gradient mapping is
[Gα​(x)]ℓ​=αyℓ​−Tαλ​(zℓ​)​,zℓ​=yℓ​−α[∇f(x)]ℓ​.​
(7)
Whole blocks can become zero, while nonzero blocks retain their direction after the gradient step. For a block of size one, radial soft thresholding agrees with the soft-thresholding operator in part (a).

First use the supplied inequality (1) with z=xj:
ϕ(xj+1)≤ϕ(xj)−2L1​∥gj​∥2≤ϕ(xj).
(8)
Thus the objective function values, and hence the errors δj​, are nonincreasing. Next use (1) with z=x∗:
δj+1​≤gjT​(xj−x∗)−2L1​∥gj​∥2.
(9)
Expansion of the squared Euclidean norm gives
rj+1​=rj​−L2​gjT​(xj−x∗)+L21​∥gj​∥2,
(10)
so the last bound is exactly δj+1​≤L(rj​−rj+1​)/2.
The telescoping sum over j=0,…,k−1 therefore gives
∑j=0k−1​δj+1​≤2L​(r0​−rk​)≤2L​r0​.
(11)

The bound holds for the last iterate. The monotonicity of the objective function values is what converts an averaged estimate into this last-iterate estimate.

Fix x, and write p=x−αGα​(x) and g=Gα​(x). The supplied subgradient relation gives v=vα​∈λ∂ψ(p) with g=∇f(x)+v. The evaluation point in ∇f is x, whereas v is a subgradient at the new point p.
Apply the subgradient inequality at p, with comparison point x:
λψ(p)−λψ(x)≤vT(p−x)=−αvTg.
(13)
The descent lemma for the L-Lipschitz gradient of f gives
f(p)−f(x)≤−α∇f(x)Tg+2Lα2​∥g∥2.
(14)
Adding these estimates proves the stronger, explicit inequality
ϕ(p)−ϕ(x)≤−α(1−2Lα​)∥Gα​(x)∥2.​
(15)
In particular, the objective function decreases for 0<α<2/L whenever the proximal gradient mapping is nonzero.
To justify the requested big O notation, we must check that g, which depends on α, stays bounded. Suppose ∂(λψ)(x) is nonempty, and choose a fixed w in this subdifferential. This holds for a finite-valued convex function on Rn, including both norms in parts (a) and (b). By monotonicity of a convex subdifferential,
(v−w)T(p−x)≥0.
(16)
Substituting p−x=−αg and v=g−∇f(x), and then using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, yields
∥g∥2≤(∇f(x)+w)Tg≤∥∇f(x)+w∥∥g∥.
(17)
Thus ∥g∥≤Cx​:=∥∇f(x)+w∥, uniformly in α>0. This local boundedness of the proximal gradient mapping makes the remainder at most LCx2​α2/2, so
ϕ(x−αGα​(x))−ϕ(x)≤−α∥∇f(x)+vα​∥2+O(α2).​
(18)
No differentiability of ψ is needed. If an proper extended-real function is allowed as the regularization term, the subdifferential condition at x is a qualification for this uniform remainder; the preceding explicit descent inequality remains the useful statement whenever its quantities are finite.

Let d=y−x and take 0<t≤1. Rearrange the defining inequality for a strongly convex function, applied to x+td:
f(y)≥f(x)+tf(x+td)−f(x)​+2γ​(1−t)∥d∥2.
(19)
Because f is differentiable at x, its directional derivative in direction d is
limt↓0​tf(x+td)−f(x)​=∇f(x)Td.
(20)

f(y)≥f(x)+∇f(x)T(y−x)+2γ​∥y−x∥2.​
(21)
This is the supporting affine function inequality strengthened by a positive squared Euclidean distance. Differentiability at x alone suffices; no derivative at y is used.

For the linear least-squares problem objective function, direct expansion with d=y−x gives
f(y)=21​∥Ax−b+Ad∥2=f(x)+(Ax−b)TAd+21​∥Ad∥2.
(22)
Its gradient is ∇f(x)=AT(Ax−b), so the middle term equals ∇f(x)Td.
Use the supplied thin singular value decomposition A=USVT. Since UTU=In​, the linear map w↦Uw preserves the Euclidean norm. The square matrix V is an orthogonal matrix, so ∥VTd∥=∥d∥. Consequently
∥Ad∥2=∥SVTd∥2=∑i=1n​si,i2​(VTd)i2​≥smin2​∑i=1n​(VTd)i2​=smin2​∥d∥2.
(23)
Substitution into the expansion proves
f(y)≥f(x)+∇f(x)T(y−x)+2smin2​​∥y−x∥2.​
(24)
Thus f is a strongly convex function with modulus γ=smin2​>0. This modulus is sharp: choose d=Vei​ for an index attaining the smallest singular value. The Hessian matrix is ATA=VS2VT, so the same conclusion says that its smallest eigenvalue is smin2​.

There is a summation-index misprint. On the native PDF, paper page 3, equation (4) has Xk inside a sum indexed by j. The intended telescoping estimate has Xj inside that sum; this is the estimate proved below and used in part (d). The index mismatch is present in the original PDF, rather than being introduced by the transcription.
Take the expectation of the supplied conditional expectation bound (3), using the tower property:
Ef(Xj+1)≤Ef(Xj)−2L1​E∥∇f(Xj)∥2+2LM​.
(25)
Rearranging and forming a telescoping sum gives
2L1​∑j=0k−1​E∥∇f(Xj)∥2≤f(x0)−Ef(Xk)+2LkM​.
(26)
Because x∗ is a global minimizer, f(Xk)≥f(x∗) for every realisation. Hence the corrected equation (4) is
j=0∑k−1​2L1​E∥∇f(Xj)∥2≤f(x0)−f(x∗)+2LkM​.​
(27)
All expectations at a finite iteration are finite here: a deterministic starting point and finitely many choices of the sampled index produce only finitely many possible iterates. The bound controls accumulated squared gradients, with an additive contribution from the stochastic gradient noise floor.

Write Δ0​=f(x0)−f(x∗). Dividing the corrected estimate from part (c) by k/(2L) gives the averaged stationarity bound for stochastic gradient descent
k1​∑j=0k−1​E∥∇f(Xj)∥2≤M+k2LΔ0​​.
(28)
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, first within each expectation and then over the k indices, gives
k1​∑j=0k−1​E∥∇f(Xj)∥≤(k1​∑j=0k−1​E∥∇f(Xj)∥2)1/2.
(29)
Thus
0≤j<kmin​E∥∇f(Xj)∥≤M+k2LΔ0​​​≤M​+k2LΔ0​​​.​
(30)
Equivalently, if J is chosen independently of the run with the discrete uniform distribution on {0,…,k−1}, then E∥∇f(XJ)∥2≤M+2LΔ0​/k. The same bound for the expected Euclidean norm follows by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
The guarantee is averaged or best-iterate stationarity. It does not assert that every last iterate converges, or that the limiting Euclidean norm equals M​. The squared gradient bound has excess O(k−1) above M; the displayed expected Euclidean norm bound is within O(k−1/2) of M​, including the zero-noise case. For fixed M>0, it is sharper:
M+k2LΔ0​​​−M​=M+2LΔ0​/k​+M​2LΔ0​/k​≤kM​LΔ0​​.
(31)
For example, to ensure minj<k​E∥∇f(Xj)∥≤M​+ε with ε>0, it suffices that k≥2LΔ0​/(2M​ε+ε2). The big O notation here concerns these upper bounds, rather than an asserted asymptotic equality for the stochastic gradient noise floor.

E[h∣Xk=x]=m1​∑i=1m​∇fi​(x)=g.
(32)
Write σk2​=E[∥h−g∥2∣Xk=x]≤M. Expanding h=g+(h−g) and using the zero conditional mean of h−g proves the stochastic gradient second-moment identity
E[∥h∥2∣Xk=x]=∥g∥2+σk2​.
(33)
The stochastic gradient descent update is Xk+1=x−h/L. Expanding the squared Euclidean distance and using the preceding identities gives
Dk​:=2L​E[∥Xk−x∗∥2−∥Xk+1−x∗∥2​Xk=x]=gTr−2L1​(∥g∥2+σk2​).
(34)

E[f(Xk+1)∣Xk=x]​≤f(x)−2L1​∥g∥2+2LM​≤f(x∗)+gTr−2L1​∥g∥2+2LM​=f(x∗)+Dk​+2Lσk2​+M​≤f(x∗)+Dk​+LM​.​

The printed distance needs a square. The native PDF, paper page 3, prints ∥x0−x∗∥ in the final bound, but the general conclusion from (5) contains ∥x0−x∗∥2. We first prove the corrected statement and then show that the printed version can fail.
Take the expectation in (5), use the tower property, and sum over j=0,…,k−1. The squared Euclidean distances form a telescoping sum:
∑j=0k−1​(Ef(Xj+1)−f(x∗))≤2L​(∥x0−x∗∥2−E∥Xk−x∗∥2)+LkM​.
(37)
Discard the final nonnegative squared Euclidean distance. The smallest of k numbers is at most their arithmetic mean, so
0≤j<kmin​Ef(Xj+1)−f(x∗)≤2kL​∥x0−x∗∥2+LM​.​
(38)
This is an O(k−1) bound above the stochastic gradient noise floor M/L for the best expected objective function value. By Jensen's inequality, it also bounds Ef(Xˉk)−f(x∗) for the averaged iterate Xˉk=k−1∑j=0k−1​Xj+1, because f is a convex function.
To test the printed version, take one component function, f1​(x)=f(x)=x2/4, with L=1, x∗=0 and x0=10. This convex quadratic function has ∇f(x)=x/2, hence it has a 1-Lipschitz gradient. The stochastic gradient descent update is deterministic, its conditional variance is zero, and the assumed positive bound M=1/10 is valid. At k=1, the new iterate is X1=5, so
f(X1)−f(x∗)=425​>5+101​=2L​∥x0−x∗∥+LM​.
(39)
Thus the printed inequality is false under its stated assumptions. The squared Euclidean distance in the corrected bound resolves the defect.

Fix x and put g=∇B​f(x), the block coordinate gradient. For t≥0, define the one-variable function q(t)=f(x−tg). By the chain rule,
q′(t)=−∇f(x−tg)Tg=−∇B​f(x−tg)Tg.
(40)
The last equality holds because the coordinates of g outside B are zero. The fundamental theorem of calculus and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality therefore give
f(x−αg)−f(x)​=−α∥g∥2−∫0α​(∇B​f(x−tg)−g)Tgdt≤−α∥g∥2+∫0α​∥∇B​f(x−tg)−g∥∥g∥dt.​
(41)
For α≥0, the supplied condition (6), applied at the fixed starting point x with parameter t, bounds the integrand by LB​t∥g∥2. Integration yields
f(x−α∇B​f(x))≤f(x)−α(1−2LB​α​)∥∇B​f(x)∥2.​
(42)
This argument needs only the given directional Lipschitz continuity and continuous differentiability; convexity is not needed for this part. If the block coordinate gradient is zero, the inequality is equality.

The subgradient inequality for the differentiable convex function f gives
ϕ(u)=f(u)−f(y)−∇f(y)T(u−y)≥0for every u.
(43)
Thus y is a global minimizer of ϕ with value zero. Subtracting an affine function preserves convexity.
We must also justify using part (a) for ϕ, since its partial derivative can differ from that of f. First show that condition (6) for a singleton block implies the full coordinate Lipschitz continuity estimate
∣∂i​f(z+tei​)−∂i​f(z)∣≤Li​∣t∣,z∈Rn, t∈R.
(44)
If ∂i​f(z)=0, choose α=−t/∂i​f(z) in (6). If it is zero but ∂i​f(z+tei​)=0, apply the same argument starting at z+tei​ and reverse the displacement. If both partial derivatives are zero, the estimate is immediate. The availability of all real α in (6) justifies these choices.
Now ∂i​ϕ(z)=∂i​f(z)−∂i​f(y), so differences of this partial derivative along coordinate lines are unchanged. Hence ϕ has the same coordinate Lipschitz continuity constant Li​ and satisfies the singleton version of (6). Apply part (a) to ϕ with step size 1/Li​:
ϕ(x−Li​∂i​ϕ(x)​ei​)≤ϕ(x)−2Li​[∂i​ϕ(x)]2​.
(45)
Combining with its already established nonnegativity proves
0≤ϕ(x−Li​∂i​ϕ(x)​ei​)≤ϕ(x)−2Li​[∂i​ϕ(x)]2​.​
(46)
The coordinate estimate survives subtraction of the supporting affine function. This is the step that allows the arbitrary comparison point y to be exploited in part (c).

Df​(x,y)=f(x)−f(y)−∇f(y)T(x−y)≥0,δi​=∂i​f(x)−∂i​f(y).
(47)
Part (b), with its supporting affine function constructed at this y, implies the coordinate derivative bound by Bregman divergence
δi2​≤2Li​Df​(x,y).
(48)
For a nonempty block B, set SB​=∑i∈B​Li​ and δB​=∇B​f(x)−∇B​f(y). Sum the coordinate inequalities:
∥δB​∥2≤2SB​Df​(x,y).
(49)
Since y in part (b) was arbitrary, interchange x and y to obtain the same left side bounded by 2SB​Df​(y,x). Adding the two inequalities and dividing by two gives
∥δB​∥2≤SB​(Df​(x,y)+Df​(y,x))=SB​(∇f(x)−∇f(y))T(x−y).
(50)
Now require that x−y have zero coordinates outside B. The last dot product is then δBT​(x−y). By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
∥δB​∥2≤SB​∥δB​∥∥x−y∥.
(51)
If δB​=0, the desired estimate is immediate; otherwise divide by its Euclidean norm. We have proved the stronger block Lipschitz constant from coordinate smoothness estimate
∥∇B​f(x)−∇B​f(y)∥≤(i∈B∑​Li​)∥x−y∥when x−y is supported in B.​
(52)
In particular, take y=x−α∇B​f(x) for any real α. This proves (6), with a valid choice
LB​=i∈B∑​Li​≤∣B∣i∈Bmax​Li​.​
(53)
The least admissible Lipschitz constant is therefore no larger than this choice. For the empty block, the block coordinate gradient vanishes identically and its least nonnegative Lipschitz constant is zero. The proof uses only C1 differentiability, convexity and coordinate Lipschitz continuity, with no assumed Hessian matrix.

d OurBigBook.com
The supporting inequality in part 2(a) bounds f below by an affine function plus a positive squared Euclidean norm, making it a coercive function. Its continuity gives a global minimizer x∗, which is unique by strict convexity. Let Lmax​=maxi​Li​, ℓ=n/m and Δ0​=f(x0)−f(x∗). Here m is the block size, so its role differs from the number of component functions in Question 2. Use randomized block coordinate descent with uniformly sampled blocks of size m, independently at each update. Sampling uniformly from a fixed partition into ℓ equal-sized blocks gives the same calculation, since each coordinate has inclusion probability 1/ℓ=m/n.
Let β be a common upper bound on the block Lipschitz constants. Part (c) permits β=mLmax​. With step size 1/β, part (a) gives
f(x−β1​∇B​f(x))≤f(x)−2β1​∥∇B​f(x)∥2.
(54)
Summing squared partial derivatives according to their inclusion probabilities,
EB​∥∇B​f(x)∥2=ℓ1​∥∇f(x)∥2.
(55)
For a strongly convex function, part 2(a) also yields the strong convexity gradient inequality
∥∇f(x)∥2≥2γ(f(x)−f(x∗)).
(56)
Indeed, substitute y=x∗ into the supporting inequality and complete the square in x∗−x: the expression ∇f(x)Td+γ∥d∥2/2 is at least −∥∇f(x)∥2/(2γ) for every d.
Combining the last three estimates gives a contraction in conditional expectation:
E[f(Xt+1)−f(x∗)∣Xt]≤(1−ℓβγ​)(f(Xt)−f(x∗)).
(57)
By the tower property and repeated application,
E[f(Xt)−f(x∗)]≤(1−ℓβγ​)tΔ0​≤exp(−ℓβγt​)Δ0​.
(58)
An optimization epoch counts n coordinate partial derivative evaluations: it comprises ℓ block updates or one full gradient update. With sampling with replacement, an optimization epoch is this work measure; it need not visit every coordinate. Consequently the block method needs
O(γβ​logεΔ0​​) epochs,O(γmLmax​​logεΔ0​​) using β=mLmax​.​​
(59)
to bring the expected objective function gap below ε, for 0<ε<Δ0​, up to integer rounding.
For the steepest descent method, use a global Lipschitz gradient constant Lf​. Part (c) with the full block permits Lf​=∑i​Li​≤nLmax​. The descent lemma with step size 1/Lf​, together with the strong convexity gradient inequality, gives
f(xt+1)−f(x∗)≤(1−Lf​γ​)(f(xt)−f(x∗)).
(60)
An exact line search along the negative gradient does at least as well in objective function decrease per update. Ignoring its additional work, the resulting epoch complexity is
O(γLf​​logεΔ0​​) epochs,O(γnLmax​​logεΔ0​​) using Lf​=nLmax​.​​
(61)
The common coordinate-based guarantees favour blocks by a factor n/m=ℓ in epochs. In fact, with the conservative constants β=mLmax​ and Lf​=nLmax​, both methods have the same guaranteed contraction per update, while a block update uses only m coordinate partial derivatives.
This compares the bounds justified by the given constants. For a particular objective function, sharper constants give the comparison β/γ against Lf​/γ; an actual speedup of ℓ is not universal. Computing a block coordinate gradient also need not cost exactly m/n of a full gradient evaluation. The epoch complexity comparison assumes that coordinate partial derivative counts are a useful measure of work, and does not itself establish a wall-clock comparison.
