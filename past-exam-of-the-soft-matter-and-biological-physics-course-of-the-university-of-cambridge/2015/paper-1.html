Write ε=ε0​εr​ and take n0​ to be the reservoir density of each monovalent ion species. Equal electrochemical potentials in equilibrium give the Boltzmann ion densities in a symmetric electrolyte,
n+​=n0​e−eψ/(kB​T),n−​=n0​eeψ/(kB​T),ρe​=e(n+​−n−​)=−2en0​sinhkB​Teψ​.
(1)
Combining this with the electrostatic Poisson equation gives the planar Poisson-Boltzmann equation,
ψ′′=ε2en0​​sinhkB​Teψ​,κ2=εkB​T2e2n0​​.​
(2)
The Debye length is κ−1. If the symbol n0​ instead denotes the combined density of both species, the factor two is absent; the per-species convention above makes the normalization explicit. At ∣eψ∣≪kB​T, the linearized planar screening solution obeys ψ′′=κ2ψ. With ψ(0)=ζ<0 and ψ(∞)=0,
ψ(x)=ζe−κx.​
(3)
The positive ions are counterions and the negative ions are co-ions. At weak potential,
n+​≃n0​(1−kB​Teζ​e−κx),n−​≃n0​(1+kB​Teζ​e−κx).
(4)
They approach n0​ from above and below respectively, with equal first-order excess and deficit. For stronger negative potential, interpret the source's strong-potential comparison as a statement about ∣eψ∣, since ψ itself is negative. Counterions become strongly concentrated near the wall, while co-ions are exponentially depleted and remain nonnegative. Linearization misses this asymmetry and would eventually predict unphysical negative co-ion density. Point-ion mean-field theory itself eventually needs finite-size and correlation corrections; it provides no steric cap at arbitrarily large counterion concentration.
For steady tangential electro-osmosis, with no imposed pressure gradient and negligible inertia, the Stokes equation is ηv′′+ρe​E=0. Since ρe​=−εψ′′,
ηv′′=εEψ′′.
(5)
The conditions v(0)=0 and v′(∞)=0 give
v(x)=ηεE​[ψ(x)−ζ]=U(1−e−κx),U=−ηεζE​.​
(6)
The printed constant speed is the bulk velocity outside the double layer, not the velocity at the no-slip wall. With negative ζ the positive counterion excess drives fluid along the applied field. The far-field value is the Helmholtz-Smoluchowski electro-osmotic velocity.[ref]
For the finite channel, first model the added wall as an ideally uncharged insulating boundary with no normal field on its outer side: ψ′(d)=0, while ψ(0)=ζ. The one-charged-wall electro-osmotic channel then has
ψ(x)=ζcosh(κd)cosh[κ(d−x)]​.
(7)
Integrating the same force balance and enforcing v(0)=v(d)=0 gives
v(x)=U[1−(1−sech(κd))dx​−cosh(κd)cosh[κ(d−x)]​].​
(8)
For U>0, the maximum occurs at
xmax​=d−κ1​arsinh[κdcosh(κd)−1​]≃κlog(κd)​(κd≫1).​
(9)
In that limit v/U≃1−x/d−e−κx: the velocity rises rapidly from zero at the charged wall, reaches a maximum a few screening lengths away, then falls approximately linearly to zero at the neutral wall. One charged wall does not give a symmetric plug across a channel with no-slip at the other wall.
An alternative electrode condition ψ(d)=0 means a wall held at reservoir potential; it is not the same as zero surface charge. Under that convention,
v(x)=U[1−dx​−sinh(κd)sinh[κ(d−x)]​],
(10)
with cosh[κ(d−xmax​)]=sinh(κd)/(κd). Both boundary conventions have the same leading thin-double-layer profile above. An externally imposed pressure gradient would add a pressure-driven profile; none is specified here.
For dilute nonadsorbing uncharged polymers in equilibrium or sufficiently weak flow, the entropic polymer depletion near nonadsorbing walls suppresses chain configurations near each wall. A depletion layer of order the coil radius of gyration is expected at both walls, with a bulk-like central region when d is much larger than the coil size. Strong confinement rounds this into a central enrichment. Neutrality removes a direct electrophoretic force, not excluded-volume entropy. Adsorption, concentration and strong-flow migration were not specified, so this is a conditional equilibrium sketch rather than a universal flowing-polymer profile.

The generic instrument uses a magnetically responsive bead, a controllable external field and optical tracking of bead position or orientation. A field gradient supplies translational force; rotating an appropriate anisotropic field or bead moment can supply torque. For a permanent dipole,
U=−m⋅B,F=∇(m⋅B),τ=m×B.
(11)
For an isotropic unsaturated induced moment with effective susceptibility contrast Δχ and bead volume V, the induced magnetic bead force instead follows from
U=−2μ0​VΔχ​B2,F=2μ0​VΔχ​∇B2.​
(12)
The factor one-half accounts for inducing the moment. A spatially uniform field alone supplies no translational force; an isotropic induced moment parallel to the field supplies no orienting torque either. Saturation or magnetic anisotropy requires the corresponding magnetization model.
Position tracking needs a length calibration, and force calibration needs measured field gradients, a known reference load, or calibrated thermal fluctuations. For a generic tensioned tether of extension ℓ, small transverse displacement x costs energy Fx2/(2ℓ), so F=kB​Tℓ/⟨x2⟩ under equilibrium, small-angle and single-mode assumptions. Attachment geometry, bead rotation, wall drag and finite acquisition bandwidth can affect this calibration. These equations describe generic magnetic tweezers mechanics, without assigning a biological response.

Fluctuation-induced forces arise because separation changes the fluctuations available to a system and therefore changes its free energy. The force is F=−∂F/∂d at fixed relevant control variables, not necessarily a force from a permanent charge or dipole.
For two neutral polarizable particles, correlated electromagnetic fluctuations give a short-distance dispersion attraction of the form −C6​/r6. Retardation changes the asymptotic distance dependence. Boundaries produce a related Casimir force; for parallel ideal conducting plates at zero temperature,
AE​=−720d3π2ℏc​,P=−240d4π2ℏc​.​
(13)
The negative pressure denotes attraction. Material response, nonplanar geometry and finite temperature alter the ideal result. The effect can be described through the separation-dependent electromagnetic mode spectrum rather than by treating a divergent absolute vacuum energy as observable.[ref]
In a classical depletion force, smaller particles or polymer coils are excluded from a region near larger surfaces. Overlap of excluded regions increases their available volume. For ideal dilute depletants of osmotic pressure Π, U(d)=−ΠVoverlap​(d); the resulting attraction is entropic and scales with temperature. Confined interface fluctuations can instead generate repulsion when close approach removes entropy. Near a continuous phase transition, a large correlation length makes boundary-conditioned order-parameter fluctuations produce a critical Casimir force. Its sign depends on the boundary preferences, so fluctuation forces are not universally attractive.
The useful common idea is a free-energy difference with explicit boundary conditions; quantum vacuum, thermal field and excluded-volume mechanisms have different physical fluctuations and different ranges.

A generic chemical reaction–diffusion system couples local kinetics to spatial transport:
∂t​u=f(u,v)+Du​∇2u,∂t​v=g(u,v)+Dv​∇2v,Du​,Dv​>0.
(14)
A homogeneous stationary state satisfies f=g=0. Let its reaction Jacobian be J=(ac​bd​). Local well-mixed stability requires a+d<0 and ad−bc>0. A Fourier perturbation of wavevector magnitude q evolves under J−q2diag(Du​,Dv​), whose determinant is
Du​Dv​q4−(Dv​a+Du​d)q2+detJ.
(15)
Since its trace remains negative, a diffusion-driven stationary instability appears if the determinant becomes negative for some allowed wavevector. In a spatially large domain the conditions are
Dv​a+Du​d>0,(Dv​a+Du​d)2>4Du​Dv​detJ.​
(16)
Together with reaction-only stability, these give a finite unstable wavelength range. Equal diffusion coefficients cannot generate this instability: they simply shift each reaction eigenvalue by −Dq2. On a finite domain, an admissible spatial eigenmode must actually lie in the unstable band. Nonlinearities then determine saturation and whether a predicted pattern is stable, so a linear unstable band alone does not calculate a final pattern.[ref]
Other kinetics support travelling fronts or oscillatory waves rather than a stationary pattern. For instance, the scalar abstract equation ∂t​u=D∂x2​u+ru(1−u/us​) has a pulled-front speed 2Dr​ for a sufficiently steep invading initial profile. Here it is used as an abstract kinetics-and-diffusion example, without a biological interpretation. The ratio of diffusion time L2/D to reaction time characterizes whether spatial mixing is rapid enough to justify a well-mixed model.

For generic optical tweezers, a tightly focused laser and objective with high numerical aperture create an intensity gradient that attracts a suitable dielectric bead toward the focus. Momentum transfer also creates a scattering force; stable three-dimensional trapping requires sufficient restoring gradient force. A condenser and photodetector or camera track displacement. Near a stable focus, Fx​=−kx defines the stiffness and requires both a position calibration and a force calibration.[ref]
At thermal equilibrium in a harmonic well, the equipartition trap calibration is k⟨(x−⟨x⟩)2⟩=kB​T. Alternatively, fitting the overdamped harmonic-trap power spectrum gives fc​=k/(2πγ), hence k=2πγfc​. Detector noise, finite sampling and exposure time, wall corrections to drag, and drift must be controlled rather than attributed to stiffness.
For an isolated sphere in a Newtonian fluid, define Re=ρf​v(2a)/η using its diameter. The supplied viscosity converts to 10−3Pas. The generic Stokes drag calculation gives
FD​=6πηav=1.88×10−14N=0.0188pN,γ=6πηa=1.88×10−8Nsm−1.​
(17)
Here Re=2×10−6[ρf​/(103kgm−3)]; this retains the unspecified fluid density as a parameter rather than treating a density as supplied data. Ignoring biological coupling, the mechanical thermal model is the Langevin equation for a harmonic trap,
mx¨+γx˙+kx=ξ(t),⟨ξ(t)⟩=0,⟨ξ(t)ξ(t′)⟩=2γkB​Tδ(t−t′).
(18)
For independent repeats with the specified initial conditions, the ensemble mean X=⟨x⟩ obeys the homogeneous damped-oscillator equation. An average over N independent zero-mean force samples reduces its variance by N, with a finite detector bandwidth needed to define samples of white noise. High bandwidth alone does not remove random forces; averaging independent records or sufficiently separated samples is the additional operation. A single trajectory retains thermal fluctuations. For stationary overdamped position fluctuations, a long time average over duration T≫τk​=γ/k has approximately Var(xˉ)≃2(kB​T/k)τk​/T.
The initial-value solution of a damped harmonic trap has characteristic roots r±​=[−γ±γ2−4mk​]/(2m). For distinct roots,
X(t)=r+​−r−​v0​−r−​x0​​er+​t+r+​−r−​r+​x0​−v0​​er−​t.​
(19)
For critical damping, γ2=4mk,
X(t)=e−γt/(2m)[x0​+(v0​+2mγx0​​)t].
(20)
For the underdamped case the same real solution can be written e−γt/(2m)[x0​cosωd​t+(v0​+γx0​/(2m))sinωd​t/ωd​], with ωd2​=k/m−γ2/(4m2). These describe the deterministic response or conditional mean, not the entire noisy trajectory.
For overdamped trap relaxation after an inertial boundary layer, let ϵ=km/γ2≪1. The slow and fast roots are
rs​=−γk​[1+O(ϵ)],rf​=−mγ​[1+O(ϵ)],τm​=γm​≪τk​=kγ​.
(21)
After t≫τm​, the fast exponential is negligible and the surviving amplitude is x0​+(m/γ)v0​+O(ϵx0​,ϵmv0​/γ). If also ∣(m/γ)v0​∣≪∣x0​∣, then to leading overdamped order
X(t)≃x0​e−kt/γ.​
(22)
Small Reynolds number justifies the linear viscous force; it does not alone prove the separate small inertial-trap parameter ϵ. Neglecting v0​ requires both rapid momentum relaxation and a negligible slow-mode amplitude shift. If x0​=0, a nonzero initial velocity can leave a small slow displacement even after its own fast transient decays. The result concerns generic bead mechanics only.
