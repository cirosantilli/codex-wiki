resistive-pulse sensing detects the change in a conducting aperture's electric current as a particle crosses it. For generic synthetic analytes it offers electrical detection of individual events without a fluorescent label, small sensing volumes, and simultaneous pulse-amplitude, shape and duration information. Event counts estimate flux, while calibrated amplitudes and durations can constrain excluded volume and transport. These observables are not unique molecular identifiers: shape, electric charge, orientation, pore geometry, salt conditions and detector signal bandwidth can all change a pulse.

Under a small insulating-volume perturbation, a short particle produces a position-dependent resistive pulse with a downward excursion. If its excluded volume is Ω and small compared with the local sensing volume, a local volume-exclusion model gives ΔR(z)∝ρΩ/[π2r(z)4]. Thus ΔI≃−I0​ΔR/R is strongest near either narrow end. A sufficiently resolved event can show two deeper end features separated by a shallower middle depression, then return to baseline. Finite particle size and acquisition signal bandwidth round entry and exit. The original illustration below shows this conditional small-particle mechanism, not a fitted amplitude. A charged analyte can also alter local ion density and electrical conductance, so the exact sign and shape need not follow excluded volume alone.

Also b/a+a/b≥2, so nonuniform electric field makes this point-particle transit no faster than the uniform-field value at the same length and electric potential difference. The wide, weak-field interior dominates residence time.
The final length-dependent observations do not uniquely identify a microscopic mechanism. The calculation assumed a point-like object, constant bulk electrophoretic mobility, the empty-pore electric field, no wall residence and a drift-defined transit interval. An extended chain can sample entrance and reservoir fields simultaneously, alter its conformation and tension, and change the effective driving-to-friction ratio. These effects can make a bulk-mobility, local-field prediction underestimate the driving. For a small object, wall interactions, reversible trapping, or repeated entry excursions can add residence time that the deterministic formula omits. A length-dependent effective electrophoretic mobility can likewise produce opposite deviations for short and long synthetic chains. Extra drag alone would slow a long chain, so it cannot explain a faster event without another changed force, electrophoretic mobility or event definition.
Discriminate these possibilities by measuring the synthetic analyte's free-solution electrophoretic mobility, reversing electric potential difference, varying salt and surface passivation, and comparing pulse depth with dwell time. Resolved multiple occupancy or folds can alter amplitude as well as timing. Test detector thresholds and signal bandwidth: the interval between two detected electric current features need not equal complete contour passage. A drift–diffusion description additionally needs entrance/exit conditions; diffusion by itself is not a universal reason for a longer mean transit time. No unique anomalous-duration prediction follows from the provided parameters alone.
