Static friction (∣fs​∣≤μs​N)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Classical mechanics Force
OurBigBook.com Words: 403 Articles: 1
Static friction is the tangential contact force that maintains zero contact slip. “Static” describes the contact state: both surfaces can move together in the chosen inertial frame. In the ideal Coulomb dry-contact model, the allowable magnitude is bounded by
∣fs​∣≤μs​N,
(125)
where N≥0 is the magnitude of the normal force and μs​≥0 is the coefficient of static friction. The actual static friction is whatever the equations of motion require to maintain zero contact slip, provided it lies within this bound. Equality specifies limiting static friction; a smaller load generally requires a smaller contact force. [ref]
Use the stipulated toy model from kinetic friction: mass m=1kg, normal force N=10N, coefficient of static friction μs​=0.5, and coefficient of kinetic friction μk​=0.3. A box initially at rest on stationary horizontal ground is pushed right by F≥0. To remain at rest, Newton's second law requires F+fs​=ma=0, hence fs​=−F. For F=4N, this gives fs​=−4N and a=0, within the 5N bound. Automatically setting ∣fs​∣=μs​N would predict the wrong force and acceleration.
For F=6N, maintaining rest would require ∣fs​∣=6N>5N, so rest cannot persist. Once the box slides right, kinetic friction is fk​=−3N and the acceleration is a=(6−3)/1=3m/s2. At exactly F=5N, the inequality still permits rest with fs​=−5N. An already right-sliding box under the same applied force instead has fk​=−3N and a=2m/s2. Applied force alone therefore does not specify the friction branch: the contact state and motion history matter.
The diagram compares conditional states, rather than specifying a force-ramp trajectory. The static branch includes its 5N endpoint; the sliding branch also exists below that load when rightward contact slip is already present. To solve a new problem, first impose zero contact slip, find the required static friction and normal force from the equations of motion, and check the inequality. If that contact condition fails, use kinetic friction with the actual slip direction.
This distinction makes static friction useful for traction. For an ideal driven tire undergoing rolling without slipping on stationary ground, the road can exert a forward static friction that accelerates the vehicle while the tire's contact point has zero velocity. Thus static friction can point along the center of mass motion; its role is to prevent contact slip, rather than always to oppose the motion of the whole body.
