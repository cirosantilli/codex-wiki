Use ℏ=c=1, the Minkowski metric η=diag(1,−1), and p2=(p0)2−(p1)2. The momentum-space Feynman rules for the real scalar field are
internal line: p2−m2+iϵi​,four-leg vertex: −ig4​.​
(1)
At each interaction vertex impose two-dimensional momentum conservation; before removing the overall conservation factor this is (2π)2δ(2)(∑j​pj​). Integrate each independent loop momentum with d2k/(2π)2, and divide a Feynman diagram by its symmetry factor. External lines carry the same scalar propagator in an unamputated two-point correlation function; they are removed in an amputated scattering amplitude.
The quadratic action is inverted with the Feynman i-epsilon prescription to obtain the scalar propagator. Expanding the Dyson series gives −ig4​/4! per insertion; the 4! attachments in the Wick theorem cancel the factorial. Fourier integration over the insertion point supplies the momentum conservation factor. Automorphisms that leave the labelled external attachments fixed account for the remaining symmetry factor. In two spacetime dimensions the mass dimensions are [ϕ]=0 and [g4​]=2.

Write ϕB​=Z1/2ϕ, Z=1+δZ​, and define the mass counterterm δm​ as a squared-mass coefficient:
Lct​=21​δZ​(∂ϕ)2−21​δm​ϕ2,δm​=ZmB2​−m2.
(5)
With the self-energy convention in b, the inverse scalar propagator is p2−m2+δZ​p2−δm​−Σ(p2). Requiring the pole mass to equal m therefore gives the on-shell renormalization scheme condition
δm​=m2δZ​−Σ(m2).
(6)
At one loop order, Σ1′​=0; hence a unit pole residue also gives δZ(1)​=0, and
δm(1)​=−8πg4​​log(1+m2Λ2​),mB2​=m2+δm(1)​+O(g42​).​
(7)
The mass counterterm Feynman rule is −iδm​, cancelling the tadpole diagram. A convention defining a positive mass subtraction by mB2​=m2−δmsub​ instead has δmsub​=−δm​; specifying this convention avoids a spurious sign disagreement.
There is no one-loop wave-function renormalization because the tadpole diagram is independent of momentum. Each one-loop four-point bubble diagram has two scalar propagators, so its large Euclidean loop momentum integral behaves as ∫d2kE​/kE4​ and is ultraviolet finite. Thus no divergent quartic interaction counterterm is required. Finite coupling redefinitions remain possible. At higher loop orders, finite wave-function renormalization may be needed if unit pole residue is imposed; power counting only rules out its ultraviolet divergence.

Let I,L,V count internal lines, independent loops, and interaction vertices of a connected Feynman diagram. The graph identity L=I−V+1 and the two inverse powers from each scalar propagator give its superficial degree of divergence:
ω=2L−2I=2(1−V).​
(8)
The same formula applies to every connected subgraph. Graphs with V≥2 have negative superficial degree of divergence, but this alone does not remove a possible ultraviolet subdivergence. A one-vertex subgraph with external legs in the quartic scalar field theory can diverge only by a tadpole diagram, whose subtraction was fixed in c. Subtract every such tadpole diagram; every remaining loop subgraph then has V≥2 and negative superficial degree of divergence. Its ultraviolet divergence is absent. Equivalently, normal ordering the quartic interaction removes all self-contractions at a single interaction vertex. Thus no new divergent field-dependent counterterm structure is needed, and in a normal-ordering subtraction scheme in two dimensions the one-loop mass subtraction exhausts these divergent coefficients.
The literal assertion that there are no further counterterm contributions in the on-shell renormalization scheme is stronger and generally false. A concrete counterexample is the two-loop sunset diagram. After analytic continuation below its three-particle threshold, the magnitude of its self-energy at p2=m2 contains the strictly positive finite integral
​Σsunset​(m2)​=6(4π)2m2g42​​∫x,y,z≥0​dxdydzxy+xz+yz−xyzδ(1−x−y−z)​>0(g4​=0).
(9)
The integrable corners do not spoil convergence. Its nonzero value shifts the pole mass, requiring an additional finite mass counterterm if m is to remain the exact pole mass; its derivative can likewise change the pole residue. These are compatible with the power counting result. If vacuum diagrams are retained rather than divided out in normalized correlation functions, a separate constant vacuum energy counterterm is also required: the one-vertex double-loop vacuum diagram is already divergent. The claim about the mass subtraction concerns field-dependent counterterms and normalized correlation functions.

δg4(1)​=−2g6​​TΛ​=−8πg6​​log(1+m2Λ2​).​
(11)

VB​(ϕ)=24g4​​(ϕ4−6Tϕ2+3T2)+720g6​​(ϕ6−15Tϕ4+45T2ϕ2−15T3).
(12)

Additional finite on-shell renormalization scheme adjustments are subject to the same qualification as d.

For nonzero real g, the continuous internal symmetry of a classical field theory is the global U(1) symmetry
ϕ⟼e−iαϕ,Φ⟼e−2iαΦ,α constant.
(14)
The kinetic terms and mass terms are invariant separately, while the phases in ϕ2Φ† cancel. Independent phase rotations would instead require 2αϕ​−αΦ​=0 modulo 2π, leaving one connected circle. Simultaneous complex conjugation is also a discrete symmetry, reversing the continuous charge; including it gives U(1)⋊Z2​≅O(2) for generic parameters. At g=0 the continuous symmetry enlarges to U(1)×U(1).
The Noether current with particles assigned positive charges is
jμ=i(ϕ†∂μϕ−(∂μϕ†)ϕ)+2i(Φ†∂μΦ−(∂μΦ†)Φ).​
(15)
For example, put A=ϕ2Φ† and B=ϕ†2Φ. The Euler-Lagrange equations give ∂μ​jϕμ​=ig(A−B) and ∂μ​jΦμ​=ig(B−A), with the factor 2 already included in jΦμ​. Their sum vanishes. The Noether charge Q=∫d3xj0 is conserved and, after normal ordering, counts
Q=Nϕ​−Nϕˉ​​+2(NΦ​−NΦˉ​).
(16)
Thus a Φ particle carries two units of the same conserved charge carried by a ϕ particle; their antiparticles carry the negatives. The interaction permits conversion of one Φ particle into two ϕ particles when allowed by conservation of energy, while preserving this weighted number.

Four external Φ lines require four cubic interaction vertices, since each such interaction vertex contains only one heavy field. Their other eight endpoints must form four light internal lines. A connected Feynman diagram therefore has V=I=4 and L=1: the leading contribution is a one-loop box diagram, of order g4, with four ϕ scalar propagators. Charge-two incoming and outgoing heavy lines attach alternately around the box, as shown in panel (d) of b's figure. Each incoming heavy line supplies the two outgoing light-charge arrows at its corner, and each outgoing heavy line absorbs them. With external labels fixed there is one such box and its symmetry factor is one.
For ∣p∣≪M≪m, the external energies are also small compared with m, so expand the loop integrand in all external four-momenta. Its leading term is
iMΦΦ​=g4∫(2π)4d4k​(k2−m2+iϵ)41​+O(m6ig4M2​).
(23)
A Wick rotation and the Euclidean massive loop integral give
∫(2π)4d4k​(k2−m2+iϵ)41​=i∫(2π)4d4kE​​(kE2​+m2)41​=96π2m4i​.
(24)
Hence, more precisely than just its requested scaling,
MΦΦ​=96π2m4g4​[1+O(m2M2​,m2∣p∣2​)].​
(25)
The mass dimension [g]=1 makes g4/m4 dimensionless, as a four-dimensional scattering amplitude should be. The leading amplitude has no extra identical-particle multiplicity.

A parity symmetry in quantum field theory reverses spatial orientation, so its spatial matrix must lie in the determinant-−1 component of the orthogonal group. In two spatial dimensions, reversing both coordinates gives −I2​, which has determinant +1 and is a rotation through π in the special orthogonal group in two dimensions. It cannot test a discrete orientation-reversing symmetry independent of ordinary rotations. Reflecting one coordinate, for example diag(−1,1), has determinant −1 and supplies the required parity symmetry in quantum field theory.
In three spatial dimensions, −I3​ has determinant −1, so simultaneous inversion of all three coordinates is the standard choice. A single-coordinate reflection also reverses orientation there, and differs from −I3​ by a proper rotation through π. Thus, in a rotationally invariant theory, it tests the same discrete symmetry. The word is in the source specifies conventional representatives: the essential distinction is orientation reversal, rather than a unique choice of axis. Both transformations preserve the ordinary spatial integration measure through the absolute value of their Jacobian determinants.
