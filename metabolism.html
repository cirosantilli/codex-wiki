Metabolism

 Home Biology
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 91
The network of chemical reactions that maintains a living system, supplies energy and builds or processes its material. Gas emissions can arise as primary metabolic byproducts or secondary metabolic byproducts, with different implications for an atmospheric biosignature gas.
Enzymes make this network operate on biologically useful timescales. Following material through successive substrates and reaction products explains why a single fast enzyme need not make the entire network fast: another step may constrain the flow. Changing enzyme amounts or activity therefore changes which routes can carry material, connecting molecular chemical kinetics to the maintenance and growth of a biological cell. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: multienzyme complexes and cell metabolism.

Photosynthesis

Words: 1k Articles: 29
Metabolism OurBigBook.com
Photosynthesis captures light energy and transforms it into chemical forms that a biological cell can use. In plants, algae and cyanobacteria, this supports carbon fixation: incorporation of carbon from carbon dioxide into chemical compounds used to build living material. Capturing light and incorporating carbon are distinct processes coupled through metabolism. See OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.1: overview of photosynthesis.
For oxygenic photosynthesis, a simplified net equation writes a six-carbon sugar, glucose, as the representative product:
6CO2​+6H2​O+light⟶C6​H12​O6​+6O2​.
(1)
This is net stoichiometry, not a one-step mechanism. A worked bookkeeping check counts six carbon chemical atoms, twelve hydrogen chemical atoms, and eighteen oxygen chemical atoms on each side: the product side has six oxygen chemical atoms in the sugar and twelve in molecular oxygen. The light label supplies energy, not additional chemical atoms.
Crucially, the released molecular oxygen comes from water, not carbon dioxide. Cancelling water made and consumed in different steps hides this distinction in the net equation. The Calvin cycle supplies a net three-carbon triose phosphate, such as glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, for subsequent sugar synthesis; it does not directly export glucose. See OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.2: light-dependent reactions and OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.3: using light energy to make organic molecules.
The useful achievement is connecting incoming light to both usable energy and new biological material. ATP is an immediate energy currency; NADPH supplies electrons for synthesis; carbon dioxide supplies the carbon incorporated into carbohydrates. These roles are complementary. Conservation of energy still holds: photosynthesis transforms energy, never creates it, and some absorbed energy is dissipated as heat. A plant also carries out cellular respiration, including in biological cells that perform photosynthesis. Gas exchange reflects both processes. Oxygenic photosynthesis remains central to interpreting terrestrial molecular oxygen as an atmospheric biosignature gas. See OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.3: photosynthesis and respiration.

Oxygenic photosynthesis

Photosynthesis OurBigBook.com
Oxygenic photosynthesis uses water as an electron source and releases molecular oxygen. Plants, algae and cyanobacteria provide examples. The defining distinction from anoxygenic photosynthesis is molecular oxygen release, not merely capture of light.

Anoxygenic photosynthesis

Words: 39
Photosynthesis OurBigBook.com
Anoxygenic photosynthesis captures light without producing molecular oxygen. It occurs in some bacteria and uses electron sources other than water. Whether carbon fixation accompanies this light capture depends on the organism and conditions; photosynthesis does not imply one universal carbon-assimilation pathway. See Alberts et al.: chloroplasts and photosynthetic bacteria.

Light-dependent reactions

Words: 297 Articles: 10
Photosynthesis OurBigBook.com
In a chloroplast, light-dependent reactions take place at thylakoid membranes. Chlorophyll captures light; electron transfer helps establish an electrochemical gradient across the thylakoid membrane, enabling formation of ATP. The oxygenic photosynthesis pathway also forms NADPH and releases molecular oxygen from water. ATP and NADPH become available in the chloroplast stroma for the Calvin cycle. See Alberts et al.: chloroplast light reactions.

Carbon fixation

Words: 161 Articles: 2
Metabolism OurBigBook.com
Carbon fixation incorporates carbon from carbon dioxide into organic chemical compounds. It identifies a material transformation, rather than a particular source of energy: carbon fixation can be supported by photosynthesis or by other metabolism. The Calvin cycle is one pathway, not the definition of all carbon fixation.

Calvin cycle

Words: 126 Articles: 1
Carbon fixation OurBigBook.com
The Calvin cycle couples carbon fixation to use of ATP and NADPH. In a chloroplast it takes place in the chloroplast stroma. Its chemical reactions incorporate carbon dioxide, use NADPH-supplied electrons, and regenerate the molecules that accept further carbon dioxide.
In the standard Calvin cycle bookkeeping, three carbon dioxide molecules require nine ATP and six NADPH for one net three-carbon triose phosphate. A six-carbon sugar equivalent therefore corresponds to six carbon dioxide, eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH; subsequent sugar synthesis is separate. These are pathway requirements, not a universal measured photosynthesis yield. See OpenStax Biology 2e, 8.3: Calvin cycle bookkeeping.
Calling the Calvin cycle light-independent means its individual chemical reactions do not directly capture light; it does not mean the Calvin cycle runs only at night or independently of the supplies from light-dependent reactions.

RuBisCO

Words: 23
Calvin cycle OurBigBook.com
Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase, the enzyme catalysing carbon-dioxide addition to ribulose bisphosphate in the Calvin cycle. Its competing oxygenase reaction links carbon assimilation to photorespiration.

Cellular respiration

Words: 1k Articles: 11
Metabolism OurBigBook.com
Cellular respiration transfers energy from chemical fuels into usable forms including ATP. Oxidation of the fuel is coupled to reduction of an electron acceptor; the energy transfer can support ATP synthesis. Plants carry out cellular respiration as well as photosynthesis. Their biological cells need usable energy in darkness too, and a seed can draw on stored fuel during germination.[ref][ref]
Follow three different accounts: carbon moving from fuel to products, electrons moving to an electron acceptor, and energy becoming available for cellular work. They are connected, but an oxygen uptake measurement alone does not identify the fuel or count the ATP made. The pea seed respirometry experiment makes this distinction visible with simple apparatus.

Aerobic respiration

Words: 449 Articles: 1
Cellular respiration OurBigBook.com
Aerobic respiration uses molecular oxygen as the final electron acceptor. For complete oxidation of glucose, the net material balance is
C6​H12​O6​+6O2​⟶6CO2​+6H2​O.
(2)
This records the overall conversion of glucose, molecular oxygen, carbon dioxide and water; it is not one enzyme-catalyzed step, and it omits the separate ATP accounting. For an original stoichiometry check, each side contains six carbon, twelve hydrogen and eighteen oxygen chemical atoms. If exactly 1mmol of glucose undergoes this complete conversion, the equation requires 6mmol of molecular oxygen and yields 6mmol each of carbon dioxide and net water. This is an equation-based calculation, not an observed seed measurement.
The molecular oxygen is reduced to water, rather than being the source of the carbon dioxide's carbon. A formal redox electron balance, using acidic half-reaction notation, makes the roles explicit:
C6​H12​O6​+6H2​O6O2​+24H++24e−​⟶6CO2​+24H++24e−,⟶12H2​O.​
(3)
Adding and cancelling gives the net equation above. Both half-reactions conserve chemical atoms and electric charge. The 24 electrons balance a net transfer; they are carried through cellular intermediates, rather than released as a pool of free electrons. The final respiratory reduction can be written O2​+4H++4e−⟶2H2​O. These equations are bookkeeping, not a tracing experiment that assigns every product oxygen chemical atom to its original molecule.[ref]

Respiration and ATP coupling (ADP+Pi​⟶ATP)

Words: 269
Aerobic respiration OurBigBook.com
Glycolysis converts glucose into two pyruvate molecules, with a net two ATP and reduced NADH; it does not directly consume molecular oxygen. Pyruvate processing and the citric acid cycle, also called the TCA cycle, release carbon dioxide and supply reduced carriers. Thus releasing carbon dioxide and consuming molecular oxygen occur at different stages.[ref]
An electron transport chain reoxidizes carriers such as NADH. Coupling some of its redox steps to proton transport establishes an electrochemical gradient; ATP synthase can use that gradient to form ATP from adenosine diphosphate and inorganic phosphate. This is oxidative phosphorylation. A separate route, substrate-level phosphorylation, transfers a phosphate group from a reacting substrate to adenosine diphosphate. In typical eukaryotic biological cells, the respiratory chain occupies the inner membrane of a mitochondrion.[ref]
The useful achievement is controlled transfer: fuel oxidation can replenish an ATP supply that supports active transport and synthesis. ATP is a reusable chemical carrier, not newly created energy; ATP hydrolysis and the coupled cellular process have their own net free energy balance. Some fuel energy is dissipated as heat.[ref]
There is no universal yield of 36 or 38 ATP per glucose. Actual ATP recovery depends on carrier transfer routes, coupling and diversion of intermediates to other metabolism. In particular, an oxygen uptake rate cannot be converted into an ATP production rate by assuming one fixed multiplier.[ref]

Anaerobic respiration

Words: 84
Cellular respiration OurBigBook.com
Anaerobic respiration uses a respiratory electron transport chain with a final electron acceptor other than molecular oxygen. External acceptors can include sulfate, depending on the organism. “External” distinguishes a supplied terminal acceptor from the fuel-derived organic intermediates used in fermentation; it does not mean that the reduction must take place outside the biological cell. In fermentation, regeneration of carriers such as NADH to their oxidized form permits glycolysis to continue without a respiratory electron transport chain. Both routes can operate without molecular oxygen, but they are different mechanisms.[ref]

Respirometer

Words: 658 Articles: 5

A respirometer measures gas changes associated with cellular respiration. In the absorber-based pea seed respirometry apparatus, the changing gas inventory is inferred from a liquid boundary's displacement. A respirometer is therefore a physical measurement coupled to a biological process, not a direct ATP counter.

Pea seed respirometry

Words: 274
Respirometer OurBigBook.com
The University of Oklahoma K20 Center's handout describes three sealed vial-and-pipette respirometers in each temperature bath: soaked, germinating peas; nongerminating peas plus inert plastic beads; and beads alone. Displaced water establishes equal occupied volumes. Absorbent cotton holds potassium hydroxide, which removes carbon dioxide; nonabsorbent cotton separates it from the peas. The source equilibrates the vials before submersion and again before the initial reading. Colored water makes the boundary visible in each calibrated pipette. Readers then record positions every five minutes for twenty minutes.[ref]
Bath temperature, vial position and seal integrity matter. The beads-only respirometer detects common temperature or atmospheric pressure drift; its change is subtracted from each pea reading. Nongerminating peas provide a biological comparison, not an assumption of zero cellular respiration. A larger corrected uptake slope for germinating peas would support greater molecular oxygen consumption by that sample under the tested conditions.[ref]
Equal displaced volumes help match the gas spaces; they do not establish equal living mass or equal developmental state. Comparing uptake per seed and uptake per dry mass asks different questions. Leaks, incomplete carbon dioxide absorption, gas dissolution and changing water-vapour contributions can distort the respirometer inference. A blank correction cannot repair a sample-specific leak. These are consequences of the respirometer gas balance, rather than measurements reported in the handout.

Respirometer gas balance

Words: 322 Articles: 1
Respirometer OurBigBook.com
For an original idealized mathematical model, let nO2​​(t) be the amount of substance of molecular oxygen consumed since the initial reading and nCO2​​(t) the carbon dioxide produced. Suppose a fraction f of that carbon dioxide is removed from the gas, all other gas inventories remain fixed, and temperature T and gas pressure P stay constant. The ideal gas law gives the positive gas contraction
D(t)=V(0)−V(t)=PRT​[nO2​​(t)−(1−f)nCO2​​(t)],
(4)
where R is the molar gas constant and V the gas volume. With complete absorption, f=1, the contraction reflects molecular oxygen uptake regardless of the amount of carbon dioxide made. For a uniform pipette bore of area A, an inward boundary displacement x gives D=Ax. A graduated pipette can instead supply the displaced volume directly.
Let Ds​ denote sample contraction and Db​ the matched blank contraction. Define Dcorr​=Ds​−Db​ with both taken relative to their own initial readings. Under the preceding assumptions,
nO2​​(t)≃RTPDcorr​(t)​,rO2​​=RTP​t2​−t1​Dcorr​(t2​)−Dcorr​(t1​)​.
(5)
The slope of corrected volume against time estimates uptake over the chosen interval. The approximate sign acknowledges that a liquid boundary also responds to hydrostatic and capillary pressure, while the gas can exchange water vapour. A fixed-volume apparatus would instead show a pressure decrease; its ideal conversion is nO2​​≃V(P(0)−P(t))/(RT) after complete absorption and appropriate blank correction.
The ideal gas law also exposes drift without any biology: for fixed gas inventory, V∝T/P. A one-percent rise in absolute temperature at fixed pressure produces a one-percent expansion. Matching the initial gas spaces makes such common changes easier to subtract. Subtracting unmatched blanks can leave a physical signal that looks biological.
Respiratory quotient (RQ=nCO2​​/nO2​​)
Words: 89
Respirometer gas balance OurBigBook.com
The respiratory quotient compares carbon dioxide production with molecular oxygen consumption over the same interval. Complete glucose oxidation in the net equation above gives RQ=6/6=1. In the ideal respirometer gas balance with no absorber, f=0, that conversion gives D=0: substantial cellular respiration can occur without any net gas contraction. A zero contraction therefore does not by itself demonstrate zero metabolism.
Other fuels, incomplete conversion and concurrent synthesis can alter the ratio. Absorber-based respirometry isolates molecular oxygen uptake by deliberately removing the other gas signal; it cannot independently determine the respiratory quotient or identify glucose as the consumed fuel. Separate carbon dioxide measurement would supply the missing account.

Secondary metabolic byproduct

Words: 42
Metabolism OurBigBook.com
A compound released through specialized functions such as signaling, defense or stress adaptation rather than the core reactions needed for energy and biomass. Dimethyl sulfide and chloromethane are examples discussed as atmospheric biosignatures. Their specialized origins may improve specificity while lowering the global emission flux.

Primary metabolic byproduct

Words: 38
Metabolism OurBigBook.com
A product released by reactions needed for energy acquisition, growth or biomass construction. Methane from energy-yielding microbial reactions and molecular oxygen from oxygenic photosynthesis are examples. Abiotic chemistry can make some of the same molecules, so production mechanism and environmental abundance matter.
