Calorimetry

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 8
Calorimetry measures heat transfers. A calorimeter, a calibrated heat capacity and an accounting of unwanted heat transfer connect an observed temperature change to an energy balance, as in heat-engine calorimetry and heat-pump calorimetry.
The central idea is to measure a response that is easier to observe than the transfer itself: a known heat capacity turns a temperature change into an energy estimate. Mixing calorimetry can determine an unknown specific heat capacity, while a chemical reaction can be studied by measuring the response of its surroundings. The useful observable is a change in temperature, together with a stated thermodynamic system boundary; a hot object does not contain a separately conserved store of heat.
For a closed system with unchanged bulk kinetic energy and potential energy, let Qin​ be net inward heat and Won​ net inward work. The first law of thermodynamics gives
ΔU=Qin​+Won​.
(1)
If the boundary includes a hot sample, receiving water and the participating vessel, their mutual heat transfer is internal. With no external input, the total storage change is minus the signed outward heat leakage, not the positive amount transferred from sample to water. A calorimeter calibration with an immersed resistor instead supplies electrical work across this boundary; Joule heating converts it into internal energy. Choosing only the receiving water as the boundary changes which transfers count as external.
The mechanical constraint decides which storage quantity the readout represents. For a closed system at fixed volume, with no other work, QV​=ΔU. At constant external pressure, with only expansion work pΔV, Qp​=ΔH, where H=U+pV is enthalpy. Thus the corresponding warming balances use heat capacity at constant volume or heat capacity at constant pressure. Electrical or shaft work must be included separately when present. These endpoint balances do not require the actual exchange to be a reversible thermodynamic process.
A covered cup open to atmospheric pressure approximately realizes the second constraint when escaping material is negligible. A sealed rigid reaction vessel realizes the first; its surrounding water, vessel and immersed parts form the calibrated receiver. The reaction's outward heat equals the receiver's gain only after losses and ignition input have been accounted for. The distinction concerns the reaction boundary, even if its receiver is open to atmospheric pressure.[ref]
A historical quantitative result appears in James Prescott Joule's On the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat (1850): Table IX gives 772.692 foot-pounds of mechanical work per warming of one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit, with weighing reduced to vacuum. His final rounded statement specifies water between 55 and 60 degrees Fahrenheit. He acknowledged residual vibration and sound as possible upward bias. This is an original reported conversion result, not the definition of the modern joule; the apparatus and resting controls are described in Joule's paddle-wheel experiment.[ref]
