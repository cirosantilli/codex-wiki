A commutative ring R is a Noetherian ring if every ideal has a finite generating set of an ideal. Equivalently, it satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals. Indeed, the union of an ascending chain is an ideal, and finitely many generators all belong to one term, after which the chain stabilizes. Conversely, an ideal without a finite generating set of an ideal would allow us to choose successively an+1​∈/(a1​,…,an​), producing a strictly ascending chain of ideals.
Let ϕ:R→S be a surjective ring homomorphism, where R is a Noetherian ring. For any ideal J of S, its inverse image is an ideal of R, say ϕ−1(J)=(a1​,…,ar​). Taking images gives
J=(ϕ(a1​),…,ϕ(ar​)).
(1)
Thus homomorphic images of Noetherian rings are Noetherian. This also applies to the image of an arbitrary ring homomorphism, by taking its image as the codomain.
The Hilbert basis theorem states that if R is a Noetherian ring, then the polynomial ring R[t] is a Noetherian ring. Repeating this gives the result for finitely many variables.
To prove it, fix an ideal K⊆R[t]. For n≥0, define the coefficient ideals of a polynomial ideal
An​={a∈R:some f∈K of degree at most n has coefficient a at tn}.
(2)
Here the zero polynomial is allowed. Each An​ is an ideal: addition and multiplication by constants preserve the required degree bound. Multiplying a representing polynomial by t gives An​⊆An+1​. The ascending chain condition therefore supplies N such that An​=AN​ for every n≥N.
For each 0≤n≤N, choose a finite generating set of an ideal an1​,…,anrn​​ for An​, omitting zero generators, and choose fnj​∈K of polynomial degree n with leading coefficient anj​. If An​=0, choose no such polynomials. We claim that the finitely many fnj​ generate K.
Take a nonzero f∈K of polynomial degree d. Put n=min(d,N). Its leading coefficient belongs to Ad​=An​, so write it as ∑j​bj​anj​. The polynomial
g=f−∑j​bj​td−nfnj​
(3)
belongs to K and is either zero or has polynomial degree strictly below d. Induction on polynomial degree now expresses f as a combination of the chosen fnj​; when d=0, the displayed difference is zero. Hence K is finitely generated. Since K was arbitrary, R[t] is a Noetherian ring.

For x∈X, let ex​ be the indicator function of {x}. In the ring of functions R=RX, multiplication is pointwise, so
ex​f=f(x)ex​.
(4)
Suppose first that X is finite, and let J be an ideal of R. Define S={x∈X:f(x)=0 for some f∈J}. If x∈S, choose such an f; multiplying it by ex​/f(x) shows that ex​∈J. Every f∈J satisfies
f=∑x∈S​f(x)ex​,
(5)
where the real scalars are constant functions. Thus J=(ex​:x∈S) has a finite generating set of an ideal, so R is a Noetherian ring. If X is empty, R is the zero ring, and the conclusion still holds.
If X is infinite, consider the ideal
F={f∈RX:{x:f(x)=0} is finite}.
(6)
It is an ideal because a sum has support contained in the union of the two supports, and multiplication by any function cannot enlarge support. If f1​,…,fr​ generated F, their supports would have a finite union T. Every element of (f1​,…,fr​) would vanish outside T, whereas ex​∈F for any x∈X∖T. This contradiction proves
RX is Noetherian⟺X is finite.​

The radical of an ideal I is
I​=rad(I)={a∈R:an∈I for some n≥1}.​
(12)
We first establish the properties needed below. If am,bn∈I, every term of the binomial theorem expansion of (a+b)m+n−1 contains either am or bn, so a+b∈I​. Also (−a)m∈I and (ra)m=rmam∈I. Thus I​ is an ideal. The definition gives I⊆I​ and shows that I⊆J implies I​⊆J​. Finally, if an∈I​, then (an)m∈I for some m, so a∈I​. Hence I​​=I​; in other words, I​ is a radical ideal.
We prove the stronger fact that the radical of an ideal is the intersection of the prime ideals containing it:
I​=⋂P⊇IP prime​​P.
(13)
If a∈I​ and P⊇I is a prime ideal, then an∈P for some n, and repeated application of the definition of a prime ideal gives a∈P. This proves one inclusion.
Conversely, fix a∈/I​. The multiplicatively closed set T={1,a,a2,…} is disjoint from I. Consider the ideals containing I and disjoint from T. This collection is nonempty, and the union of any chain in it is another such ideal. By Zorn's lemma, there is a maximal member P. It is proper because 1∈T.
To prove that P is a prime ideal, suppose bc∈P but b,c∈/P. Maximality forces both P+(b) and P+(c) to meet T. Thus, for some u,v≥0,
au=p+rb,av=q+sc,p,q∈P,r,s∈R.
(14)
Multiplication gives au+v∈P, since every term lies in P, including rsbc. This contradicts disjointness from T. Therefore P is a prime ideal containing I and avoiding a. This proves the other inclusion, including the required prime ideal avoiding a multiplicative subset argument without using any unproved facts about the nilradical.
If I=I​, the displayed intersection represents I as required. Conversely, an intersection of ideals that are all prime ideals is a radical ideal: if an belongs to every one of them, then so does a. Consequently
I=I​⟺I is an intersection of prime ideals.​
(15)
The unit ideal I=R is included by interpreting the empty intersection as R.

The Krull dimension of a commutative ring R is
dimR=sup{d:there exist prime ideals P0​⊊P1​⊊⋯⊊Pd​}.​
(29)
The length of a prime chain counts strict inclusions, rather than the number of prime ideals.
Let R be a principal ideal domain that is not a field. We first show that every nonzero prime ideal is a maximal ideal. Write such a prime ideal as P=(p) with p=0, and suppose P⊆(a). Then p=ab for some b∈R. Since ab∈P, either a∈P, in which case (a)=P, or b∈P. In the second case b=pc for some c, and p=apc gives 1=ac by multiplicative cancellation in an integral domain. Thus (a)=R. This proves maximality.
Since R is an integral domain, (0) is a prime ideal, and every other prime ideal is maximal. Consequently every prime chain has length at most one. For the opposite inequality, choose a nonzero nonunit a, which exists because R is not a field. The proper principal ideal (a) is contained in a maximal ideal m; existence follows from Zorn's lemma applied to the proper ideals containing (a). A maximal ideal is a prime ideal, since its quotient ring is a field. Therefore (0)⊊m is a prime chain of length one. Thus the Krull dimension of a principal ideal domain that is not a field is
dimR=1.​
(30)

Let P be a prime ideal of R disjoint from the multiplicatively closed set Y. In the localization S=Y−1R, its extension of an ideal is
SP={p/y:p∈P, y∈Y}.
(31)
Indeed, finite sums of such fractions have a common denominator in Y and numerator in P, and every generator p/1 has this form.
We show that its contraction of an ideal recovers P:
{r∈R:r/1∈SP}=P.​
(32)
One inclusion is immediate. Conversely, if r/1=p/y with p∈P and y∈Y, equality of fractions in the field of fractions of the integral domain R gives yr=p∈P. Since y∈/P and P is a prime ideal, r∈P.
If SP1​=SP2​, contracting would therefore give P1​=P2​, contrary to the hypothesis. Hence
SP1​=SP2​.​
(33)
We will also need the fact that SP is a prime ideal, part of the prime ideal correspondence for localization. It is proper by the contraction identity, since otherwise 1∈P. If (a/u)(b/v)∈SP, multiplication by the denominator gives ab/1∈SP, and contraction gives ab∈P. Thus a∈P or b∈P, so one of the two fractions belongs to SP.

(0)⊊(p)⊊(p,t)

To prove the upper bound, suppose there were a prime chain of length at least three. Since Z[t] is an integral domain, we could insert (0) at its beginning if necessary and select a chain
(0)⊊P1​⊊P2​⊊P3​.
