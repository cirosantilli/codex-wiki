An ideal q is p-primary if it is a proper primary ideal whose radical of an ideal is p. Explicitly,
q​=p,ab∈q, a∈/q ⟹ bn∈q for some n≥1.​
(1)
Equivalently, ab∈q and b∈/p imply a∈q: multiplication by every element outside p is injective on the quotient ring R/q.

A minimal primary decomposition is a finite expression
I=j=1⋂t​qj​​
(2)
in proper primary ideals such that the prime ideals pj​=qj​​ are pairwise distinct and no primary ideal can be omitted. More explicitly, for every j,
⋂i=j​qi​⊆qj​.
(3)
Thus minimality means both irredundancy and distinct radicals of ideals; it does not assert that the primary ideals themselves are uniquely determined. For I=R, one may use the empty intersection convention.

In the terminology of a minimal primary decomposition, an associated prime of a primary decomposition of I is a prime ideal occurring as qj​​ in such a decomposition. The first uniqueness theorem for primary decomposition says that this collection is independent of the chosen minimal primary decomposition. Among these prime ideals, an isolated prime of a primary decomposition is minimal under inclusion, equivalently a minimal prime over an ideal I; an embedded prime ideal strictly contains another member of the collection. Thus
isolated=minimal over I,embedded=not minimal over I.​
(4)
When R is a Noetherian ring, these are precisely the associated primes of a module R/I. In that case the equivalent definition is
p=AnnR​(x+I)=(I:x)for some x∈/I,
(5)
where (I:x) is a colon ideal. The decomposition terminology applies when a minimal primary decomposition exists; a general ring need not provide one for every ideal.

Suppose first that a is m-primary. Since R is a Noetherian ring, choose a finite generating set of an ideal m=(x1​,…,xd​). The equality of radicals of ideals a​=m supplies positive integers ei​ with xiei​​∈a. Set
n=1+∑i=1d​(ei​−1).
(6)
Every product of n generators has some xi​ appearing at least ei​ times. Such products generate the product of ideals mn, so mn⊆a. Also a⊆a​=m. If m=0, take n=1 directly.
Conversely, suppose mn⊆a⊆m. Taking radicals of ideals gives
m=mn​⊆a​⊆m.
(7)
Hence a​=m. The only maximal ideal containing a is m: any such maximal ideal contains a​ and therefore equals m. By the correspondence theorem for ideals, the quotient ring R/a is a local ring with maximal ideal m/a. Every element outside this maximal ideal is a unit.
If ab∈a and a∈/a, then b cannot be outside m, since multiplying by its inverse in the quotient ring would give a∈a. Thus b∈m and bn∈a, proving the required primary ideal condition. We have proved the maximal-primary ideal criterion
a is m-primary⟺mn⊆a⊆m for some n≥1.​
(8)

Put A=C[X,Y], P=(X) and Q=(X2,Y). The quotient ring A/P≅C[Y] is an integral domain, so P is a prime ideal and hence a primary ideal. Put m=(X,Y). Then
m2=(X2,XY,Y2)⊆Q⊆m.
(9)
The polynomial ring A is a Noetherian ring by the Hilbert basis theorem, and m is a maximal ideal since A/m≅C. Therefore the maximal-primary ideal criterion from c makes Q m-primary. In particular its radical of an ideal is m, distinct from P.
Neither primary ideal can be omitted: X∈P∖Q, while Y∈Q∖P. Thus the given intersection is already a minimal primary decomposition. To express the ideal by generators, write an element of P∩Q as Xh=X2u+Yv. Reducing modulo the prime ideal (X) gives Yvˉ=0 in the integral domain C[Y], so v=Xw. Cancelling X in the integral domain A gives h=Xu+Yw. Hence
J=(X2,XY)=(X)∩(X2,Y).​
(10)
By the first uniqueness theorem for primary decomposition, the associated primes of a module A/J are exactly the two radicals of ideals. Their strict inclusion P⊊m gives
AssA​(A/J)={(X),(X,Y)},(X) isolated,(X,Y) embedded.​
(11)
One can also see both associated primes of a module directly through annihilators:
AnnA​(Y+J)=(J:Y)=(X),AnnA​(X+J)=(J:X)=(X,Y).
(12)
For the first colon ideal, reducing hY∈J modulo (X) forces h∈(X), and the reverse inclusion follows from XY∈J. For the second colon ideal, cancel X in hX∈X(X,Y). Both represented elements of the quotient module A/J are nonzero.

Because (r) is a proper prime ideal, each (rk) is proper and its radical of an ideal is
(rk)​=(r)=p.
(13)
Indeed, (rk)⊆(r) gives one inclusion of radicals of ideals, while (rc)k∈(rk) gives the other.
We prove the saturation condition for a primary ideal: if ab∈(rk) and a∈/p, then b∈(rk). For k=1 this is the defining property of a prime ideal. For k>1, write ab=rkc. Since a∈/(r), the prime ideal condition gives b=rb1​. The element r is a non-zero-divisor, so cancellation gives
ab1​=rk−1c.
(14)
Induction gives b1​∈(rk−1), hence b∈(rk). Together with the computed radical of an ideal, this proves
(rk) is p-primary for every k≥1.​
(15)

Since the radical of an ideal q​ is (r), some positive power of r belongs to q. Choose the least such exponent k. Then (rk)⊆q and rj∈/q for 0≤j<k, with r0=1 excluded because the primary ideal q is proper.
Take x∈q. If x∈/(rk), choose the largest j∈{0,…,k−1} for which x∈(rj), and write x=rju. Maximality of j implies u∈/(r)=q​: otherwise x∈(rj+1), a contradiction, including when j=k−1. The primary ideal condition applied to rju∈q therefore forces rj∈q, contrary to the choice of k.
Consequently every x∈q lies in (rk), and
q=(rk),k=min{n≥1:rn∈q}.​
(16)
Together with i, this classifies the primary ideals for a regular principal prime. The exponent is unique: if (rk)=(rk+1), then rk=rk+1c, and cancellation of the non-zero-divisor rk would give 1=rc, contradicting that (r) is proper. No Noetherian hypothesis is needed.

The image f/1 is a unit in the localization at a prime ideal Rp​, because f∈/p. The universal property of localization, applied to the ring homomorphism R→Rp​ and the multiplicative subset {1,f,f2,…}, gives a unique ring homomorphism fixing the image of R:
θf​:Rf​⟶Rp​,θf​(a/fn)=a/fn.​
(17)
Fixing the image of R is exactly the required compatibility with the algebra over a ring structures. Uniqueness also follows directly: such a ring homomorphism must send 1/f to the inverse of f/1, and these elements together with R generate Rf​.

Consider the kernel of a ring homomorphism
K=ker(R⟶Rp​)={a∈R:sa=0 for some s∈/p}.
(19)
Since R is a Noetherian ring, this ideal has a finite generating set of an ideal, say K=(a1​,…,at​). For each i, choose si​∈/p with si​ai​=0, and put
f=∏i=1t​si​.
(20)
Then f∈/p by the prime ideal condition, and fK=0. If K=0, take the empty product f=1.
If a/fn maps to zero under the ring homomorphism θf​ from a, then a/1=0 in Rp​ because f/1 is a unit there. Hence a∈K, so fa=0. The vanishing criterion in a module localization now gives a/fn=0 already in Rf​. Therefore
kerθf​=0,θf​:Rf​↪Rp​.​
(21)
This is the finite generation clears localization kernels argument: the finite generating set of an ideal permits a single element outside p to annihilate the entire kernel of a ring homomorphism.

For n≥1, let Kn​=ker(ϕn), where the power denotes repeated composition of the ring endomorphism. These ideals form an ascending chain:
K1​⊆K2​⊆K3​⊆⋯.
(22)
The ascending chain condition for a Noetherian ring gives KN​=KN+1​ for some N≥1. Take a∈kerϕ. Since ϕ is surjective, its iterate ϕN is surjective, so choose b∈R with ϕN(b)=a. Then
ϕN+1(b)=ϕ(a)=0,
(23)
so b∈KN+1​=KN​. Therefore a=ϕN(b)=0.
Thus the kernel of a ring homomorphism ϕ is zero. This proves that a surjective endomorphism of a Noetherian ring is an automorphism, and in particular
ϕ is injective.​
(24)

Use the direct product of rings
T=∏n≥0​R,
(25)
with coordinatewise addition and multiplication and unit (1,1,…). Define the shift
g(a0​,a1​,a2​,…)=(a1​,a2​,a3​,…).
(26)
Coordinatewise operations show that g preserves addition, multiplication and the unit, so it is a ring endomorphism. It is surjective because (0,b0​,b1​,…) maps to any prescribed (b0​,b1​,…). Its kernel of a ring homomorphism is
kerg={(a,0,0,…):a∈R}=0,​
(27)
so it is not injective. This is the noninjective surjective shift on a product of fields.
This ring fails the Noetherian hypothesis in c. Indeed, let en​ have a single 1 in coordinate n. The ideals In​=(e0​,…,en​) form the strictly ascending chain
I0​⊊I1​⊊I2​⊊⋯,
(28)
since en+1​∈/In​.

A ring is reduced if its only nilpotent element is zero. Equivalently,
xn=0 for some n≥1 ⟹ x=0,or (0)​=0.​
(29)
The second expression says that its nilradical vanishes. A reduced ring may have zero divisors; for example, the direct product of rings R×R is reduced but (1,0)(0,1)=0.

Write N=(0)​. Every element of the extension of an ideal NRp​ is a fraction a/s with a∈N and s∈/p: a finite sum of such fractions has a common denominator and numerator still in the ideal N. If an=0, then (a/s)n=0, so
NRp​⊆(0)Rp​​​.
(30)
Conversely, let a/s be a nilpotent element of the localization at a prime ideal Rp​. For some n≥1, an/sn=0. The vanishing criterion in a module localization supplies t∈/p with tan=0. It follows that
(ta)n=tn−1(tan)=0,
(31)
so ta∈N. Since ts∈/p,
sa​=tsta​∈NRp​.
(32)
This proves that the nilradical commutes with localization:
Nil(Rp​)=NRp​.​
(33)
The multiplication by t is necessary: the numerator a of a nilpotent element in a localization need not itself be nilpotent in R.

If R is reduced, its nilradical N is zero. By b, every localization at a prime ideal has zero nilradical, so in particular Rm​ is reduced for every maximal ideal m.
Conversely, suppose every such localization is reduced. Take a nilpotent element x∈R. Then x/1 is nilpotent in every Rm​, hence zero there. To see directly that x=0, suppose otherwise and consider the proper annihilator
AnnR​(x)={a∈R:ax=0}.
(34)
Choose a maximal ideal m containing this annihilator. The equality x/1=0 in Rm​ would imply sx=0 for some s∈/m, by the vanishing criterion in a module localization. But then s∈AnnR​(x)⊆m, a contradiction. This is the principle that localization detects zero elements. Therefore
R is reduced⟺Rm​ is reduced for every maximal ideal m.​
(35)
The argument also includes the zero ring, which is reduced and has no maximal ideals.

Let N be the nilradical of R. By b, the reduced locus of a ring is
R={p∈SpecR:NRp​=0}.
(36)
The Noetherian ring hypothesis makes the ideal N finitely generated; write N=(n1​,…,nt​). Fix p∈R. For every i, the vanishing criterion in a module localization gives si​∈/p with si​ni​=0. The product f=s1​⋯st​ lies outside the prime ideal p and satisfies fN=0. When N=0, take f=1.
For every prime ideal q in the distinguished open set
D(f)={q∈SpecR:f∈/q},
(37)
the element f/1 is a unit in Rq​. The equality fN=0 therefore implies NRq​=0, so Rq​ is reduced. Thus p∈D(f)⊆R, proving that R is a Zariski-open subset of the spectrum of a ring.
More precisely, let A=AnnR​(N), the annihilator of a module N. The argument shows that NRp​=0 if and only if some f∈A lies outside p. Hence
R=f∈AnnR​(N)⋃​D(f)=SpecR∖V(AnnR​(N)).​
(38)
Equivalently, the complement is the support of a module N. This is vanishing of a finitely generated module near a prime applied to N: its finite generation lets the separately chosen denominators be combined into one element annihilating all of N.

Set L=M/aM, a quotient module. The exactness of localization gives, for every maximal ideal m,
Lm​≅Mm​/aRm​Mm​.
(39)
If m⊇a, the hypothesis Mm​=0 makes this quotient module zero. If m⊇a, choose s∈a∖m. Its image in Rm​ is a unit, so aRm​=Rm​ and the displayed quotient module is again zero. Thus
Lm​=0for every maximal ideal m.
(40)
For completeness, if 0=ℓ∈L, its annihilator is a proper ideal, and hence lies in a maximal ideal m. The vanishing criterion in a module localization shows ℓ/1=0 in Lm​: any element annihilating ℓ lies inside m. This contradicts the vanishing just proved. Hence L=0, and
M/aM=0,aM=M.​
(41)
The use of localization detects zero elements is valid for every module; neither finite generation nor a Noetherian hypothesis is needed.
