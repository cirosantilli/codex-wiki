Kepler's third law (T2=4π2a3/[G(M+m)])

 Home Physics Mathematical physics Kepler's laws of planetary motion
OurBigBook.com Words: 302 Articles: 1
For an isolated two-body problem in Newtonian gravity, the relative elliptic orbit's orbital period T obeys
T2=G(M+m)4π2a3​=μ4π2a3​.
(29)
Here a is the relative semi-major axis, and M+m is the sum of the two masses. The separation r varies during an elliptic orbit, so replacing a by r works only for a circular orbit, where r=a. Likewise, either body's individual semi-major axis about the centre of mass must first be converted to the relative one. When m is negligible compared with M, the denominator can be approximated by GM.
For fixed gravitational parameter, the orbital period depends on the semi-major axis and is independent of orbital eccentricity. Our a=1, μ=1 example gives T=2π both for e=0.6 and for a circular orbit with e=0. The eccentric body's rapidly changing speed compensates for its different path. A compact way to see the cancellation uses Kepler's second law: divide the ellipse's area πab by its areal velocity h/2. With b=a1−e2​ and specific angular momentum h=μa(1−e2)​, the result is 2πa3/μ​.
Compare two separate Kepler orbits with the same gravitational parameter, a1​=1 and a2​=4:
T1​T2​​=(a1​a2​​)3/2=43/2=8.
(30)
With μ=1, their orbital periods are 2π and 16π; measured in units of T1​, these are 1 and 8. Quadrupling the size therefore takes eight times as long to complete a circuit. This power law makes comparisons possible without first choosing physical units.
The same relation can be inverted to weigh a system: M+m=4π2a3/(GT2). An observed relative semi-major axis and orbital period determine the total mass, while additional information is needed to separate the two masses. This is useful for binary stars, as discussed in NASA, Orbits and Kepler's Laws: measuring masses. Geometry and a clock together become a weighing instrument.
