Quotient topological space (X/∼)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Topology Quotient topology
OurBigBook.com Words: 605 Articles: 2
A quotient topological space is a quotient set equipped with the quotient topology. For a concrete gluing, give the closed interval I=[0,1] its usual subspace topology, identify 0∼1, and leave every interior point in its own singleton equivalence class. The resulting quotient space is a circle: the two endpoints become one point, while interior points remain distinct.
The exact continuous map to the unit circle in the plane is
p(t)=(cos(2πt),sin(2πt)),pˉ​([t])=p(t).
(18)
Here sine and cosine give p(0)=p(1)=(1,0), and p(s)=p(t) holds exactly when s=t or {s,t}={0,1}. Thus p induces a bijection pˉ​:I/∼→S1. [ref] The universal property of the quotient topology makes pˉ​ continuous. The quotient of a compact space is compact, and the unit circle is Hausdorff, so the compact-to-Hausdorff continuous bijection theorem makes pˉ​ a homeomorphism. This verifies the topology, not merely the point identifications.
To inspect an open neighbourhood of the seam, choose 0<ε<1/2 and let Uε​=p([0,ε)∪(1−ε,1]). Its full preimage is
p−1(Uε​)=[0,ε)∪(1−ε,1].
(19)
Both pieces are open in the subspace topology on I, so Uε​ is open in the circle. In contrast, A=[0,ε) is open in I, but
p−1(p(A))=[0,ε)∪{1}
(20)
is not open in I: it contains 1 without any neighbourhood of 1. Hence p(A) is not open in the circle. A quotient map can therefore fail to be an open map.
For a continuous function f:I→R, the only gluing condition is f(0)=f(1). For example, f(t)=cos(2πt) defines a continuous function on the circle, whereas f(t)=t does not. This lets us use an interval as a coordinate for a periodic function while making the seam invisible. More general quotient spaces encode glued geometric pieces in the same way: describe continuous maps before gluing and check that their values agree on every equivalence class.
The Hausdorff property need not survive a quotient topology. In the non-Hausdorff quotient of the real line by rational translation, x∼y means x−y∈Q. A nonempty open preimage W must contain all translates by rational numbers of any open interval inside it. The density of the rational numbers then forces W=R: for any y, some rational number r has y−r in that interval. Thus this quotient space has the indiscrete topology. The irrationality of the square root of two gives distinct equivalence classes for 0 and 2​, so this quotient space is not Hausdorff. Unlike the endpoint-gluing circle, this gluing identifies a dense subset with each point.
