The raw natural filtration is
FtB​=σ(Bu​:0≤u≤t).​
(1)
Equivalently it is the smallest sigma-algebra making every observation Bu​, u≤t, measurable. As s≤t enlarges the set of observations, FsB​⊆FtB​. It is the smallest filtration to which the process is adapted. If the usual conditions for a filtration are imposed, take the completion by the probability-zero sets and its right-continuous augmentation. Brownian independent increments remain valid for this usual augmentation. Adding arbitrary future information is not part of the generated filtration.

If X is Brownian motion with respect to the given filtration, independent Gaussian increments give the identity immediately.
Conversely, fix s<t and let Δ=Xt​−Xs​. Taking expectations gives its characteristic function e−ξ2(t−s)/2, so Δ∼N(0,t−s). To prove independence, for any A∈Fs​ define the finite measure νA​(E)=P(A∩{Δ∈E}). The conditional identity gives
∫eiξuνA​(du)=P(A)e−ξ2(t−s)/2.
(9)
Uniqueness of Fourier transforms of finite measures implies νA​=P(A)N(0,t−s). This is independence of Δ from Fs​. Iterating over times proves independent increments. Together with the stated continuity and X0​=0, this gives standard Brownian motion.
Here a process with the stated filtration is understood to be adapted, as required when saying it is Brownian with respect to that filtration. If adaptedness is not implicit, it must be added: an ordinary Brownian motion with a trivial filtration satisfies the displayed conditional characteristic function but is not adapted to that filtration.

Lévy characterization of Brownian motion says that a continuous local martingale M with M0​=0 is standard Brownian motion with respect to its filtration if its quadratic variation is ⟨M⟩t​=t. Equivalently one may require Mt2​−t to be a local martingale.
Any weak solution has the integral form Zt​=∫0t​sgn(Zs​−2)dWs​. Its bounded integrand makes it a continuous local martingale starting at zero, and
⟨Z⟩t​=∫0t​sgn(Zs​−2)2ds=t.
(10)
The specified value of the sign at zero has square one as well. Lévy characterization of Brownian motion therefore proves that every such weak solution is Brownian motion. This is a statement about its law and filtration; it does not assert Z=W or provide a strong solution as a function of a specified driver.

i OurBigBook.com
First take the intended annulus configuration 0<R~<R<R^ with R≥1. The first stopping time is finite by the allowed fact, and continuity gives ∣ZHR​​∣=R. After HR​, the annulus exit time is bounded by the exit time of the outer disc, which is almost surely finite; use the Strong Markov property at HR​. Thus HR,R~,R^​<∞ almost surely.
The function log∣z∣ is harmonic on this annulus. Its stopped value is a bounded martingale, so optional stopping theorem and continuity give, if p is the probability of hitting the inner circle first,
logR=plogR~+(1−p)logR^.
(25)
Consequently
p=log(R^/R~)log(R^/R)​.​
(26)
For fixed R~<1, one may take R=1 and let R^→∞. The probability of hitting the inner circle before the outer one tends to one. The probability of ever hitting that inner circle is at least each of these probabilities, hence is one. Taking the countable intersection for R~=1/n, n≥2, proves visits to balls of arbitrarily small radius almost surely.
The source permits R<1 as well. Then HR​=0 and the actual starting radius for the next exit is one, rather than R. More generally put s=max(1,R). If R^>s, the answer for the literal definition is log(R^/s)/log(R^/R~). If R^≤s, the infimum defining the next exit is already HR​ almost surely and the inner-circle probability is zero. Positive R~ is implicit in an annulus and is necessary for the logarithmic calculation.

If z=w, apply translation, rotation and Brownian scaling to
Zt​=1+z−wZ∣z−w∣2t​−z​=z−wZ∣z−w∣2t​−w​.
(27)
This is standard planar Brownian motion started at one. Its almost-sure visits to arbitrarily small discs about zero translate to visits of Z to discs about w, with radii multiplied by ∣z−w∣. Therefore the same neighborhood recurrence holds for every pair z,w. If z=w, every such disc is already hit at time zero. If positive hitting times are requested, continuity gives positive times within every fixed disc as well. A countable sequence of shrinking radii suffices to obtain one probability-one event for this assertion.

For a continuous local martingale M starting at zero, Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequalities compare its maximal size with its quadratic-variation clock:
cp​E⟨M⟩Tp/2​≤Esupt≤T​∣Mt​∣p≤Cp​E⟨M⟩Tp/2​,p>0.
(28)
They remain valid with infinite values, by stopping and passage to the limit. Their significance here is that the horizontal and vertical martingales share the same stopping-time clock, so one coordinate's maximum can determine moments of the other and of the clock. For a process started away from zero, first subtract its initial value.
Starting at i, the vertical coordinate starts at one and is stopped on first hitting zero. For λ>1, it reaches level λ before zero with probability 1/λ, by optional stopping theorem of one-dimensional Brownian motion on [0,λ]. Thus
ρ(λ)={1,1/λ,​0<λ≤1,λ>1.​​
(29)
The tail integral formula gives
E(Y∗)p=p∫0∞​λp−1ρ(λ)dλ=1−p1​(0<p<1),
(30)
and it diverges for p≥1.
The stopped martingale Yt∧TH​​−1 has quadratic variation t∧TH​. Its maximum in absolute value is max(1,Y∗−1), between Y∗/2 and Y∗, so it has precisely the same finite positive moments as Y∗. BDG therefore gives ETHp/2​<∞ exactly for p<1. Applying BDG to Xt∧TH​​, which starts at zero and has the same clock, yields
Ei​(XTH​∗​)p<∞⟺0<p<1,Ei​THq​<∞⟺0<q<21​.​
(31)
Taking the displayed 1/p or 1/q powers does not change finiteness. In particular the exit time is almost surely finite while its mean is infinite.

First, planar Brownian motion started away from zero almost surely never hits zero. To see this without confusing it with neighborhood recurrence, stop in an annulus with inner radius ϵ and fixed outer radius R>∣Z0​∣. The preceding logarithmic calculation gives probability log(R/∣Z0​∣)/log(R/ϵ) of reaching the inner circle first. A hit of zero before the outer exit would require all these inner hits, so its probability is zero as ϵ↓0. On every finite path interval the continuous path is bounded; a countable union over integer outer radii and integer times proves the all-time assertion.
Consequently the integrand 1/Z is locally square integrable: on every compact time interval the nonvanishing continuous path has a positive minimum modulus. Write Z=X+iY with independent standard real coordinates. Complex quadratic covariation satisfies d[Z,Z]=dt−dt+2id[X,Y]=0, so analytic Itô's lemma has no second-derivative drift. For L=∫Z−1dZ one likewise has d[L,L]=0, and d[e−L,Z]=0. The product rule therefore gives
d(Zt​e−Lt​)=e−Lt​dZt​−Zt​e−Lt​dLt​=0.
(32)
Its initial value is Z0​, proving, simultaneously for all times by continuity,
Zt​=Z0​eLt​.​
(33)
Thus ℜLt​=log(∣Zt​∣/∣Z0​∣) and ℑLt​ is the continuous accumulated change of complex argument of the path about zero, measured in radians. It is a winding angle, without reduction modulo 2π. A single-valued logarithm on the whole punctured plane is unnecessary; the path has a continuous stochastic logarithm of planar Brownian motion.

Write Z0​=r0​eiα0​ with 0<r0​<1 and choose its initial argument once. The exponential map in the source is a covering of the punctured disc, rather than a bijective map onto the full disc. Its pathwise inverse is the continuous lift
Wt​=−i(logr0​+iα0​+Lt​)=α0​+θt​−ilog∣Zt​∣.
(34)
For t<TD​ this lies in the upper half-plane, and at the disc exit it lies on the real line. Local conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion, or the quadratic variation of its analytic derivative −i/Z, shows that Wt​ is planar Brownian motion run at the clock
At​=∫0t​∣Zs​∣−2ds.
(35)
The disc exit time is finite almost surely. The path avoids zero on the compact interval through its exit, so its minimum modulus there is positive and ATD​​<∞. Before this terminal clock time the lifted imaginary coordinate is positive, and at it the coordinate is zero. Hence it is exactly the upper-half-plane exit time of the time-changed Brownian motion, not an additional arbitrary stopping time.
The starting point of the lift is α0​+ib with b=−logr0​>0. Its real exit coordinate has Cauchy distribution b/[π((s−α0​)2+b2)]. Since that coordinate is α0​+θTD​​, we obtain the stronger exact law
θTD​​ ∼ Cauchy(0,−log∣Z0​∣).​

(37)
There is no contradiction with the terminal point lying on the compact unit circle: reducing its argument modulo 2π loses the number and direction of turns accumulated before exit.

Using the Cauchy distribution from the preceding part gives one common probability space for all starting points:
ϕ(x+iy)=E[f(x+yC)](y>0).
