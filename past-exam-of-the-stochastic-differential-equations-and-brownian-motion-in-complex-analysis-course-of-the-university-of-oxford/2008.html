X∼N(0,1/3),P(X>0)=21​.​
(1)

Use a filtration with respect to which B is Brownian. The process is adapted and continuous because it is a continuous function of Bt​ and t. The moment-generating function gives
EMt​=1,EMt2​=e−α2tEe2αBt​=eα2t<∞.

Lévy's characterization of Brownian motion states that a real continuous local martingale M with M0​=0 and quadratic variation ⟨M⟩t​=t is a standard Brownian motion with respect to its filtration. Equivalently, its increments are independent of the past and have centered normal distribution with variances equal to elapsed times.
Let Hs​=sgn(supr≤s​Br​−1). The running maximum is continuous and adapted, so H is bounded and progressively measurable process. It has a predictable version for integration; changing its value at the single switching time does not change its stochastic integral. Thus X=∫HdB is a continuous local martingale starting at zero. Since Hs2​=1,
⟨X⟩t​=∫0t​Hs2​ds=t.
(5)
Lévy's characterization of Brownian motion yields X is standard Brownian motion. The sign depends on the entire past through the running maximum, but the unit quadratic-variation rate is exactly what the characterization needs.

The drift coefficient b(z)=rz and diffusion coefficient a(z)=σz are Lipschitz continuity and have linear growth. The standard existence and pathwise uniqueness theorem for stochastic differential equations therefore supplies a unique global strong solution of a stochastic differential equation, adapted to the driving Brownian filtration.
Apply Itô's formula to xexp((r−σ2/2)t+σBt​). Its time derivative contributes (r−σ2/2)Xt​ and its Itô correction contributes σ2Xt​/2, giving the required equation and initial value. Hence
Xt​=xe(r−σ2/2)t+σBt​.​
(6)
In particular the solution stays positive. For t>s, factor out Xs​ and use independence and the normal law of Bt​−Bs​:
E(Xt​∣Fs0​)=er(t−s)Xs​.​
(7)
The exponential moment exists, so this conditional expectation is well-defined for every finite s,t.

(Lf)(z)=rzf′(z)+21​σ2z2f′′(z).​

Fix a finite horizon T and use the completed natural Brownian filtration. Martingale representation theorem states that every F∈L2(FT​) can be written
F=EF+∫0T​Hs​dBs​,E∫0T​Hs2​ds<∞,
(14)
where H is predictable and is unique up to equality for dsdP almost every (s,ω).
The Martingale representation theorem states that every square-integrable martingale M in this filtration has a continuous version of the form
Mt​=M0​+∫0t​Hs​dBs​,0≤t≤T,​
(15)
with the same integrability and uniqueness condition. The completed natural filtration has only null-set randomness at time zero, so M0​ is almost surely constant. The first statement gives the second by representing MT​ and taking conditional expectations; the second gives the first by taking Mt​=E(F∣Ft​).
There is also the local version: every local martingale in the Brownian filtration has a continuous version representable by a predictable, locally square-integrable integrand. The filtration restriction matters; an enlarged filtration with independent additional martingales need not have representation using B alone.

Apply Itô's formula to Mt​=eNt​−⟨N⟩t​/2. The quadratic-variation correction cancels the finite-variation term:
dMt​=Mt​dNt​,M0​=1.
(21)
Localization of the integrand proves that M is a nonnegative continuous local martingale.
For completeness, Conditional Fatou lemma says that for nonnegative random variables Yn​,
E(liminfYn​∣Fs​)≤liminfE(Yn​∣Fs​).
(22)
Choose a localizing sequence τn​↑∞ such that each stopped M is a martingale. Fatou first gives EMt​≤1. For s<t, its conditional form then gives
E(Mt​∣Fs​)≤liminfn​Ms∧τn​​=Ms​.
(23)
Thus M is a supermartingale.
A sufficient condition for it to be a true martingale on [0,T] is Novikov's condition
Eexp(21​⟨N⟩T​)<∞.​
(24)
Under this condition the stochastic exponential is a uniformly integrable martingale on that finite interval. Requiring it for every finite T gives the martingale property at all finite times; it does not automatically give uniform integrability as t→∞.

A finite-horizon form of Girsanov theorem is as follows. If θ is predictable, ∫0T​θs2​ds<∞ almost surely, and its stochastic exponential M is a true martingale of mean one, then under the probability dQ=MT​dP the process
Bt​−∫0t​θs​ds,0≤t≤T,
(29)
is Brownian motion. The same conclusion holds on the completed natural filtration, since a strictly positive density gives equivalent probabilities.
Part (b) establishes precisely the required density martingale and integrand θs​=∂x​logp(1−s,Bs​,y). Therefore
Xt​=Bt​−∫0t​1−sy−Bs​​ds is Q-Brownian on [0,T].​
(30)
In particular it is the requested local martingale. Equivalently, under Q, B solves dBt​=(y−Bt​)(1−t)−1dt+dXt​, the Brownian bridge drift coefficient toward y at time one. Keeping T<1 avoids the singular endpoint in this density change.

Write Z=X+iY, with Y a real Brownian motion started at one. For the nontrivial strip calculation assume r>1, so the starting point is in its interior. The first exit time is
TS​=inf{t≥0:Zt​∈/S}.
(31)
For a continuous Brownian path started inside, this equals the first hitting time of Y=0 or Y=r almost surely: Brownian motion started at a boundary immediately visits the exterior, so using the closed strip in the definition does not change the infimum.
If TS​>n, then Yn​∈[0,r]. Its normal density is bounded by (2πn)−1/2, hence
P(TS​>n)≤P(0≤Yn​≤r)≤2πn​r​⟶0.
(32)
Thus the exit time is finite almost surely.
The stopped harmonic coordinate Yt∧TS​​ is a martingale bounded between zero and r. bounded convergence theorem and optional stopping theorem give EYTS​​=Y0​=1. Since the exit coordinate is zero or r,
P(YTS​​=r)=r1​,P(YTS​​=0)=1−r1​.​
(33)
If T is the first hit of the real axis, exit through the lower boundary implies T<∞, so P(T<∞)≥1−1/r for every r>1. Letting r→∞ proves
P(T<∞)=1.​
(34)
The strip assumption is worth making explicit: at r=1 the initial point is the upper boundary and exit is immediate; for 0<r<1 it starts outside the strip and the displayed interior probabilities do not apply.

ii OurBigBook.com
planar Brownian motion is recurrence of planar Brownian motion when, almost surely, it returns to every nonempty open neighborhood infinitely often, at arbitrarily large times. This does not mean that it hits each specified point; a prescribed point is polar for planar Brownian motion.
recurrence of planar Brownian motion and continuity imply that all the circle-hitting times Sk​,Tk​ in the question are finite. Starting on radius ϵ, it eventually visits the exterior of radius 2ϵ and must cross that circle; it later visits the interior of radius ϵ and must cross back. Each duration Δk​=Tk​−Sk​ is strictly positive by path continuity, because the two radii are separated.
At Sk​, the Strong Markov property restarts Brownian motion at its current point on the inner circle. rotational invariance of Brownian motion makes the law of the subsequent first exit time from the radius-2ϵ disk independent of that starting angle. Its conditional distribution given the past is therefore the same fixed law at every cycle, proving that the Δk​ are independent and identically distributed.
To prove divergence of the conformal clock for any nonconstant entire f, choose a point z0​ with f′(z0​)=0. By continuity, there exist ρ,c>0 such that ∣f′(z)∣2≥c throughout the closed disk ∣z−z0​∣≤2ρ. Use the same recurrence of planar Brownian motion circle cycles centered at z0​, with radii ρ,2ρ. On each segment from the inner circle to the outer circle, the path remains in that disk, giving
∫0∞​∣f′(Zs​)∣2ds≥c∑k≥0​(Tk​−Sk​).
(35)
The durations are positive iid variables. If their mean has not been computed, apply the strong law of large numbers to the bounded iid variables min(Δk​,1): their mean is strictly positive, so their sum, and hence ∑Δk​, diverges almost surely. Therefore
∫0∞​∣f′(Zs​)∣2ds=∞almost surely.​
(36)
Centering the disks at a noncritical point avoids an unjustified positive lower bound on ∣f′∣ around the original origin.
Write f=u+iv. Harmonicity and Itô's formula make u(Zt​),v(Zt​) continuous local martingales. The Cauchy-Riemann equations give equal brackets ∫0t​∣f′(Zs​)∣2ds and zero mutual bracket. Consequently Lévy's characterization of Brownian motion after time change of a continuous process makes W a planar Brownian motion started at f(Z0​), with no finite clock lifetime because the clock just proved is unbounded. The clock is strictly increasing: otherwise f′ would vanish along a nonconstant path interval, contradicting its isolated zeros.
For the notation in the source, ∣∇f∣2 is interpreted as the conformal scale ∣f′∣2=∣∇u∣2=∣∇v∣2. If it instead means the sum of the squared magnitudes of both complex coordinate derivatives, it is 2∣f′∣2, and the resulting W runs at half the usual Brownian speed. This convention does not change divergence of the integral. The mechanism is recurrent disk occupation makes an entire conformal clock infinite.

Take λ>0 and let σλ​ be the first time X hits λ. Reflect the horizontal coordinate after σλ​, leaving the vertical coordinate unchanged. The Strong Markov property and symmetry preserve the law of planar Brownian motion and leave its real-axis exit time unchanged. On paths with σλ​<τ, the reflection swaps the two terminal events Xτ​<λ and Xτ​>λ. There is no terminal atom at λ. Hence the Brownian reflection principle gives
Pi​(supt≤τ​Xt​>λ)=2Pi​(Xτ​>λ)=π2​∫λ∞​1+x2dx​.​
(37)
Horizontal symmetry gives the same probability for −infX>λ. A union bound therefore yields
Pi​(X∗>λ)≤π4​∫λ∞​1+x2dx​.​
(38)
Also X∗≥∣Xτ​∣, whose Cauchy distribution is 2π−1∫λ∞​(1+x2)−1dx. Thus the maximum tail is bounded above and below by positive constants times λ−1 for large λ. The tail integral formula for moments for positive moments gives
E(X∗)p=p∫0∞​λp−1P(X∗>λ)dλ.
(39)
The large-λ integral is finite exactly when 0<p<1; it diverges for p≥1. Near zero its upper bound by ∫01​pλp−1dλ is finite.
The vertical coordinate remains nonnegative up to exit and starts at one. The strip calculation, now with upper boundary λ≥1, gives
P(Y∗>λ)=λ1​.
(40)
Thus Y∗ has density λ−2 on [1,∞), and in fact
E(Y∗)p=1−p1​ (p<1),E(Y∗)p=∞ (p≥1).​
(41)
For completeness, if the source's p<1 includes negative powers, X∗ also has all negative moments. Let ση​ be the first horizontal exit from (−η,η). Then
P(X∗≤η)≤P(τ≤η)+P(ση​≥η)≤Ce−c/η(0<η≪1).
(42)
The first bound follows from the Gaussian reflection bound for the vertical hit of zero; the second follows by splitting time into blocks of length η2, each having a fixed positive chance of exiting the horizontal interval. This small-value estimate integrates against every negative power. At p=0 both moments equal one.

For 0<α<2, write z=reiθ with 0<θ<π. The chosen power map sends this to rαeiαθ, so
D={reiθ:r>0, 0<θ<απ}.​
(43)
At α=2 this is the plane slit along the nonnegative real axis. For every 0<α≤2, the map is conformal on the upper half-plane.
To check finiteness of the physical exit time, start a Brownian motion B at i and let T be its real-axis exit. The time change of a continuous process in conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion is
AT​=∫0T​α2∣Bs​∣2α−2ds.
(44)
We know T<∞ almost surely. The Cauchy exit law from a Brownian half-plane assigns probability zero to BT​=0. On such a path, Bs​=0 throughout the compact interval [0,T], because it is in the open upper half-plane before exit and has nonzero endpoint. Its modulus therefore has a positive minimum and finite maximum. The displayed integrand is bounded on that interval, proving AT​<∞. The image Brownian motion exits D at this finite changed time.
The endpoint α=0 printed in the source is degenerate: z0=1, so its image is the singleton {1}, not an open domain, and the map is not conformal. If exit from that set is defined for Brownian motion started at one, its exit infimum is zero. It cannot be included in a nondegenerate conformal-domain argument.
Here is the martingale argument for maximal comparisons, including the starting-value issue. For P=f+ig, the Cauchy-Riemann equations and Itô's formula show that
Ft​=f(Zt∧τ​)−f(iα),Gt​=g(Zt∧τ​)−g(iα)
(45)
are orthogonal continuous local martingales with the same quadratic variation. Localize before large disks and before exit. The Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequalities then give
E(G∗)p≤Cp​E(F∗)p,p>1,
(46)
with a constant depending only on p; Fatou's lemma removes the localization. These are centered martingales. For the uncentered processes this implies
Esup∣g(Zt​)∣p≤Cp′​(Esup∣f(Zt​)∣p+∣g(iα)∣p),
(47)
since ∣f(iα)∣≤sup∣f(Zt​)∣.
The source normalizes P(0)=0 at a boundary point, rather than the value of g at the starting point. Consequently its displayed constant cannot be uniform in α with only p dependence. To see this, take P(z)=iz and 0<α<min(1/2,1/p). Then f=−Imz and g=Rez. Inside this narrow wedge,
sup∣f(Zt​)∣≤tan(απ)sup∣g(Zt​)∣.
(48)
Both pth moments are finite and the right one is positive; finiteness follows from the wedge radial-maximum tail of order R−1/α, justified below. The source inequality would force Cp​≥cotp(απ), unbounded as α↓0. At α=0, the immediate-exit interpretation gives the direct contradiction 1≤Cp​0 for the same polynomial.
For a fixed nondegenerate wedge, an uncentered inequality does hold with a constant allowed to depend on α as well as p. One rigorous outline for the polynomial class in this question is as follows. Separation of variables in a sector of radius R gives, for exit through its circular arc, the expansion
Preiθ​(hit radius R before the sides)=π4​∑n≥1n odd​​n1​(r/R)n/αsin(nθ/α).
(49)
Thus at the specified starting point the radial-maximum tail is comparable to R−1/α. Hitting any fixed interior subarc has that same power, with positive leading coefficient. If a nonzero polynomial has degree d, its real part has magnitude at least cRd on some fixed interior subarc for sufficiently large R. Therefore its maximal real-part pth moment is infinite whenever dp≥1/α. In that case the desired extended-real inequality is automatic.
The remaining polynomials have d<1/(αp), so belong to a finite-dimensional vector space space. On that space
P⟼(Esup∣f(Zt​)∣p)1/p
(50)
is a genuine norm: if it is zero, Brownian motion's positive probability of reaching any interior neighborhood forces ReP=0 throughout D, so analyticity makes P a purely imaginary constant, and P(0)=0 makes it zero. The corresponding imaginary-part functional is bounded on this finite-dimensional vector space normed space; its finiteness follows from dp<1/α and the radial tail bound. equivalence of norms in finite dimensions gives
Esup∣g(Zt​)∣p≤Cp,α​Esup∣f(Zt​)∣p(0<α≤2).​
(51)
This establishes the fixed-domain interpretation and identifies exactly why the universal centered martingale inequality cannot silently be applied to the printed uncentered boundary normalization. It is the distinction in starting-value obstruction in wedge conjugate maximal inequalities.
