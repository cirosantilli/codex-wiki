Let V=∑n≥1​∥Xn​∥∞​. It is a nonnegative extended random variable, and EV<∞ implies P(V<∞)=1. Otherwise its expectation would be infinite. Intersect this event with the countably many probability-one events on which the individual sample paths are continuous.
For every path in that intersection, ∑n​∥Xn​(⋅,ω)∥∞​<∞. The Weierstrass M-test gives absolute and uniform convergence of ∑n​Xn​(t,ω) on the entire stated time domain. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. Therefore Y has continuous paths almost surely. The finite expectation is used to obtain pathwise summability before applying this deterministic theorem.

In the Lévy construction of Brownian motion, each level is a continuous piecewise linear sum of tents with disjoint interiors. Its supremum norm is therefore the largest absolute tent height, not the sum of the heights. With the standard construction's height standard deviation 2−(n+2)/2,
∥Xn​∥∞​=2−(n+2)/2Mn​=21​2−n/2Mn​.
(9)
Part (c) makes E∑n​∥Xn​∥∞​ finite, and part (a) proves almost sure uniform convergence to a continuous path on [0,1]. The separately chosen linear endpoint term completes the construction; its Gaussian finite-dimensional distributions and covariance must also be checked to identify the limit as Brownian motion. Independent copies on successive unit intervals extend it to the half-line.
The source calls 2−(n+2)/2 a variance. Literally using that variance would give height standard deviation 2−(n+2)/4 instead. The same O(n​) maximum estimate still proves uniform convergence, since ∑n​2−n/4n​<∞, but the resulting covariance is not that of the standard Lévy construction. The standard midpoint Brownian bridge variance on an interval of length 2−n is 2−n/4=2−(n+2). Thus continuity follows under either summable scaling, while Brownian covariance requires the correct variance.

For a continuous semimartingale X and a C1,2 function f, Itô's formula is
df(t,Xt​)=ft​(t,Xt​)dt+fx​(t,Xt​)dXt​+21​fxx​(t,Xt​)d[X]t​.
(10)
For Brownian motion B, d[B]t​=dt. With f(t,x)=e−t/2coshx, the drift cancels:
ft​+21​fxx​=−21​e−t/2coshx+21​e−t/2coshx=0.
(11)
Consequently
d(e−t/2coshBt​)=e−t/2sinhBt​dBt​.​
(12)
The stochastic-integral representation makes this a local martingale; localization handles unbounded B. In fact it is the average of the two positive exponential Brownian martingales eBt​−t/2 and e−Bt​−t/2, so it is a true martingale on every finite time interval.

The two-dimensional Itô formula gives the local martingale part of u(Xt​,Yt​) as ∫0t​ux​dX+∫0t​uy​dY, with all derivatives evaluated along (Xs​,Ys​). Independent Brownian coordinates have brackets [X]t​=[Y]t​=t and [X,Y]t​=0. Hence the quadratic variation is
⟨u(X,Y)⟩t​=∫0t​(ux2​+uy2​)(Xs​,Ys​)ds.​
(13)
Similarly, the quadratic covariation with v(X,Y) is
⟨u(X,Y),v(X,Y)⟩t​=∫0t​(ux​vx​+uy​vy​)(Xs​,Ys​)ds.
(14)
Sufficient pointwise conditions are equal gradient lengths and perpendicular gradients:
ux2​+uy2​=vx2​+vy2​,ux​vx​+uy​vy​=0.
(15)
The Cauchy-Riemann equations ux​=vy​, uy​=−vx​ imply both. These are the quadratic covariations of an analytic Brownian image; harmonicity is needed to remove the drift, but the two bracket identities themselves follow just from the displayed derivative relations.

A uniformly bounded local martingale is a true martingale: stop at a localizing sequence and use dominated convergence in its conditional-expectation identities. Its deterministic bound also gives uniform integrability. The uniformly integrable martingale convergence theorem therefore yields an integrable limit Z∞​ with convergence in L1 and
EP​Z∞​=EP​Z0​=1,EP​(Z∞​∣Ft​)=Zt​.
(18)
Since Z∞​≥0, the Radon-Nikodym derivative defines a positive measure of total mass one:
Q(Ω)=1.​
(19)
The limit can vanish on a set of positive P measure, so global equivalence is not required. At each finite time, however, the density is the strictly positive Zt​.

Write the integral with an explicit dummy time:
Xt​=Mt​−∫0t​Zs−1​d⟨M,Z⟩s​.
(20)
On every compact time interval, strict positivity and continuity of Z make its reciprocal locally bounded; the covariation has locally finite variation. Thus X is a well-defined continuous semimartingale.
Apply the Itô product rule. Subtracting a finite-variation process does not change covariation, so [Z,X]=[Z,M]. The finite-variation terms cancel:
d(ZX)​=XdZ+ZdX+d[Z,X]=XdZ+ZdM−d[M,Z]+d[Z,M]=XdZ+ZdM.​
(21)
Thus ZX is a local martingale under P.
For the transfer step, choose stopping times increasing to the horizon that bound X, M, reciprocal Z, and the brackets needed for the stochastic integrals. For each such time σ, the same product calculation with Xσ shows that Zt​Xt∧σ​ is a true P martingale. The part after σ is simply the bounded held value Xσ​ times the density martingale. By the Bayes formula for conditional expectation, for s≤t,
EQ​(Xt∧σ​∣Fs​)=Zs​EP​(Zt​Xt∧σ​∣Fs​)​=Xs∧σ​.
(22)
The stopping times exhaust the horizon also under Q≪P. Hence X is a continuous Q local martingale, proving this form of the Girsanov theorem without assuming a positive terminal density everywhere.

Conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion means invariance after changing the clock, not preservation of the original speed. If f:D→D′ is a conformal bijection and Z is planar Brownian motion stopped on exiting D, put
At​=∫0t∧TD​​∣f′(Zs​)∣2ds.
(23)
Then f(Zt​) has the law of planar Brownian motion started at f(Z0​) and run at time At​, up to the corresponding lifetime. Equivalently, composing with the inverse clock produces ordinary Brownian motion in D′.
Indeed, the real and imaginary parts of a holomorphic function are harmonic, so Itô's formula gives zero drift. The Cauchy-Riemann equations give equal coordinate brackets At​ and zero cross bracket. The Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion after time change identifies the image. In particular the harmonic measure of boundary arcs is carried to their conformal images.

Continuity and the regularity of zero for a one-dimensional Brownian coordinate make S the first contact with the imaginary axis. The Strong Markov property and symmetry of the subsequent real increments imply that reversing those increments after S preserves the Brownian law. The imaginary coordinate is unchanged, so Z is again planar Brownian motion started at one.
Reflection preserves distance to the origin, hence TDr​​=TDr​​ pathwise. On TDr​​<S, the path exits through the right semicircle and reflection has not yet acted. On S<TDr​​, reflection reverses the sign of the exit real part. Thus
P(ReZT​>0)=P(T<S)+P(S<T,ReZT​>0),
(26)
P(ReZT​<0)=P(S<T,ReZT​>0).
(27)
The two circular points on the imaginary axis have zero exit probability, so they cause no extra term. Subtracting proves the reflection identity for Brownian exit from a half-disc requested in the question.
Both processes have the same exit law. With p=π−1tan−1((r2−1)/(2r)) from part (b),
P(TDr​​<S)=1−2p=π4​tan−1(1/r).​

A continuous nondecreasing function Φ with Φ(0)=0 has moderate growth if for some fixed a>1 and finite K,
Φ(ax)≤KΦ(x)(x≥0).
(29)
Iteration and monotonicity then give such an inequality, with a different constant, for every fixed dilation factor. Powers Φ(x)=xp, p>0, are standard examples.
The Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequalities say that for a real continuous local martingale M with M0​=0 and any stopping time τ, there are positive finite constants cΦ​,CΦ​, depending only on Φ, such that
cΦ​EΦ(⟨M⟩τ​​)≤EΦ(Mτ∗​)≤CΦ​EΦ(⟨M⟩τ​​).​
(30)
Here Mτ∗​=sups≤τ​∣Ms​∣. Expectations may be infinite, with the inequalities understood by localization. For Φ(x)=xp, these compare the pth moment of the running maximum with the p/2th moment of quadratic variation.

Suppose first that d=∣z0​∣>ϵ. Apply part (a) with inner radius ϵ and outer radius R>d:
P(hit radius ϵ before radius R)=log(R/ϵ)log(R/d)​⟶1(R→∞).
(39)
Each event implies finite hitting time of the ϵ circle, so that hitting probability is one. If d=ϵ, the hitting time is zero. If d<ϵ, it is the exit time from a bounded disk. The martingale ∣Zt​−z0​∣2−2t, stopped before that exit, gives a finite mean exit-time bound (ϵ2−d2)/2. Thus the asserted hitting time is almost surely finite in this case too.
For alternating visits, choose two closed smaller disks Ki​ strictly inside the given open disks Di​, so their distance is positive. Define successive first hitting times of K1​,K2​,K1​,K2​,…. Translation invariance and the Strong Markov property apply the just-proved hitting result from every successive starting point. A countable intersection makes all these times finite almost surely, with the required disk memberships.
These increasing times cannot accumulate at a finite time. On a compact time interval a continuous Brownian path is uniformly continuous, whereas every consecutive visit must cross the fixed positive distance between K1​ and K2​. Therefore
Tn​↑∞almost surely.​
(40)
This is the recurrence of planar Brownian motion in neighborhoods; it does not assert that every individual point is hit.

Assume that a nonconstant polynomial f has no zero. Then g=1/f is entire. The leading polynomial term makes ∣f(z)∣→∞ as ∣z∣→∞, so g(z)→0 there. On any remaining compact disk, continuity and absence of zeros bound 1/∣f∣. Consequently g is bounded on the entire plane.
Part (c)(i) makes u(Zt​)=Reg(Zt​) a bounded local martingale, hence a true bounded martingale. The martingale convergence theorem says it converges almost surely as t→∞.
The harmonic function u cannot be constant. Otherwise the Cauchy-Riemann equations would make both derivatives of Img zero, so g and then f would be constant. Choose points z1​,z2​ with u(z1​)<u(z2​). Continuity supplies two disjoint disks on which the ranges of u are separated by a positive gap. By part (b), the Brownian path visits these disks alternately at times tending to infinity. The corresponding subsequences of u(Zt​) then remain separated, contradicting its almost sure convergence.
The zero-free assumption is impossible. Hence
Every nonconstant complex polynomial has a complex root.
This proves the fundamental theorem of algebra using recurrence and martingale convergence, without invoking Liouville's theorem as a prior algebraic shortcut.
