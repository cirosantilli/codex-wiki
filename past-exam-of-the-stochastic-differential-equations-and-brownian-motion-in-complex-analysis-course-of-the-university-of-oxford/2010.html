There is a printed domain error: Rez>0 defines the right half-plane. Taken literally, continuity forces ReZTH​​=0 almost surely, so its law is a point mass at zero. The displayed density belongs to the intended upper half-plane Imz>0, with b>0. We prove that intended assertion explicitly.
Write Zt​=a+Xt​+i(b+Yt​) for independent standard Brownian motions. The exit time T is the first time Y hits −b, and depends only on Y. Its density, by the supplied Brownian first-passage time law, is b(2πt3)−1/2e−b2/(2t). Conditional on T=t, the real exit coordinate is normally distributed with mean a and variance t. Independence therefore gives
h(x)=2πb​∫0∞​t−2exp(−2tb2+(x−a)2​)dt.
(16)
With u=[b2+(x−a)2]/(2t), the integral is 2/[b2+(x−a)2]. Thus the Cauchy exit law from a Brownian half-plane is
h(x)=π[b2+(x−a)2]b​.​
(17)
For the literal right half-plane, the corresponding nondegenerate coordinate is ImZTH​​, whose density is a/{π[a2+(y−b)2]} for a>0.

If f:D→D′ is a conformal map, the image of planar Brownian motion in D is Brownian motion in D′ after the increasing clock change
At​=∫0t​∣f′(Zs​)∣2ds.
(18)
More precisely, f(ZA−1(u)​) is planar Brownian motion, starting at f(Z0​) and run up to the relevant exit time. The Itô formula and the Cauchy-Riemann equations give image-coordinate local martingales with common bracket A and zero cross bracket; the Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion identifies the time-changed process. In particular, conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion transports exit locations and harmonic measure, while changing traversal speed.

For z=x+iy in the strip,
f(z)=cosh(πx/a)cos(πy/a)+isinh(πx/a)sin(πy/a),
(19)
so Imf(z)>0. This is a bijective conformal map onto the upper half-plane. One way to verify bijectivity is to factor it as w=eπz/a, followed by (w+w−1)/2. The first map gives Imw>0, ∣w∣>1. The two inverse roots of the second map have product one; for any point in the upper half-plane exactly one root has modulus greater than one, and its imaginary part is positive because Im[(w+w−1)/2]=(1−∣w∣−2)Imw/2.
The three boundary pieces map as follows: the lower edge goes to (1,∞), the upper edge to (−∞,−1), and the vertical edge to (−1,1). Thus the desired exit event becomes exit from the upper half-plane to the left of −1. Write
A=cosh(πp/a)cos(πq/a),B=sinh(πp/a)sin(πq/a)>0.
(20)

P(ImZTS​​=a)=∫−∞−1​π[B2+(x−A)2]B​dx=21​−π1​arctanB1+A​.​

The strip exit is finite almost surely, since its imaginary coordinate exits (0,a) in finite time; corners have probability zero because individual points are polar points for planar Brownian motion. This is the Top-edge exit probability of a Brownian half-strip.

The disk exit time is finite almost surely; for example, either coordinate of the planar Brownian motion eventually leaves (−1,1). By path continuity, the exit lies on the unit circle. Starting at the origin, rotational invariance of planar Brownian motion makes the exit law invariant under every circle rotation. The unique rotation-invariant probability measure is normalized arc length. Consequently ZTD​​ is uniform on the unit circle, or equivalently its angle has density 1/(2π) on [0,2π).

a OurBigBook.com
Use first that planar Brownian motion avoids a fixed point, so the path starting at 1 never reaches zero. Its continuous logarithmic lift, initialized at zero, has real part Ut​=log∣Zt​∣ and imaginary part the continuous unwrapped angle Θt​. Local branches of the complex logarithm, patched along the path, and the Itô formula show that both coordinates are continuous local martingales with
⟨U⟩t​=⟨Θ⟩t​=∫0t​∣Zs​∣2ds​=:τ(t),⟨U,Θ⟩t​=0.
(30)
The clock is finite on every compact time interval, since a continuous nonvanishing path stays a positive distance from zero there. It is continuous and strictly increasing. Moreover τ(∞)=∞: by recurrence, planar Brownian motion spends infinite total time in a fixed disk, and inside the unit disk the clock integrand is at least one. This is the divergence of a positive planar Brownian occupation integral.
The common-clock time change of orthogonal local martingales, using the Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion, supplies independent Brownian coordinates ζ1,ζ2 such that Ut​+iΘt​=ζτ(t)​. Exponentiating gives
Zt​=exp(ζτ(t)​),ζ0​=0.​
(31)
To identify the inverse clock, write A=τ−1. The derivative of an inverse function and the representation just obtained give
A′(s)=∣ZA(s)​∣2=e2ζs1​,A(s)=∫0s​e2ζu1​du.
(32)
Consequently
τ(t)=∫0t​∣Zs​∣−2ds=inf{s≥0:∫0s​e2ζu1​du>t}.​
(33)
The strict inequality in this generalized inverse has the same infimum as the unique inverse value, since A is continuous and strictly increasing onto the half-line.

Assume 0<r<1<R, as required for the annulus and logarithms. The logarithmic radius of planar Brownian motion is a local martingale. Stopping at T=Tr,R​ keeps it between the constants logr and logR, so the stopped process is a bounded martingale. The exit time is finite almost surely because the annulus is bounded and Brownian motion eventually exits its containing disk.
The optional stopping theorem followed by dominated convergence gives Elog∣ZT​∣=log∣Z0​∣=0. Continuity puts the exit radius at either r or R. If p is the inner-exit probability, then plogr+(1−p)logR=0. Hence
p=logR−logrlogR​.​
(34)
Stopping before taking expectations matters: the unstopped logarithmic-radius process is a strict local martingale, so one cannot assume its unrestricted expectation is constant.

The second coordinate ζ2 is one-dimensional Brownian motion, whose zero set is unbounded almost surely by recurrence. The clock τ and its inverse are continuous, increasing, and unbounded, so the unwrapped angle Θt​=ζτ(t)2​ has zeros at arbitrarily large original times.
To ensure the times are stopping times for the original Brownian filtration, define directly
T0​=0,Tn​=inf{t≥Tn−1​+1:Θt​=0}.
(35)
The logarithmic lift makes Θ a continuous adapted process. Hitting a closed set after a stopping time is a stopping time under the usual filtration conditions, and the unbounded zero set proves inductively that each Tn​ is finite almost surely. Since Tn​≥Tn−1​+1, the sequence tends to infinity, and continuity gives
ζτ(Tn​)2​=ΘTn​​=0for every n.​
(36)
Geometrically, the Brownian path returns to the positive real ray at arbitrarily late times with zero net unwrapped winding angle about the origin. The assertion is stronger than principal argument zero, which also allows any integer number of completed turns.

logt2Θt​​ dt→∞​ C,fC​(x)=π(1+x2)1​.​
