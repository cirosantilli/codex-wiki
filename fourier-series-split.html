Fourier series (f(x)∼a0​/2+∑n≥1​[an​cos(nx)+bn​sin(nx)])

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 4k Articles: 67
A Fourier series describes a periodic function through sines and cosines whose frequencies are integer multiples of one base frequency. These Fourier harmonics give a useful alternative to describing the value at every input: each Fourier coefficient measures how much of one oscillating pattern is present. For a real, integrable function of period 2π, the real form is
f(x)∼2a0​​+∑n=1∞​[an​cos(nx)+bn​sin(nx)],an​=π1​∫−ππ​f(x)cos(nx)dx,bn​=π1​∫−ππ​f(x)sin(nx)dx.
(1)
The cosine formula includes n=0; the sine formula uses n≥1. Thus a0​/2 is the mean value over a period, not a0​ itself. The symbol ∼ associates a function with its Fourier coefficients; an equality requires a specified kind of convergence, such as pointwise convergence. For general period T>0, replace nx by 2πnt/T and the coefficient prefactor by 2/T. Fourier orthogonality explains why integrating against one mode removes the others. [ref].
For a finite example, take
f(x)=21​+cosx+21​sin(2x).
(2)
Its mean is 1/2, its real Fourier coefficients are a0​=1, a1​=1, b2​=1/2, and all other an​,bn​ vanish. At x=π/4, the three contributions add to 1/2+1/2​+1/2=1+1/2​≈1.70710678. The function is already a trigonometric polynomial, so retaining these modes reconstructs it exactly. Here x is an angular coordinate in radians; if it instead represents time, a base angular frequency must supply the corresponding units.
The change of viewpoint is useful because many linear problems act separately on these modes. The second derivative maps sin(nx) to −n2sin(nx) and maps cos(nx) in the same way. Consequently a differential equation that is complicated for a whole profile can become a simpler calculation for each Fourier harmonic. A linear time-invariant system similarly has a separate response to each complex exponential. Superposition combines the responses when the relevant sums and operations are justified. [ref].
The infinite case contains a subtlety absent from the finite example. Under pointwise convergence of a piecewise smooth Fourier series, the value at a jump discontinuity is the average of the two one-sided limits. For a square-integrable function, the complex Fourier series instead converges in the L2 norm: the integrated squared error tends to zero. Neither statement says that the error becomes small everywhere at once. A square wave shows the difference vividly through the Gibbs phenomenon.
A historical source for this connection between profiles and modes is Joseph Fourier's 1822 book Théorie analytique de la chaleur. Chapter III develops expansions in sines and cosines while studying the propagation of heat. The heat equation gives a continuing application of this approach. [ref].
