For a Matrix Lie group, Tg​G consists of derivatives γ′(0) of differentiable curves in G with γ(0)=g, viewed inside Mn​(C) as a real vector space. At the identity, multiplying two such curves adds their derivatives, and reparametrizing a curve multiplies its derivative by any real scalar. Thus L(G)=TI​G is a real vector subspace.
If Y∈L(G) and g∈G, conjugating a curve with derivative Y proves gYg−1∈L(G). Let g(s) have g(0)=I and g′(0)=X∈L(G). Differentiating the curve g(s)Yg(s)−1 inside the finite-dimensional, hence closed, vector subspace L(G) gives
XY−YX∈L(G).
(1)
The matrix commutator is real bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the Jacobi identity by associativity of matrix multiplication. This proves that L(G) is a real Lie algebra.
The exponential-map theorem for a linear Lie group says expX∈G whenever X∈L(G). Applying it to every real multiple of X gives exp(tX)∈G for every real t. Conversely that curve has derivative X at zero, so X∈L(G). Hence
X∈L(G)⟺exp(tX)∈G for every t∈R​.
(2)

Write gC​=L(GC​) and h=L(H). A root is a nonzero weight α∈h∗ of the adjoint action. Its root space is
gα​={Y∈gC​:[Z,Y]=α(Z)Y for all Z∈h}.
(9)
At the group level these weights come from nontrivial characters of the Cartan subgroup acting by conjugation. The Cartan root-space decomposition is
gC​=h⊕⨁α∈Φ​gα​.
(10)
The zero-weight space is h; for the complexification of a compact group the algebra is reductive, with any centre contained in this zero-weight space.
For the product in question,
GC​=SL2​(C)×SL2​(C),gC​=sl2​(C)⊕sl2​(C).
(11)
Take the diagonal Cartan subalgebra generated by H1​=(diag(1,−1),0) and H2​=(0,diag(1,−1)). On Z=xH1​+yH2​, define α1​(Z)=2x and α2​(Z)=2y. The roots are exactly
Φ={α1​,−α1​,α2​,−α2​}​.
(12)
If E=E12​ and F=E21​, their four root spaces are respectively C(E,0), C(F,0), C(0,E), and C(0,F). These four lines and the two-dimensional diagonal subalgebra form the full six-dimensional decomposition. There are no mixed roots: the two direct summands commute. This is the root system A1​×A1​.

a OurBigBook.com
With the convention applicable also to disconnected compact groups, the Weyl group is W=NG​(T)/T, where the normalizer consists of elements taking T to itself by conjugation. Put t=L(T) and Λ=ker(exp:t→T). This is a full lattice with T≅t/Λ.
If ψ is a torus automorphism, exponential compatibility gives ψ(expX)=exp(DψI​X). Therefore DψI​ sends Λ into itself; applying the inverse proves it sends the lattice onto itself. In a lattice basis this derivative is an element of GLr​(Z).
Conjugation by a connected subgroup Q⊂NG​(T) induces a continuous family of these integer matrices. Since GLr​(Z) is discrete, this family is constant and equals its value at the identity. Its action on t is therefore trivial, and surjectivity of the torus exponential shows that Q centralizes T.
Apply this to the identity component NG​(T)0. It lies in G0 and centralizes T. The allowed maximal-abelian property of T in G0 gives NG​(T)0⊆T, while the connected group T lies in that identity component. Thus NG​(T)0=T. The normalizer is a closed compact Lie subgroup, so its component quotient is discrete and compact, hence finite. Consequently W is finite and discrete. For disconnected G, its action on the lattice need not be faithful; the component argument is essential.
The distinction is recorded in Weyl group of a disconnected compact Lie group.

Use the given torus of independent rotations in n mutually orthogonal two-planes and a fixed final axis. A normalizer element permutes the two-plane rotation characters, and on each plane either preserves or reverses its orientation. Its induced action therefore permutes the n angle coordinates and changes their signs independently.
Every such signed permutation is realized. Permutations of two-dimensional blocks have determinant one. A reflection in any chosen plane reverses that angle; reflecting the remaining one-dimensional axis as necessary restores total determinant one. The kernel consists precisely of independent plane rotations: commuting with a generic torus element forces preservation of its distinct rotation planes, and the determinant condition then fixes the final axis. Thus the kernel is T and
W≅(C2​)n⋊Sn​,∣W∣=2nn!​.
(15)
This is the signed permutation group, the Weyl group of type Bn​.

As usual here, representations are finite-dimensional and continuous. Start with any positive definite Hermitian form h0​ and normalized Haar measure μ on the compact group. Define
h(v,w)=∫G​h0​(gv,gw)dμ(g).
(16)
This is Hermitian, sesquilinear, and positive definite: for v=0 its continuous integrand h0​(gv,gv) is strictly positive. Bi-invariance of Haar measure shows h(av,aw)=h(v,w) for every a∈G.
If U⊂V is invariant, then so is its orthogonal complement: for v⊥U and u∈U, h(av,u)=h(v,a−1u)=0. Hence V=U⊕U⊥ as a representation. Induct on dimension, splitting whenever a nontrivial proper invariant subspace exists. This proves every such representation is a direct sum of irreducible representations.

Schur orthogonality for irreducible complex characters of a compact group, with normalized Haar measure, is
⟨χα​,χβ​⟩:=∫G​χα​(g)χβ​(g)​dμ(g)=δαβ​.
(17)
Equivalent representations clearly have equal characters, since trace is invariant under conjugation. Conversely complete reducibility gives χV​=∑α​mα​χα​, where the nonnegative integer multiplicity is recovered by mα​=⟨χV​,χα​⟩. Equal characters thus give equal multiplicities of every irreducible constituent, hence equivalent representations.

The usual Weyl character formula requires a compact connected Lie group. Choose a maximal torus T, a positive root system Φ+, Weyl group W, and ρ=21​∑α∈Φ+​α. Irreducible representations are indexed by dominant integral highest weights λ belonging to the character lattice of the actual group T. For X∈L(T) such that expX is regular,
χλ​(expX)=∑w∈W​det(w)ewρ(X)∑w∈W​det(w)ew(λ+ρ)(X)​​.
(18)
Weights are written as linear functionals on the complexified torus algebra; their values on the compact real torus algebra are imaginary. The denominator is eρ(X)∏α>0​(1−e−α(X)). If ρ is not itself a character of T, the individual expressions are interpreted on its covering algebra; their quotient is single-valued and descends to T. The apparent singularities at nonregular elements are removable, and conjugacy into a maximal torus determines the character on the whole connected group.
The formula explicitly determines all irreducible characters from their highest weights, and its limit at the identity gives the dimension formula
dimVλ​=∏α>0​⟨ρ,α∨⟩⟨λ+ρ,α∨⟩​.
(19)
Character multiplication and orthogonality then compute tensor-product multiplicities. For SU2​, highest weights are integers m≥0, and at diag(z,z−1),
χm​=z−z−1zm+1−z−m−1​=zm+zm−2+⋯+z−m,dimVm​=m+1.
(20)
Connectedness cannot simply be omitted from this version. For a nontrivial finite group the maximal torus is trivial, yet characters can differ away from the identity. The root data on that torus alone cannot supply the characters of all disconnected compact groups.

One definition of Tx​M is the space of derivations at x: linear maps v:C∞(M)→R satisfying v(fh)=f(x)v(h)+h(x)v(f). In local coordinates u1,…,un, each has the unique form v=∑i​vi∂/∂ui∣x​, identifying Tx​M with Rn. Equivalently it is the space of velocities of curves through x, identifying curves with the same coordinate derivative.
For a chart ϕ:U→Rn, give TM∣U​ coordinates (x,v)↦(ϕ(x),Dϕx​v). If F=ψ∘ϕ−1 is a base transition, the induced transition is
(u,v)⟼(F(u),DFu​v).
(21)
This is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse coming from the reverse chart. The resulting bundle atlas makes TM a smooth manifold of dimension 2n. These local product charts also give the usual Hausdorff and second-countable topology, inherited from a countable base atlas and its fibre coordinates.
For n≥1, identify nonzero vectors in each fibre up to nonzero real scalar multiplication. Locally this gives P(TM)∣U​≅U×RPn−1. In a projective chart where vj=0, use the ratios vi/vj for i=j. Under a base change, the fibre line maps by [v]↦[DFu​v]; its projective coordinates are ratios of smooth linear expressions with nonzero denominator on the overlap. This defines a smooth manifold of dimension n+(n−1)=2n−1.
For the sphere, a point is a pair (x,L) with ∣x∣=1 and an unoriented line L⊂x⊥. Choose a unit v∈L. The triple (x,v,x×v) is a positively oriented orthonormal frame. Given a second pair, the unique orientation-preserving linear map between chosen frames lies in SO3​ and carries the first pair to the second. Thus the action is transitive.
A rotation stabilizing (x,L) fixes x, so rotates its tangent plane. To preserve the unoriented line it must act by v↦v or v↦−v, giving exactly the identity and a half-turn about x. Hence the stabilizer has order two, and P(TS2)≅SO3​/C2​.
This is an example of the projectivized tangent bundle.

In a coordinate chart, define the exterior derivative by
d(∑I​aI​dxi1​∧⋯∧dxir​)=∑I​daI​∧dxi1​∧⋯∧dxir​.
(22)
The chain rule makes this independent of coordinates. It is real linear, increases form degree by one, agrees with the differential on functions, satisfies d2=0, and obeys the graded Leibniz rule
d(α∧β)=dα∧β+(−1)rα∧dβ.
(23)
It also commutes with pullback by a smooth map. These properties characterize it locally and hence globally.
de Rham cohomology is the quotient of closed forms by exact forms. If dα=dβ=0, Leibniz gives d(α∧β)=0. Changing α to α+dη changes the product by d(η∧β); changing β to β+dξ changes it by (−1)rd(α∧ξ). Thus
[α][β]=[α∧β]​
(24)
is independent of representatives. This gives the graded-commutative de Rham cohomology ring.

For compact connected oriented n-manifolds without boundary, the degree is characterized by
∫M​F∗η=deg(F)∫N​η
(29)
for every smooth top-degree form η. Equivalently, at any regular value y,
deg(F)=∑x∈F−1(y)​signdetDFx​.
(30)
The preimage is finite by compactness and the inverse function theorem. Taking a form supported in a small neighbourhood of a regular value and applying change of variables proves the signed-count formula. The top de Rham cohomology of a compact connected oriented manifold is one-dimensional, with integration giving its coordinate; pullback and Stokes theorem make the integral characterization independent of the chosen normalized form. Thus this signed count is independent of the regular value. In particular a nonzero degree forces surjectivity, and the number of points in any regular fibre is at least the absolute degree. Degree is invariant under homotopy, as follows by applying Stokes theorem to the pullback on M×[0,1].
For the polynomial assertion take k≥1. The map P:C→C extends smoothly to a map of the Riemann sphere fixing infinity: in reciprocal coordinates its target coordinate is
P(1/ζ)1​=1+a1​ζ+⋯+ak​ζkζk​.
(31)
The homotopy Pt​(z)=zk+t(a1​zk−1+⋯+ak​) extends smoothly in the same way. The map z↦zk has k distinct inverse images of the regular value one, each orientation-preserving, since the real Jacobian of multiplication by a nonzero complex derivative is its squared absolute value. Its degree is k, so degP=k=0. It is onto the sphere and must attain zero at a finite point. This proves every nonconstant complex polynomial has a root, without assuming the result to count roots of a general polynomial.
For a proper smooth F:Rn→Rn, choose a compactly supported n-form η with ∫η=1 and define
degF=∫Rn​F∗η.
(32)
Properness makes its pullback compactly supported. If η′ is another choice, the given result gives η−η′=dβ with compact support. The difference of the defining integrals is ∫d(F∗β)=0, proving independence. Sard's theorem supplies a regular value. A proper map has finitely many preimages of it, and a sufficiently small neighbourhood has no additional inverse sheets outside the chosen inverse-function neighbourhoods: otherwise a sequence of extra preimages would have a convergent subsequence by properness and limit in that fibre. A normalized form there therefore gives the same finite signed-count formula, proving that the degree is an integer.
Finally x↦xk is proper for k≥1. For odd k, a positive regular value has one inverse image with positive derivative. For positive even k, it has two inverse images with opposite derivative signs. Consequently
deg(x↦xk)={1,0,​k≥1 odd,k≥2 even.​​
(33)
For k=0 it is the constant map one, whose inverse image of the compact singleton {1} is all of R. It is not proper, so its degree is not defined by the proper-map construction requested here.

A Riemannian metric is a smooth positive definite symmetric bilinear form gq​ on each tangent space. The inverse form on the cotangent space defines the kinetic Hamiltonian
H(q,p)=21​gq−1​(p,p)=21​gij(q)pi​pj​.
(34)
It is smooth and satisfies H(q,ap)=a2H(q,p). With the canonical symplectic form Ω=∑i​dqi∧dpi​, define ιXH​​Ω=dH. This gives Hamilton's equations
q˙​i=∂pi​​H=gijpj​,p˙​i​=−∂qi​H.
(35)
Thus pi​=gij​q˙​j. Differentiating the inverse metric yields p˙​i​=21​∂i​gjk​q˙​jq˙​k. Differentiating pi​=gij​q˙​j and substituting gives
q¨​i+Γjki​q˙​jq˙​k=0.
(36)
Therefore the projections of the Hamiltonian integral curves are affinely parametrized geodesics, and lifting a geodesic by its metric covector gives a Hamiltonian integral curve.
For the Killing vector field define the fibrewise linear function f(q,p)=p(Yq​)=pi​Yi(q). Along the lifted geodesic it equals g(q˙​,Y). Metric compatibility and the geodesic equation give
dtd​g(q˙​,Y)=g(q˙​,∇q˙​​Y)=21​(LY​g)(q˙​,q˙​)=0.
(37)
Thus f is conserved. Equivalently the cotangent lift of the isometry flow preserves H, and its generator has Hamiltonian f, so their Poisson bracket vanishes.
For the final two metrics write s=ℓ(x)+m(y), restricting to an open set where the printed tensors are smooth nondegenerate metrics. Their inverse forms give
H1​=2spx2​+py2​​,H2​=2sm(y)px2​−ℓ(x)py2​​=m(y)H1​−21​py2​.
(38)
Along the H1​ flow, H1​ is constant, while
y˙​=py​/s,p˙​y​=2s2m′(y)​(px2​+py2​)=sm′(y)H1​​.
(39)
Hence
dtdH2​​=m′(y)spy​​H1​−py​sm′(y)H1​​=0​.
(40)
This is the Liouville metric geodesic integral, a quadratic conserved quantity. It does not require a Killing vector field; it arises from the separated metric coefficients. When both printed metrics are Riemannian, their positivity in particular requires s>0, m>0 and ℓ<0; the calculation also applies wherever the corresponding inverse tensors are defined.
