Mellin transform (M)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Integral transform
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 9
The Mellin transform is an integral transform suited to changes of scale: replacing f(x) by f(ax) multiplies its transform by a−s for a>0. For a locally integrable function f:(0,∞)→C, define
Mf(s)=∫0∞​xs−1f(x)dx,s=σ+iτ,
(8)
where the improper integral converges. The complex number xs−1=exp((s−1)logx) uses the real natural logarithm because x>0. Its absolute value is xσ−1, so absolute convergence depends on the real part σ rather than the imaginary part τ. The Mellin fundamental strip records the open range of such real parts.[ref]
A familiar example is the exponential function f(x)=e−x:
M{e−x}(s)=Γ(s),Res>0.
(9)
This is exactly the Gamma integral. Near zero e−x tends to one, so absolute convergence requires σ>0; at infinity xre−x→0 for every real r, so there is no upper boundary. The Gamma function has a meromorphic continuation with simple poles at 0,−1,−2,…, but a finite continued value elsewhere in σ≤0 does not make the defining improper integral converge.[ref]
The Mellin transform of an interval indicator gives an elementary example with absolute convergence for every complex number s. The Mellin transform in logarithmic coordinates explains why changes of scale become translations, connecting the scaling property of the Mellin transform to the Fourier transform.
