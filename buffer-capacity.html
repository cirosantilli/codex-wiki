Buffer capacity (β)

 Home Chemistry Acid-base chemistry Buffer solution
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
Buffer capacity measures resistance to a change in pH: how much additional chemical acid or chemical base a chemical solution accommodates per small change in pH. Its differential form is a local derivative, rather than the total inventory available before a buffer solution is exhausted. Two buffer solutions can have identical pH and very different buffer capacities.
Choose a constant volume V and fixed temperature. Let nB​ count added moles of fully dissociated, monovalent chemical base supplying hydroxide, and nA​ added moles of fully dissociated, monovalent strong acid supplying hydrogen ions. Neither addition supplies the weak acid component itself. The signed addition is b=(nB​−nA​)/V, with units molL−1, and
β=dpHdb​=V1​dpHdnB​​=−V1​dpHdnA​​.

The last two forms describe separate base-only and acid-only additions. The buffer capacity is positive for the stable chemical equilibrium model below and is reported in molL−1 per unit of pH. Since pH is dimensionless, the dimensional unit is just molL−1; stating “per pH” makes the measurement convention clear. Here constant volume is an idealization, realized approximately by small additions of concentrated reagent.
For a sufficiently small addition, ΔpH≈Δb/β. A large buffer capacity means a small local change of pH for a given addition. Over a finite interval, use the changing buffer capacity instead:
Δb=∫pH0​pH1​​β(p)dp.
(41)
This integral gives a finite dose in molL−1; it need not equal the initial buffer capacity times the eventual change in pH.
