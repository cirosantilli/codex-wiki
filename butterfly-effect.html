Butterfly effect

 Home Physics Branches of physics Dynamical systems Devaney chaos
OurBigBook.com Words: 676 Articles: 1
The butterfly effect is the intuitive idea that an arbitrarily small change in an initial condition can eventually change the observed future substantially. In dynamical systems, sensitive dependence makes this precise for a map F:X→X on a metric space (X,d): there is one separation scale δ>0 such that
∀x∈X ∀ε>0 ∃y∈X ∃n≥0:d(x,y)<ε,d(Fn(x),Fn(y))>δ.
(43)
The same δ works at every point, whereas y and the separation time n may depend on the point and the requested closeness. For a flow map, replace the iteration of a map Fn by Φt​ with some t≥0. The state space matters: restricting to a single fixed point removes the alternative nearby states needed for sensitivity.
Sensitivity is compatible with deterministic evolution. Given an exact initial condition, there is still one orbit; uncertainty concerns which exact state a measurement represents. It also allows accurate short-term prediction. For a continuous function F, each fixed finite iterated function Fn is continuous, so sufficiently close initial states remain close at any specified finite step. What fails is a single initial accuracy that controls every future step.
The logistic map illustrates both sides. At r=2.8, nearby orbits in the basin of attraction of its attracting fixed point approach one another. At r=4, arbitrarily close states can eventually separate by a macroscopic fraction of [0,1]. The definition asserts the existence of separating neighbours, not that every nearby pair must separate: x and 1−x have exactly the same next state under the logistic map and hence the same subsequent orbit. Nor must separation increase at every step.
A positive Lyapunov exponent describes average exponential growth of an infinitesimal displacement along an orbit, when the relevant limit exists. It is related to sensitivity but is not a substitute for its uniform definition. For a differentiable interval map, the chain rule gives
λ(x0​)=limN→∞​N1​∑j=0N−1​log∣F′(xj​)∣.
(44)
At r=4, the logistic map has λ=log2 for Lebesgue measure-almost every initial condition, while exceptional orbits can have different exponents. Finite-separation growth is only approximately exponential while the linear approximation remains useful. In a bounded state space, a separation cannot grow exponentially forever.
Sensitivity alone is also weaker than Devaney chaos, which additionally requires topological transitivity and dense periodic points. For example, F(x)=2x on the real line has sensitive dependence and a positive Lyapunov exponent, but its only periodic point is zero, so it is not Devaney-chaotic. This distinguishes growth of uncertainty from the richer organization of recurrent orbits.
Edward N. Lorenz's "Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow" (1963) studied a thermal convection model with three ordinary differential equations. Its numerical orbits exhibited persistent nonperiodic motion and instability to small changes of the initial condition. Lorenz discussed the consequences for very-long-range weather forecasting: a deterministic law need not make a detailed distant future reliably recoverable from approximate observations. The historically relevant lesson of the Lorenz system is this growth of uncertainty under deterministic evolution.[ref]
