Six inward-directed donor orbitals form symmetry-adapted linear combinations of a1g​+eg​+t1u​ symmetry. They mix respectively with metal 4s, (3dz2​,3dx2−y2​) and 4p orbitals. Thus the sigma framework contains six occupied bonding molecular orbitals and six corresponding antibonding molecular orbitals. The metal (dxy​,dxz​,dyz​) orbitals have no sigma-compatible partner and remain nonbonding t2g​ orbitals in this model. The important metal-dominated octahedral splitting is
Δo​=ε(eg∗​)−ε(t2g​).​
(1)
The eg∗​ orbitals point directly toward the donors; the t2g​ orbitals point between them. The original diagram shows the entire sigma framework, rather than treating the five metal d levels as the whole molecular-orbital diagram.
For the same donor set and approximately the same geometry, Δo​(CoIII)>Δo​(CoII). The higher metal charge usually shortens the metal–donor distances and strengthens the interaction that raises eg∗​. This comparison holds other chemical variables fixed: changing the ligand at the same time could reverse a comparison between two unrelated complexes.

A Co(III) centre has d6, and charge balance requires one closed-shell catecholate ligand and one S=1/2 semiquinonate ligand. For low-spin Co(III), t2g6​(eg∗​)0, the metal has S=0, giving an independent-spin magnetic moment of
μeff​/μB​=20+21​(21​+1)​=3​=1.732.
(3)
Thus A1 contains low-spin Co(III), SCo​=0, with its observed moment residing on the free radical ligand. High-spin Co(III), t2g4​(eg∗​)2, would have S=2 and give 27​=5.20 under the stated independent-spin approximation, so it is inconsistent with this measurement.
For Co(II), d7, charge balance instead requires two S=1/2 semiquinonate ligands. High-spin Co(II) has t2g5​(eg∗​)2 and S=3/2, so
μeff​/μB​=223​25​+2(21​23​)​=21​=4.583.
(4)
Therefore A2 contains high-spin Co(II), SCo​=3/2. Low-spin Co(II), t2g6​(eg∗​)1, would give 9​=3.00 when the two ligand spins are included. These four checks distinguish both oxidation state and metal spin.
The larger octahedral splitting at Co(III) exceeds the effective pairing energy and favours a low-spin complex; the smaller octahedral splitting at Co(II) favours a high-spin complex with occupation of both eg∗​ orbitals before further pairing. Interconversion also transfers an electron between cobalt and a dioxolene ligand, so this is valence tautomerism, rather than a metal-only spin change. Squared moments add here because the supplied model treats the centres as independent; adding the three spins first would be a different, exchange-coupled model.

water lies below pyridine and cyanide ligand in the spectrochemical series. At fixed metal and dioxolene charges, replacement by water decreases Δo​ and favours the high-spin limits: Co(II) remains S=3/2; Co(III) can become S=2 if the splitting falls below the pairing threshold. Treat each semiquinonate ligand as a symmetric O,O chelate, with the free radical delocalized over the dioxolene framework. The ideal octahedral donor-connectivity structures are one trans form and two mirror-related cis forms, giving three stereoisomers. Trans refers to the two pyridine N donors, not to the two O atoms of a chelate, which must remain adjacent.
In the diagram, coloured curves join the two O donors belonging to one ligand. For an unambiguous coordinate specification, the trans form has Py at +z,−z, with chelate pairs (+x,+y) and (−x,−y). A cis pair has Py at +z,+x, with chelates (−z,+y) and (−x,−y); reflecting y↦−y gives the other cis form. Proper rotations cannot superpose those two cis arrangements. Ring conformations and pyridine rotations are not extra donor-connectivity isomers.

v OurBigBook.com
The Co(III) centre in A1 has t2g6​(eg∗​)0: every occupied d subset is orbitally balanced. There is no first-order metal-centred Jahn–Teller driving force in A1. This does not require all six experimental bond lengths to be equal, since pyridine and dioxolene are different donors.
The Co(II) centre in A2 has t2g5​(eg∗​)2. Its two eg∗​ orbitals each contain one electron, so that subset gives no first-order preference. The t2g​ hole does give a weaker Jahn–Teller instability. To compare tetragonal distortions, take a positive small splitting parameter δ and retain each subset's orbital energy barycentre. On axial elongation let dxz​,dyz​ shift by −δ and dxy​ by +2δ. Five electrons then give
ΔEelong​=4(−δ)+1(2δ)=−2δ.
(5)
For equal-magnitude compression the shifts reverse, and filling the lower dxy​ orbital first gives
ΔEcompress​=2(−2δ)+3(δ)=−δ.
(6)
The two singly occupied eg∗​ orbitals contribute zero net shift in either case. Thus the elementary tetragonal splitting model favours elongation for high-spin d7, rather than equal electronic stabilization by the two signs. In a strictly sigma-only octahedral molecular orbital model t2g​ is nonbonding, so this weak effect is absent; it arises when the electrostatic and non-sigma interactions neglected in that diagram are restored. spin-orbit coupling, unequal donors and chelate constraints can modify the actual distortion, so the argument does not establish which experimental Co–donor bonds must lengthen.

Four equivalents of base remove the four carboxylic-acid protons from two ligand molecules, giving two dianionic pyridine-2,5-dicarboxylate linkers per Pt/Mn pair. The ring nitrogen and neighbouring 2-carboxylate supply a five-membered N,O chelate. A consistent chain has Pt(II), N2​O2​, with square planar molecular geometry, and Mn(II), O6​, with octahedral molecular geometry.
At each Pt, two ligands bind through their ring N and one O of the adjacent 2-carboxylate; their remote 5-carboxylates point toward successive Mn sites. At each Mn, a remote carboxylate from each of two neighbouring Pt-containing units binds through both O atoms. Two coordinated water molecules complete Mn's six-coordinate sphere. The repeating connectivity, with each bracket denoting a single organic linker, is
⋯Ptone linker[κ2(N,O):L2−:κ2(O,O)]​​Mn(H2​O)2​[κ2(O,O):L2−:κ2(N,O)]Pt⋯.
(7)
Each Pt has a second N,O chelate extending in the opposite chain direction; each Mn similarly receives two carboxylate chelates. The original donor-connectivity diagram makes those counts explicit.
Pt(II) is 5d8 and strongly favours square planar molecular geometry. Its affinity for the pyridyl N donor and the chelate effect select the mixed N,O pocket. Mn(II) is high-spin 3d5, is harder, and favours the carboxylate and water O donors; six-coordinate octahedral molecular geometry is natural in water. Charge balance per repeat is +2+2+2(−2)=0. A suitable repeat formulation is [PtMn(L)2​(H2​O)2​]n​. This is a justified proposed coordination chain, not a unique crystal structure established from stoichiometry alone; water content, carboxylate bridging modes and actual porosity require structural characterization.

In ionic counting, two η5 cyclopentadienyl anions donate 12 electrons. Therefore ferrocene, cobaltocene and nickelocene contain 18, 19 and 20 valence electrons, respectively: Fe(II) is d6, Co(II) d7 and Ni(II) d8. ferrocene fills the bonding and approximately nonbonding levels while leaving the next metal–ring antibonding pair empty. cobaltocene and nickelocene add one and two electrons to that antibonding set.
Increasing antibonding occupation lowers the net metal–ring bond order. It therefore explains the simultaneous increase in M–C distance and decrease in dissociation enthalpy from Fe to Co to Ni. The trend does not follow the shrinking size of the isolated metal ions; the orbital occupation is decisive here. The specified enthalpy separates the entire sandwich into a gaseous metal dication and two gaseous ring anions. It measures that complete ionic dissociation process, not ten independent localized M–C bond energies, so dividing it by ten is not a justified bond-energy determination.

Oxidizing cobaltocene removes its antibonding nineteenth electron and gives the particularly favourable 18-electron cobaltocenium cation. iodine can accept the electrons, giving cobaltocenium iodide at the simple stoichiometry
2CoCp2​+I2​⟶2[CoCp2​]+I−.​
(12)
Each cobalt changes from Co(II), d7, to Co(III), d6; each iodine atom changes from 0 to −1. Excess iodine can change the isolated counterion to a polyiodide such as I3−​. In contrast, oxidation of 18-electron ferrocene would remove an electron from its occupied closed-shell manifold to form 17-electron ferrocenium. iodine is insufficient to drive that reaction under the conditions implicit here. ferrocene can still be oxidized by suitable stronger oxidants; an electron count is a useful rationale for the comparative redox behaviour, not a universal proof of a numerical electrode potential.

The anionic nitrogen donor replaces iodide to give
A=CpFe(CO)2​(κN-C4​H4​N),byproduct=KI.​
(13)
Here Cp denotes η5 cyclopentadienyl and κN specifies attachment through the ring nitrogen of the pyrrolyl anion, C4​H4​N−. Fe(II), d6, receives six electrons from Cp, four from the two CO ligands and two from an N-bound pyrrolyl ligand: 6+6+4+2=18.
Two coupled CO stretching modes, approximately symmetric and antisymmetric combinations, account for the two strong carbonyl-region absorptions. The bent two-CO arrangement of this piano-stool complex permits both combinations to be infrared active. They are not evidence for two different chemical products.
Heating expels two CO molecules and changes the binding of the pyrrolyl ligand from η1 to η5, giving the sandwich azaferrocene,
B=Fe(η5−C5​H5​)(η5−C4​H4​N).​
(14)
The four electrons lost with CO are replaced by the four additional electrons donated on changing the hapticity of the pyrrolyl ligand. Thus B is also Fe(II), d6, and 18-electron: 6+6+6=18. Each complex has one time-averaged Cp proton environment, two pairs of protons from the pyrrolyl ligand, one adjacent to N and one farther from N. Hence three proton environments, with integrals 5:2:2, fit both structures. For proton nuclear magnetic resonance, three signals means three chemical shift environments; the individual resonances can still show coupling.

Use ionic electron counting, counting each ordinary donor atom once and keeping ligand charge distinct from denticity. A is 16-electron, B is 14-electron and C is 16-electron, so none is an 18-electron complex under this convention.

Its two phosphines and its central N donor provide six electrons altogether, while neutral N2​ provides two. Neutrality then gives Co(I), d8, and 8+6+2=16. Treating the modified pincer as a neutral intact pyridine ligand would incorrectly produce Co(0) and a 17-electron count. A four-coordinate approximately square planar molecular geometry in a d8 complex can leave the strongly sigma-antibonding orbital empty and be stable at 16 electrons. The underlying modified-pincer chemistry has been examined using structure, spectroscopy and electronic-structure calculations.[ref]
B has a neutral tertiary amine donor at the centre of its tripod and three anionic amido arms. Their combined charge is −3, so Re is Re(III), d4. Step 1 is oxidative addition of Si–H to Co(I), giving Co(III)–H and Co(III)–SiR3​. It cleaves the Si–H bond and forms two metal–ligand bonds, increasing the formal metal oxidation state by two. The phosphine dissociation shown before this numbered step makes room for the reaction; alkene binding and phosphine exchange are ligand-substitution events rather than oxidative additions.

The electron count for the drawn sequence is consistent: the one-PMe3​ alkene adduct is 16-electron; oxidative addition gives 18; insertion gives 16; PMe3​ association gives 18; reductive elimination returns the 16-electron catalyst. Because insertion occurs into Co–H, this is the conventional Chalk-Harrod mechanism.

The larger value for the carbonyl bond means that its dissociation costs 167 kJmol−1 more for the supplied comparison. A carbonyl combines a strong sigma bond with efficient pi overlap between spatially compatible C 2p and O 2p orbitals. This gives a substantial localized multiple-bond contribution.
The short P–O linkage in a phosphine oxide is also strong, but it is more polar, with a substantial P+−O− contribution. [−B(O−)−O−B(O−)−O−B(O−)−O−]cyclic3−​.​
(17)

A bridging oxygen shares its interactions between two boron centres. An approximately planar ring is therefore expected from the trigonal units and favourable orbital alignment. This reasoning does not require describing the entire anion as benzene-like with an assumed six-electron aromatic ring; the oxygen lone pairs and terminal donations must actually be counted before making an aromaticity claim.

is one conventional Lewis representation of trimetaphosphate. Its three P and nine O atoms consist of three bridging and six terminal O atoms. Within each phosphate unit the two terminal bonds have delocalized character; drawing one P=O and one P–O− is not a claim that every free anion has permanently different terminal oxygens.
A puckered six-membered P–O ring is expected. The tetrahedral phosphorus geometry and P–O–P torsional preferences allow folding without losing a required continuous perpendicular-p orbital system. This differs from the planar trigonal-boron arrangement. Tetrahedral P alone is not a geometrical proof of nonplanarity, since its two ring bonds could lie in a plane; the argument is that puckering is energetically accessible and relieves conformational strain without sacrificing a strong planar pi-conjugation benefit. The precise chair-like or boat-like distortion depends on environment and counterions.

A reasonable product is sodium facial trimetaphosphate–molybdenum tricarbonyl, with the anion
[Mo(CO)3​(κ3O,O′,O′′-P3​O9​)]3−,gas=3CO.​

a OurBigBook.com
Label the carbon next to the carbonyl Cα​ and the OH-bearing carbon Cβ​. The three limiting routes are E1, E1cb and E2 elimination. They differ in whether Cβ​–O cleavage, Cα​–H cleavage, or both occur in the first elementary event. In the following original transition-state structures, dotted lines denote changing bonds, δ marks developing partial charge, and the bracketed structures carry ‡. Unmarked skeletal bonds remain present. The dotted line parallel to Cα​–Cβ​ denotes the developing pi bond, in addition to its existing sigma bond.
For E1, Cβ​–OH heterolysis occurs first. The electron pair in that bond goes to O, producing CH3​COCH2​C+(H)CH3​+OH−. Its first transition state has a partly broken Cβ​–O bond, developing positive charge on Cβ​ and negative charge on the leaving O. Next, water uses a lone pair to remove the alpha proton; the Cα​–H electron pair forms the Cα​=Cβ​ pi bond. This second transition state has partly broken Cα​–H, partly formed water-O–H, and a partly formed Cα​–Cβ​ pi bond. Positive charge is transferred from the organic cation toward the incoming water/hydronium unit. hydroxide from the first step is a spectator in the second.
For E1cb, water first removes the alpha proton to form a resonance-stabilized enolate plus hydronium:
CH3​C(O−)=CHCH(OH)CH3​ ↔ CH3​COC−(H)CH(OH)CH3​.
(21)
The first transition state has partly broken Cα​–H and partly formed Owater​–H, with developing negative charge delocalized over the alpha-carbon/carbonyl-oxygen system and positive charge on the proton-accepting water unit. The enolate then expels hydroxide: use the carbon-centred contributor to send its alpha-carbon lone pair into Cα​–Cβ​, while the Cβ​–O pair goes to oxygen. This second transition state has a partly formed alkene pi bond and partly broken Cβ​–O, with negative charge moving from the enolate framework onto the departing hydroxide. hydronium is a spectator in this second step.
For E2, water removes the alpha proton as hydroxide leaves in the same elementary step. Three electron-pair movements are simultaneous: water lone pair to Hα​, Cα​–H to the Cα​–Cβ​ pi bond, and Cβ​–O to O. Its single transition state therefore contains all three changing bonds: Cα​–H, water-O–H and Cβ​–O, together with the developing pi bond. The departing O becomes negative, the proton-accepting water unit becomes positive, and partial charge at carbon depends on the synchrony of proton loss and leaving-group departure. The reacting conformation should align Cα​–H and Cβ​–O approximately in an antiperiplanar conformation for efficient elimination.
These are formal limiting pathways for the displayed net process, not three claims of equal reaction rates in neutral water. In particular, unassisted loss of OH− to a secondary carbocation is strongly disfavoured, and water is a weak base for making an enolate. The enolate pathway benefits from resonance stabilization and the final product is a conjugated enone, but actual rate and dominant pathway depend on chemical conditions. The displayed hydroxide and hydronium also rapidly neutralize each other; retaining them separately here tracks which bond cleavage supplied each species. No biological catalysis is needed to describe these chemical mechanisms.
