a OurBigBook.com
The nitrite ion is an ambidentate ligand: its Lewis structures leave donor lone pairs on both nitrogen and oxygen. In the N-bound mode, M−N(O)2​, the nitrogen lone pair makes a sigma bond to the metal, while a suitable unoccupied nitrite molecular orbital can accept metal d electron density by pi backbonding. Thus N-bound nitro is a sigma donor and pi acceptor. The nitrite group remains bent at nitrogen; N coordination does not make it a linear nitrosyl.
In the O-bound mode, M−O−N=O, an oxygen lone pair makes the metal–oxygen sigma bond. A second occupied oxygen orbital can donate sideways into an available metal orbital: O-bound nitrito is a sigma and pi donor, rather than the strong pi acceptor of the N-bound description. oxygen's greater electronegativity lowers its donor orbital energies, but the occupied oxygen orbitals still explain its pi-donor classification. Actual mixing depends on metal oxidation state and geometry.
A further possibility is bidentate coordination, M(κ2-O,O-NO2​), with both oxygen atoms attached to one metal. Each attachment has oxygen-to-metal sigma donation; additional pi donation depends on orbital alignment. nitrite can also be a bridging ligand between metals. Neither alternative should be confused with linkage isomerism, which specifically compares attachment through different atoms at the same binding site.

The N-bound isomer C is the thermodynamically favored form; heating accelerates access to it. Formation of B retains the original metal–oxygen attachment and is a plausible kinetic control outcome. Rearranging that attachment requires overcoming an activation free energy, especially at a substitutionally inert low-spin cobalt(III) center. The relevant spectrochemical series order is O-bound nitrito < Ammonia < N-bound nitro. A pi-donor ligand tends to raise the metal orbitals derived from t2g​, reducing the octahedral splitting; a pi-acceptor ligand stabilizes those orbitals, increasing the splitting. Since photon energy is E=hc/λ, the stronger-field complex absorbs at the shorter wavelength. Thus
λC​=458 nm,λ[Co(NH3​)6​]3+​=474 nm,λB​=486 nm.​
(2)
The corresponding spectroscopic wavenumbers are approximately 21834, 21097 and 20576 cm−1. These are the observed band energies, not three exact independent determinations of Δo​: low-spin d6 transition energies also contain electron repulsion contributions, and replacing one ligand lowers the ideal octahedral symmetry. The relative assignment is nevertheless robust.

For electron counting, use the ionic method: subtract the metal oxidation state from its group number, then add two electrons for each neutral phosphine ligand or carbonyl ligand and two for each chloride ligand.
Ni(PPh3​)(CO)3​ is Ni(0), d10, 18-electron and tetrahedral. The count is 10+2+3(2)=18. With only one distinct ligand in an ML3​L′ tetrahedral molecular geometry, there is only one geometrical arrangement.
Rh(PPh3​)2​(CO)Cl is Rh(I), d8, 16-electron and square planar, with the two phosphines trans. Its count is 8+2(2)+2+2=16. A square planar molecular geometry is characteristic of this low-spin 4d8 configuration. Opposite triphenylphosphine groups avoid their largest mutual steric hindrance; CO and chloride occupy the other trans pair.
Pd[P(tBu)2​Ph]2​Cl2​ is Pd(II), d8, 16-electron and square planar, with the bulky phosphines trans. Its count is likewise 8+4+4=16. The two di-tert-butylphenylphosphine groups are particularly crowded if cis. These predictions concern the favored intact four-coordinate species; ligand dissociation can occur under other conditions.

ii OurBigBook.com
The usual qualitative order of increasing ligand field is
Cl−<P(tBu)2​Ph<PPh3​<CO.​
(4)
A chloride ligand is a sigma-donor ligand and pi-donor ligand; its occupied pi orbitals raise the metal t2g​-derived levels and reduce the splitting. A carbonyl ligand is a particularly effective pi-acceptor ligand, lowering those levels and producing a large splitting. Both phosphine ligands are sigma donors with acceptor orbitals of mainly P–C antibonding molecular orbital character. Replacing phenyl groups by electron-releasing tert-butyl groups increases sigma basicity but generally weakens pi acceptance, so the alkyl-rich phosphine is placed below triphenylphosphine in this qualitative pi-bonding comparison. It would be wrong to equate a stronger sigma donor automatically with a stronger-field ligand, or to invoke empty phosphorus 3d orbitals as the essential acceptors.[ref]
This is a qualitative spectrochemical series prediction, not a universal numerical ranking for all metals and geometries. The opposing sigma and pi trends mean that precise comparisons require matched complexes and measured splittings; a Tolman electronic parameter measures net effects on CO backbonding and is not itself a ligand-field splitting.

A carbonyl ligand binds through carbon, whose occupied 5σ molecular orbital has a substantial outward-facing carbon lobe. Overlap with an empty metal orbital along the M–C axis gives ligand-to-metal sigma donation. Conversely, an occupied metal dπ​ orbital overlaps sideways with an empty CO 2π∗ antibonding molecular orbital, giving metal-to-ligand pi backbonding. There are two mutually perpendicular pi channels. The original orbital schematic below shows one of them; + and − denote orbital phase, not electric charge.
The interactions are synergic: donation makes electron density available at the metal, and backbonding helps accommodate it while strengthening the metal–carbon interaction. Occupying CO π∗ reduces the C–O bond order, lengthens that bond and lowers its bond force constant. Therefore stronger pi backbonding generally lowers the carbonyl stretching spectroscopic wavenumber even while strengthening the M–C attachment.

For the same pair of atoms, the harmonic approximation gives ν=(2πc)−1k/μ​, where k is the bond force constant and μ the reduced mass. The large shifts mainly reflect changes in k from CO pi backbonding; the complexes are not distinguished simply by their total number of valence electrons.
W(CO)6​ has stronger net backbonding than Cr(CO)6​. Both are group-6 M(0), d6, 18-electron complexes. The more spatially extended tungsten 5d orbitals provide effective overlap with CO π∗, consistent with the lower stretching frequency. As a simple same-mode force-constant comparison, (1977/2000)2=0.977, about a 2.3% reduction.
Ni(CO)3​(PMe3​) has appreciable but less C–O weakening than those hexacarbonyls. Ni(0) is d10 and the complex is also 18-electron. The more contracted, lower-energy late-3d occupied orbitals give less effective donation into CO π∗ than the group-6 carbonyls in this comparison. Trimethylphosphine donates electron density and increases backbonding relative to an otherwise comparable less-donating nickel complex; that effect does not force its frequency below chromium's. Comparing different metals requires both orbital energies and overlap.
[Cu(CO)4​]+ has very weak backbonding and a frequency above free CO. Cu(I) is d10 and 18-electron here, but positive charge lowers its occupied orbital energies and suppresses backdonation. Sigma donation and the cation's electrostatic polarization can strengthen the internal C–O bond. Consequently the usual free-CO reference, about 2143 cm−1, lies below this cation's band. A blue shift is compatible with carbon coordination; it does not imply absence of a metal–CO bond. Coupled normal modes and measurement conditions limit literal extraction of a local bond force constant from one band.

A dissociative substitution begins with slow CO loss,
M(CO)5​Lkd​​M(CO)4​L+CO,
(5)
followed by capture of the incoming ligand in coordination chemistry. A more electron-releasing L increases metal-to-CO pi backbonding in the six-coordinate reactant, stabilizing it and strengthening its M–CO attachments. Removing CO sacrifices a useful pi acceptor from that electron-rich center. Thus the activation free energy for CO dissociation often rises and the substitution slows. Within an otherwise matched series, kd​∝exp[−ΔG‡/(RT)] captures this sensitivity. The rate depends on the relative energies of reactant and dissociation transition state, so the trend is conditional: large changes in steric hindrance, geometry or mechanism can override the electronic effect.

The CO trans to a phosphine ligand competes for metal pi donation with a weaker pi acceptor than CO itself, whereas a cis CO has another CO trans to it. The trans-to-phosphine carbonyl can therefore benefit from stronger M–CO pi backbonding; loss of one of the four cis carbonyls is commonly favored. Capture of the incoming phosphine at that vacancy gives the cis bis(phosphine) product. The fourfold statistical availability of cis sites reinforces an electronic preference but does not establish it by itself.
This is the carbonyl cis effect, rather than the square-planar trans effect applied without qualification. A fuller explanation compares the five-coordinate dissociation transition states and their ligand site preferences. Product geometry alone cannot prove which CO first departed, because the intermediate can rearrange before capture. Isotopically labeled CO exchange is a more discriminating experiment; the classic studies explicitly separate this issue from product stereochemistry.[ref]

ii OurBigBook.com
A formal two-electron reductive elimination couples two anionic, mutually cis donors and reduces Ti(IV) to Ti(II). There are two chemically distinct couplings: Cl–Cl elimination gives Cl2​, leaving a Ti(II) complex with the intact anionic O,N,P ligand and one chloride; O–Cl elimination gives an aryl hypochlorite group, ArOCl, breaking the original Ti–O attachment and leaving a Ti(II) center coordinated by N,P and two chlorides. The latter product can remain tethered through the N,P part of the same ligand; it is not necessarily a free small molecule. The starting facial arrangement supplies cis pairs of both kinds.
Cl–Cl coupling is the more plausible of these formal alternatives, because it retains the particularly strong early-metal Ti–O interaction and makes the stronger Cl–Cl rather than relatively weak O–Cl bond. Both alternatives reduce the valence count by two, from 12 to 10, so the 18-electron rule does not choose between them. Neither should be asserted to occur readily: reduction of a stable, oxophilic d0 Ti(IV) center is intrinsically unfavorable. Coupling a chloride to the neutral amine or phosphine is not the ordinary elimination of two X-type ligands. proton transfer between N–H and oxygen, if it occurred, would instead be acid-base chemistry, not automatically a two-electron reductive elimination.

The readily displaced acetonitrile sites have both been exhausted. For an intact complex in the usual fast-conformational-averaging regime, counting distinct chemical shifts rather than individual split lines gives C:9, D:11, E:10 signals.
In C, the uncoordinated pyridinium arm permits the paired positions of a monosubstituted cyclopentadienyl ligand to average: its four ring hydrogens give two sets, 2+2. The linker CH2​ gives one set, the four inequivalent pyridinium C–H positions give four, its N–H gives one, and the two symmetry-related bound acetonitrile methyl groups give one. Hence 2+1+4+1+1=9. The exchangeable N–H resonance may be broadened or absent experimentally; nine is the structural count before exchange removes it.
In the rigid cis-dicarbonyl chelates D and E, the different pyridyl-N and fourth-leg donors destroy the operation that related the two sides of the substituted ring. Each of the four Cp hydrogens is distinct, and the two linker hydrogens are diastereotopic. Together with four pyridine C–H sites, this gives 4+2+4=10. The remaining bound acetonitrile adds one methyl signal to D, giving 11; E has none, giving 10. The two enantiomers of a racemate have identical spectra in an achiral solvent and do not double these counts. This prediction assumes resolved resonances and no rapid chelate opening that averages inequivalent sites; variable-temperature proton nuclear magnetic resonance would test that assumption.

a OurBigBook.com
The six P–N bonds are not well described as three permanently localized single bonds alternating with three ordinary double bonds. The strongly polar P–N framework has contributions from ionic bonding and nitrogen lone pair donation into neighboring phosphorus-centered acceptor combinations, including P–Cl σ∗ orbitals. This negative hyperconjugation supplies additional P–N bonding and equivalent environments around the ring. Approximate planarity is compatible with these interactions, but equal lengths and planarity alone do not establish benzene-like aromaticity. Large phosphorus 3d participation is not required.[ref]
Gallium binds to a ring nitrogen, and the two P–N bonds flanking that nitrogen lengthen. Formation of the N→GaCl3​ coordinate covalent bond changes the available nitrogen electron density and local geometry, weakening its contribution to the adjacent P–N interactions. The other four P–N bonds retain greater multiple-bond character and are correspondingly shorter. Loss of the threefold equivalence and altered local geometry permit the ring to pucker. This is a Lewis acid-base adduct, not replacement of a P–Cl bond by GaCl3​. Single-crystal structures and variable-temperature multinuclear nuclear magnetic resonance establish this class of nitrogen-bound group-13 adducts.[ref]

Fluoride favors geminal disubstitution: after the first substitution, the second F replaces the other chloride at that same phosphorus, giving a PF2​ unit and two unchanged PCl2​ units. The first highly electronegative F withdraws electron density inductively, maintaining or increasing the electrophilicity of its phosphorus and activating the neighboring P–Cl bond toward another nucleophile. The small fluoride ion also has little steric hindrance to attack there. In this context geminal substitution means both incoming substituents on one phosphorus; it does not describe the relative positions of different ring nitrogen atoms. Electronic activation at the substituted phosphorus competes with effects elsewhere in the ring, so the argument predicts the preferred pathway rather than an unconditional exclusive product.

ii OurBigBook.com
Primary amines can favor geminal disubstitution through a proton-abstraction route. The first substitution leaves an exocyclic PCl(NHR) group. Its remaining N–H proton provides a pathway unavailable to a fully substituted amino group: base removes H, chloride elimination gives a reactive low-coordinate phosphorus intermediate with increased exocyclic P–N bonding, and a second amine attacks that same phosphorus. proton transfer then gives P(NHR)2​. Thus the directing effect is not simply the inductive effect of an amino group.
This needs a qualification: “primary amine” alone does not specify a universal product distribution. Substituent, solvent and base concentration affect whether the proton-abstraction route outcompetes direct nucleophilic substitution. Kinetic and trapping experiments with aromatic primary amines support the base-catalyzed route to geminal products, while also finding direct substitution pathways under other conditions.[ref]

Secondary amines normally favor non-geminal disubstitution, putting the second amino substituent on a different phosphorus. The first installed NR2​ group donates electron density to the P–N framework and deactivates its own phosphorus toward further attack. It is also bulkier than an initially installed primary-amino group. Crucially, P−NR2​ has no N–H proton, so the proton-abstraction/chloride-elimination pathway that can direct a primary amine geminally is unavailable. Direct attack at a less-deactivated PCl2​ site is favored. The resulting non-geminal products can have cis or trans relative orientations around the ring; non-geminal substitution alone does not select one of these stereoisomers. These are useful trends, with known substituent-dependent exceptions in experimentally characterized mixed derivatives.[ref]

The number of IR bands counts allowed, possibly overlapping normal modes, not individual CO ligands. Formula, 18-electron rule, NMR equivalence and IR together select this model; the historical experimental work independently established diiron spiro rings and triiron products containing both P–Fe and N–Fe bonds.[ref]

Use C1 for the carbonyl carbon, C2 for the adjacent carbon, and C3 for the beta carbon of the reactive enone fragment R−C1(=O)−C2H=C3H−R′. water adds conjugately at C3, giving a beta-hydroxy ketone. The oxygen lone pair of neutral water attacks C3; move the C2=C3 pi pair toward C1–C2 and the carbonyl pi pair onto oxygen. This produces the charge-separated enolate–oxonium intermediate
R−C1(O−)=C2H−C3H(OH2+​)−R′.
(9)
A second water molecule can relay a proton from the newly attached oxonium oxygen to the enolate system. Using the carbon-centered resonance structure, reform C1=O and protonate C2 while the donating O–H bond returns its pair to the added oxygen. Equivalently, oxygen protonation first gives an enol, followed by water-mediated keto-enol tautomerism. Either path yields a neutral beta-hydroxy ketone and regenerates the relay water. There is no need to introduce externally supplied H+ or OH−; solvent molecules perform the proton transfers. The uncatalyzed charge separation has a large barrier, so this mechanism describes a slow formal pathway, not a claim of rapid hydration in pure water.

The C–O-forming transition state has a partial bond from water oxygen to C3, positive charge developing on that oxygen, and negative charge delocalizing toward the carbonyl oxygen. A compact drawing is
[R−C1(Oδ−)⋯C2H⋯C3H(R′)⋯OH2​δ+​]‡.
(10)
Here the two C–C dotted connections denote redistribution of pi bonding, not cleavage of either carbon–carbon sigma bond. In a bond-order drawing retain the full sigma skeleton: C1–O is changing from double toward single, C1–C2 from single toward double, C2–C3 from double toward single, and C3–O from absent toward single. The incoming water approaches a face of the approximately planar conjugated unit and C3 begins to become tetrahedral. The total electric charge remains zero. If proton transfer to another water occurs concertedly, include a partially broken incoming-water O–H bond and a partially formed relay O–H bond; that is a solvent-assisted variant, not a different regioselectivity. At pH 7, a glutamate residue is normally carboxylate. Its most natural role is general-base activation of the attacking water. Draw the carboxylate oxygen lone pair toward a water H, the water O–H pair toward its oxygen, and that oxygen's lone pair toward C3 while the conjugated pi system moves toward the carbonyl oxygen:
Glu−CO2−​⋯H−OH⋯C3⟶Glu−CO2​H+bound beta-hydroxy enolate.
(11)
This can be concerted, avoiding a freely diffusing hydroxide intermediate. The protonated glutamate subsequently returns its proton to the enolate system, directly or through solvent, regenerating carboxylate. A favorable arrangement is needed; ordinary carboxylate does not quantitatively deprotonate bulk water in a prior equilibrium.
The side-chain amide of glutamine is neutral. Its N–H can donate a hydrogen bond to the carbonyl oxygen and stabilize developing enolate charge, while its amide oxygen can accept a hydrogen bond to orient attacking water. Draw Gln−CONH2​⋯O=C1−C2=C3, with the dotted interaction from amide N–H to substrate oxygen. This assists polarization and transition state stabilization; the amide nitrogen is not an ordinary amine base because its lone pair is delocalized into its own carbonyl.
The guanidinium group of arginine is positively charged. It can stabilize developing negative charge by hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction, drawn Arg−guanidinium+⋯Oδ−−C1. Its delocalized cation is not the preferred neutral general base at pH 7. Productive catalysis requires stronger stabilization of the reacting arrangement than of the unreactive ground-state complex.
Phenylalanine and leucine have hydrocarbon side chains and provide no conventional acid/base or hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor group here. They can position the enone, exclude unproductive solvent and select the attacked face through hydrophobic effect and steric hindrance. Those binding effects can assist catalysis but should not be drawn as an imaginary side-chain proton relay. The beta-carbon bond-forming transition state retains the same basic connectivity across this pH range, but the predominant proton-transfer route and enzyme protonation change. Near the acidic end, protonated glutamate can act as a general acid, donating a proton or a strong hydrogen bond to carbonyl oxygen and making C3 more electrophilic; it is less available as the carboxylate general base that activates water. Above its local pKa​, carboxylate-mediated water activation is favored. Acid catalysis therefore shifts positive-charge stabilization toward a protonated carbonyl, whereas base-assisted attack reduces positive-charge buildup on incoming water. Neither a unique transition-state geometry nor a numerical pH optimum follows from pH alone.
Using a free-solution glutamate side-chain pKa​≃4.2 only as an illustration, the deprotonated fraction is [1+10pKa​−pH]−1: about 0.059 at pH 3 and 0.998 at pH 7. An enzyme environment can shift that pKa​ substantially. The glutamine amide and the hydrocarbon side chains remain in their ordinary forms from pH 3 to 10; arginine guanidinium, with a typical free side-chain pKa​ around 12.5, remains predominantly positive even at pH 10. Thus the major side-chain switch among those listed is glutamate. At the alkaline end, bulk hydroxide can also contribute to nonenzymatic conjugate addition. A measured pH-rate profile must control substrate stability and enzyme folding before assigning every loss of activity to an active-site titration.
