a OurBigBook.com
The carbon end of a carbonyl ligand has a filled 5σ molecular orbital with an outward-facing lobe. Its overlap with a vacant metal orbital of the same axial symmetry gives CO-to-metal sigma donation. Occupied metal dπ​ orbitals can simultaneously overlap with the empty CO 2π∗ antibonding molecular orbitals, giving metal-to-CO pi backbonding. Two perpendicular pi channels are possible; the schematic represents one. Orbital signs indicate phase, not electric charge.
The two interactions reinforce one another: sigma donation transfers density toward the metal, and pi backbonding distributes it into the ligand acceptor orbitals. Both strengthen M–C attachment, while backbonding weakens C–O. Occupation of CO π∗ decreases the internal C–O bond order, generally increasing its bond length and lowering its stretching spectroscopic wavenumber. This is why a strongly backbonded carbonyl ligand can have a strong metal–carbon bond and a weakened internal carbon–oxygen bond at the same time.

A suitable structure is a triangular Ti3​ metal cluster with one 1,2-Bis(dimethylphosphino)ethane chelate and one terminal chloride at each titanium, and one bridging ligand μ-Cl spanning each edge. Include a direct Ti–Ti single metal-metal bond along each edge. The last panel distinguishes these direct bonds from the chloride bridges.
The six chlorides have total formal charge −6, so equivalent centers are Ti(II), d2. At each titanium the two phosphorus donors supply four electrons, the terminal chloride supplies two, and the two incident chloride bridges each donate a pair in ionic electron counting. Two incident Ti–Ti single bonds supply another two electrons:
VE(Ti)=2d​+4P,P​+2Cl​+2(2)μ−Cl​+2Ti−Ti​=14.​
(3)
Each d2 center can contribute one electron to each of its two edge bonds, making three bonds from six metal electrons overall. This is a 14-electron early-metal cluster, not an 18-electron complex. Forcing the 18-electron rule here would invent excessive bond orders. chloride bridges donate to more than one center, so summing local counts is not the same as counting distinct electrons of the whole cluster.

c OurBigBook.com
The principal electronic variable is the net population of metal–metal bonding molecular orbitals relative to antibonding molecular orbitals. For otherwise comparable complexes, a larger bond order generally gives a shorter metal-metal bond; removing an antibonding electron can shorten a bond even though the total electron count falls. Different orbital symmetries also matter: sigma, pi and delta overlaps respond differently to distance and rotation of the metal fragments.
metal size, oxidation state and radial orbital extent set the underlying distance scale. Comparing a first-row metal with a larger heavier congener does not isolate bond order. Increasing positive charge often contracts the metal orbitals, but can also change electron occupancy, ligand binding and electrostatic interactions. The ligand field and donor/acceptor properties of the surrounding ligands in coordination chemistry determine which metal orbitals remain available for direct bonding and how much density instead participates in pi backbonding.
Finally, the geometry can enforce or oppose the preferred separation. Bridging ligands impose bond-angle and bond-length constraints; bulky substituents produce steric hindrance; crystal packing can perturb the observed distance. A bond length is evidence for bonding only in its electronic and structural context. In particular, two bridges can hold metals close without proving a high direct bond order, while a sterically congested formal single bond can be unusually long.

The dimer has two unpaired electrons and a triplet ground state, S=1. Its approximately threefold-symmetric Fe2​(CO)3​ core supplies a pair of degenerate frontier molecular orbitals. The last two electrons occupy separate members with parallel spins, favored by exchange, rather than pairing in one. A useful qualitative picture is a sigma bond plus two perpendicular, singly occupied pi half-bonds: together these support a formal Fe–Fe double bond while retaining paramagnetism. The spin-only magnetic moment for S=1 is 8​μB​≃2.83μB​; real moments can include orbital and spin–orbit effects.
The formal 18-electron count does not require every molecular orbital to be doubly occupied. It is therefore compatible with an open-shell dimer. The primary structural/magnetic study establishes this unusual triplet state; an isolated-atom high-spin d7 diagram would not describe the coupled metal–ligand system.[ref]

In the ionic assignment, both tetrahedral anions would require Mn(VII), d0. Such a high oxidation state is strongly oxidizing and is best supported by strong bonding to oxygen. Short Mn–O bonds combine good orbital overlap with oxygen's resistance to further oxidation. This stabilizes permanganate relative to a hypothetical all-sulfide analogue.
sulfide is larger, more polarizable and much easier to oxidize than oxide. An attempted Mn(VII) sulfide would therefore combine an exceptionally oxidizing metal center with reducing ligands: internal redox can reduce manganese while oxidizing sulfur, for example toward S–S-bonded or elemental sulfur products. The larger sulfur donors also provide less favorable short multiple bonding to the small, highly charged manganese center. The oxygen compound supports Mn(VII); the sulfide combination is redox-incompatible. This predicts instability without requiring a specific unprovided decomposition product.

ii OurBigBook.com
For an octahedral complex, strong axial sigma donation raises the metal–ligand antibonding eg​-derived orbitals. A hydride is a very strong sigma donor: its occupied 1s orbital overlaps effectively along a metal–ligand axis and has no filled perpendicular p orbitals to act as a conventional pi donor. It can therefore produce a large octahedral splitting by raising eg​ relative to t2g​.
Triphenylphosphine also gives sigma donation, but its useful acceptor orbitals additionally receive metal pi backbonding. This lowers the metal t2g​-derived levels and enlarges their separation from eg​. The acceptors have substantial P–C antibonding character; invoking a dominant phosphorus 3d acceptor is unnecessary. Thus hydride is strong-field principally through sigma bonding, while PPh3​ combines sigma donation with pi acceptance. The spectrochemical series describes the resulting splitting, not just a ranking of ligand basicity.

The metal is Cr(II), d4. A chloride ligand produces a relatively weak ligand field, so the octahedral center is a high-spin complex with t2g3​eg1​, S=2. The singly occupied eg​ pair is orbitally degenerate: dz2​ and dx2−y2​ cannot retain identical occupancies once the distortion selects one. This is a strong setting for the Jahn-Teller effect.
On tetragonal elongation, the two axial Cr–Cl distances increase. Repulsion is reduced most for dz2​, so it falls relative to dx2−y2​; dxz​,dyz​ also fall relative to dxy​. The single eg​ electron occupies the lower dz2​-derived level. The electronic stabilization offsets an elastic cost until a distorted equilibrium geometry is reached. The high-spin d4 occupancy drives an elongated, rather than regular, octahedron. The three lower-set electrons remain one per orbital in this simple diagram; it is primarily the unequal eg​ occupancy that produces the strong distortion.

For comparable octahedral environments with the same donor set, the expected qualitative order is
Mn2+<Fe2+<Fe3+<Mo3+<Rh3+<Pt4+.​
(6)
Across comparable first-row ions, the increasing effective attraction and changing metal–ligand distances tend to increase octahedral splitting, giving Mn(II) below Fe(II). Raising the charge on the same metal gives Fe(III) above Fe(II), because the ligand interaction generally strengthens and metal–ligand distances contract. Moving from 3d to more radially extended 4d orbitals gives larger metal–ligand overlap, placing Mo(III) above Fe(III); Rh(III) lies further along the same 4d series and typically gives the larger splitting. The 5d ion Pt(IV) combines the heavier row with high charge and gives the largest splitting here.
This is a matched-environment trend, not an intrinsic list of constants for bare ions. Actual Δ also depends strongly on donor identity, geometry and distance. It would be meaningless to compare an unspecified weak-field tetrahedral compound with a strong-field octahedral compound solely through this ordering.

Start from an octahedral molecular geometry with ligands on ±x,±y,±z, with eg​=(dz2​,dx2−y2​) above t2g​=(dxy​,dxz​,dyz​). Remove the −z donor while keeping the other five positions. Loss of axial repulsion lowers dz2​ substantially; dx2−y2​ still points directly at four basal donors and remains highest. Loss of an axial donor also lowers the orbitals with z dependence relative to dxy​. The remaining fourfold axis keeps dxz​ and dyz​ degenerate.
The usual qualitative square-pyramidal molecular geometry diagram, shown in the last panel above, is therefore
dx2−y2​ > dz2​ > dxy​ > dxz​=dyz​.​
(7)
The corresponding C4v​ labels are b1​,a1​,b2​,e. Energies can be re-zeroed to the new orbital barycenter after ligand removal. Symmetry fixes the degeneracy and labels, but does not universally fix the separation of dz2​ and dxy​: a weak or distant axial donor can reverse this middle pair on approach to the square-planar limit. The displayed ordering assumes an appreciable fifth axial interaction.

Treat the two donors of 2-Picolylamine as distinct: P denotes pyridyl N and A denotes amino N. Each ligand must join two adjacent octahedral positions because its five-membered chelate ring cannot span a trans pair. The three P sites can either occupy a triangular face, giving a facial isomer, or a meridian containing a trans pair, giving a meridional isomer. Each geometry has a pair of nonsuperposable mirror images. Therefore there are four configurational stereoisomers: fac-Δ, fac-Λ, mer-Δ and mer-Λ. Rapid ring puckering or amine inversion does not introduce additional persistent configurations in this count.
The mer geometry is the reasonable steric prediction for the more stable form. It spreads the bulky pyridyl rings farther apart, allowing one pyridyl–pyridyl trans relationship, whereas the fac geometry groups all three pyridyl donors on one face. This is a qualitative preference, not a proof independent of counterions and solvent: hydrogen bonding and packing can favor the alternative. Primary structures of different salts contain both geometries, so neither should be claimed impossible.[ref]

The filled alkene π molecular orbital donates into a vacant metal acceptor orbital directed toward the center of the C=C bond. In the reverse direction, a filled metal d orbital can overlap with the alkene π∗ antibonding molecular orbital. These are the donation and pi backbonding components of the Dewar-Chatt-Duncanson model, illustrated in the lower part of the bonding schematic. Write R for the current growing chain. Propene first binds at the available site of [Cp2​ZrR]+, giving a coordinated alkene adjacent to the metal-bound chain. In the Cossee-Arlman mechanism, the chain migrates to the substituted alkene carbon while the terminal carbon forms the new Zr–C bond:
[Cp2​Zr−R]++CH2​=CHMe⇌[Cp2​Zr(R)(η2−CH2​=CHMe)]+⟶[Cp2​Zr−CH2​−CH(Me)−R]+.

a OurBigBook.com
In borazine, each B and N is approximately trigonal planar. The alternating ring contains a sigma framework with perpendicular p orbitals; the nitrogen lone pairs can donate into the boron acceptor orbitals. Six pi electrons can therefore be distributed around the ring, giving partial B–N multiple-bond character rather than six ordinary isolated single bonds. Equal ring distances are compatible with this symmetric delocalization.
The electronegativity difference makes each B–N bond strongly polar, Bδ+−Nδ−. The occupied pi orbitals are weighted toward N, and the empty acceptors toward B. In benzene, equivalent carbon centers give much more even pi distribution and strong resistance to addition that destroys the aromatic system. borazine resembles benzene geometrically but has substantially more polarized, more reactive bonding. It presents electrophilic boron sites to nucleophiles and nitrogen donors to Lewis acids. Polar reagents can add across B–N, and water can cleave the ring; benzene ordinarily favors substitution under suitable conditions rather than facile polar addition or hydrolysis. The label “inorganic benzene” should therefore not be interpreted as identical aromatic stabilization or reactivity. nitrogen-bound Lewis-acid adducts and hydrogen-halide additions demonstrate this contrast experimentally.[ref]

The methyl nucleophile attacks an electrophilic ring boron. That boron becomes tetrahedral, bearing its original methyl, the incoming methyl, and its two ring nitrogen neighbors. This interrupts its p-orbital participation, so the metal attaches to the remaining five-atom conjugated segment. A is the anionic η5 heptamethylated-ring Cr(CO)3​ complex, paired formally with [MgBr]+; solvent ligation and ion pairing at magnesium are not specified by the spectrum. The original connectivity, with the attacked boron labeled B1​, is shown in the first panel below.
There are seven methyl groups. The two attached to tetrahedral B1​ are inequivalent because the chromium-bound face distinguishes their environments: one signal of relative integral one for each. The other two B–Me groups are equivalent to one another, giving a signal of integral two. The two N–Me groups adjacent to B1​ form another equivalent pair, while the N–Me opposite B1​ is unique. The predicted methyl-group multiplicities are therefore 1,1,2,2,1, the same unordered set as the observed ratio. Multiplying by three gives proton integrals totaling 21. This assignment assumes no fast process interchanging the two B1​ methyl environments.
The six-electron neutral η6 ring donor has become an anionic six-electron η5 donor, so Cr(0) retains an 18-electron count: 6d​+6ring​+3(2)CO​=18. This borazine dearomatization explains both the changed hapticity and the extra methyl without requiring attack at chromium or loss of a carbonyl.

B is a 1:1 nitrogen-bound adduct, with N→AlBr3​ at one ring nitrogen. Its lone pair is diverted from N-to-B pi donation into the new coordinate bond. That nitrogen becomes approximately tetrahedral and the ring can pucker. The two B–N bonds adjacent to it lose pi character and lengthen; the neighboring unbound nitrogen atoms can donate more strongly into those borons, shortening the next pair of B–N bonds. This is Chemical exchange in NMR, not restoration of equal instantaneous B–N bonds. A spectrum alone cannot distinguish intramolecular migration from reversible dissociation and reassociation; concentration dependence and crossover experiments would test that distinction. Related N-bound borazine adducts have been characterized by diffraction and variable-temperature NMR, supporting the structural logic while not supplying the exact temperature dependence of this particular hexamethyl compound.[ref]

In the chromium complex, Cr(0) is d6 and receives substantial donation from four carbonyl ligands. Its occupied orbitals can give pi backbonding into the boron-rich acceptor orbitals of the B2​N2​ ring, while the nitrogen-rich occupied ring orbitals donate to the metal. This favors binding across the ring face, approximately η4, with all four ring atoms participating. A nearly planar ring maintains the p-orbital alignment needed for that interaction and accounts for comparable Cr–B and Cr–N distances. A four-electron ring donor also gives the useful count 6d​+8CO​+4ring​=18.
Ti(IV) in TiCl4​ is d0 and strongly electron deficient. It is a good acceptor for the two nitrogen lone pairs, but has no filled d set for comparable backbonding into the boron acceptors. chromium therefore favors a face-bound pi complex, while titanium favors N,N sigma coordination. Donation makes the nitrogen environments more nearly tetrahedral and permits the 2,4-Diazadiboretidine ring to pucker. The borons are not directly bound in the same way, explaining d(Ti⋯B)>d(Ti−N). The distinction follows from electron availability, rather than simply calling every short metal–ring contact a separate ordinary sigma bond.

The reactive fragment is the C6 amidine-like portion of the purine ring; the N9–ribose attachment is retained. Write it locally as N1=C6(NH2​)−, with the rest of the fused ring understood. One productive acid catalysis route protonates N1, making C6 more electrophilic. water then adds at C6 while the C6=N1 pi electrons move onto N1. Subsequent proton transfers generate a carbinolamine and then a protonated amino leaving group:
N1=C6(NH2​)​ +H+ ​N1H+=C6(NH2​) +H2​O ​N1H−C6(OH2+​)(NH2​) −H+ ​N1H−C6(OH)(NH2​) +H+ ​N1H−C6(OH)(NH3+​) −NH3​ ​N1H−C6(=OH+) −H+ ​N1H−C6(=O).​
(13)
These are local bonding fragments: C6 retains its bond to the adjoining ring carbon at every step. In the addition step, an oxygen lone pair forms C6–O and the C6=N1 pi bond opens. During collapse, an oxygen lone pair reforms C6=O and the C6–N6 bond breaks toward the departing nitrogen. Solvent mediates each proton transfer; no direct unsupported transfer across a long distance is required.
water supplies the new C6 oxygen, and Ammonia is expelled after protonation of the leaving group. Final deprotonation gives the lactam form of inosine. In acidic bulk solution the released Ammonia is largely trapped as ammonium. The catalytic proton transfers regenerate acid around the substitution cycle; ammonium trapping separately affects the net proton balance. This is hydrolytic deamination, not cleavage of the glycosidic bond.

In the diagrams E is the protein tether, and C6 is the reacting purine carbon.

The same acid could instead assist the amino leaving group: E−CO2​H+−NH2​→E−CO2−​+−NH3+​. These are two locations for general acid catalysis, rather than two different categories.
general base catalysis: E−CO2−​⋯H−OH→E−CO2​H+OH−, coupled to oxygen attack on C6. Accepting a proton makes water a more effective nucleophile. This does not require an appreciable pool of free hydroxide in bulk solution.
covalent catalysis: a glutamate oxygen can in principle attack activated C6 to form C6−O−C(O)−E. After addition/elimination replaces the amino group, water must hydrolyze that transient enzyme–substrate bond to release product and regenerate E−CO2​H/CO2−​. Such a pathway is a chemically distinct possibility, not established by the given structure.
electrostatic catalysis: the carboxylate can stabilize a developing positive charge, for example in a protonated purine or amino leaving group; a protonated carboxyl can also donate a hydrogen bond to an oxygen developing negative charge. This contributes only if the reacting configuration is stabilized relative to the bound starting state. A carboxylate placed next to an oxyanion without compensating geometry would instead be unfavorable.
proximity and orientation catalysis: donor and acceptor hydrogen bonds can hold the purine and attacking water in a productive arrangement, schematically E−CO2​H⋯N1−C6⋯OH2​. Binding reduces unproductive orientations and can distort the substrate toward a reactive geometry. This last contribution is sometimes described through substrate binding or strain; a binding interaction is not automatically catalytic unless it improves access to the transition state.

The zinc ion is coordinated by protein donors and a water oxygen placed near the reacting C6 center. Zinc acts as a Lewis acid that activates and positions water. Its positive charge withdraws electron density from oxygen, lowers the effective pKa​ of coordinated water and facilitates formation of the attacking hydroxide, potentially with assistance from a nearby base. The structure identifies a plausible reaction geometry, but a static X-ray model does not by itself establish the water's protonation state, proton-transfer order or the rate-limiting step. Structural work with bound transition-state analogues independently supports a zinc-containing catalytic center.[ref] An appropriate experimental test would compare native enzyme, a metal-depleted preparation and stoichiometrically zinc-reconstituted enzyme, checking retained fold and active enzyme concentration. Loss and recovery of activity with those controls distinguishes a catalytic metal requirement from nonspecific denaturation.

e OurBigBook.com
A chemically coherent mechanism combines zinc-activated water addition with a proton relay involving His238 and Glu217. In a simple route consistent with the displayed contacts, neutral His238 accepts a proton from zinc-bound water while protonated Glu217 donates to substrate N1. These transfers can be coupled to the bond-forming event rather than requiring accumulation of a free, doubly activated substrate. Zinc-bound hydroxide attacks C6; the C6=N1 pi electrons move onto N1, yielding the tetrahedral C6 intermediate bearing OH and NH2​ while N1 bears H.
Next, the exocyclic amino group receives a proton, through His238, Glu217 and/or intervening water as permitted by the changing geometry. The oxygen is deprotonated as it forms the C6 carbonyl, and the C6–N6 bond cleaves to release Ammonia. These transfers can be coupled in the collapse step, avoiding expulsion of NH2−​. Zinc stabilizes the reacting oxygen and the organized intermediate; the ring regains conjugation in the inosine product. Product dissociation, fresh water binding and proton exchange with solvent reset the catalytic groups.
The core result is zinc-assisted addition–elimination, with acid/base proton transfers and water supplying C6 oxygen. The contributions are metal ion catalysis, general base catalysis, general acid catalysis, electrostatic catalysis and proximity and orientation catalysis. The proposed route does not require covalent catalysis by a protein side chain: a substrate–water C–O bond is not an enzyme–substrate covalent linkage. Asp296 can retain a structural hydrogen-bonding role without acting as an indispensable acid/base.
The data establish the importance of His238 and Glu217 but do not uniquely assign the entire proton relay. In particular, an alternative has Glu217 first deprotonate water and then deliver that proton to N1, with His238 assisting hydroxide stabilization and later amino-group protonation. The primary observation that His238 substitution need not abolish analogue hydration cautions against calling its initial-base role proven.[ref] pH-rate profiles, solvent isotope effects and time-resolved intermediate experiments would discriminate these refinements. The diagram is a proposed catalytic route, not a structure directly observed by the crystal experiment.
