i OurBigBook.com
An enolate is an ambident nucleophile: the negative charge is concentrated toward oxygen, while its occupied conjugated orbital also has substantial reactive amplitude at the alpha carbon. An alkyl iodide presents an accessible, polarizable carbon electrophile. carbon attack through an SN2 reaction is generally kinetically favorable, while the more strongly charged oxygen is often coordinated to the counterion or solvated. The carbonyl reforms as the new C–C bond is made.
R−C(O−)=CHR′+MeI⟶R−C(O)−CH(Me)R′+I−.
(1)
carbon alkylation restores the carbonyl and makes the new C–C bond. oxygen alkylation instead gives an enol ether. The two outcomes are not resonance contributors of one product, and their ratio is not determined just by where the largest formal negative charge is drawn. electrophile, counterion, solvent and aggregation can change selectivity; hard silicon electrophiles and suitable acylating reagents often favor oxygen trapping. Product stability alone would not explain an irreversible kinetic choice.

Lithium diisopropylamide removes an alpha proton from the ethyl side of the ketone. The C–H electrons form the C=C part of a lithium enolate, while the C=O pi electrons move to oxygen. The phenyl side has no competing alpha C–H site. Diisopropylamine is the conjugate-acid byproduct.
PhCOCH2​MeLDA​PhC(O−Li+)=CHMe.
(2)
The carbon end of this enolate attacks the primary carbon bearing iodine in ICH2​CH2​CH2​OMe. The C–I bond breaks to iodide in an SN2 reaction, and carbonyl reformation gives
PhCOCH(Me)CH2​CH2​CH2​OMe.​
(3)
Thus a three-carbon methoxypropyl chain is attached at the ketone alpha carbon. The remote methyl ether survives. The hindered amide base is chosen to generate the enolate rather than to add to the carbonyl. No absolute stereochemical preference is specified for the new stereocenter under these achiral conditions.

The initial nucleophile attacks silicon, not the nitrile or an alkene carbon. Each trimethylsilyl group has an electrophilic Si center. A methyl group from methyllithium attacks Si, passing through a pentacoordinate silicon arrangement; Si–O cleavage releases SiMe4​ and the lithium salt of the oxygen-containing fragment. Cleaving the silyl enol ether generates a lithium enolate; cleaving the aryl silyl ether generates a phenoxide. Neither operation needs a new deprotonation at a carbon stereocenter.
R−O−SiMe3​+MeLi⟶R−O−Li++SiMe4​.
(4)
Here R−O− represents either the phenoxide or the oxygen-centered enolate contributor. Excess reagent permits both cleavages. The pre-existing methyl/nitrile stereocenter retains its configuration: it has no hydrogen to remove, and neither bond attached to that center is broken.
After HMPA is added, each anionic oxygen attacks the carbonyl carbon of allyl chloroformate. The C=O pi electrons move to the carbonyl oxygen, giving a tetrahedral intermediate; collapse expels chloride. The enolate becomes an allyl enol carbonate, and the phenoxide becomes an aryl allyl carbonate, each containing O−C(O)−OCH2​CH=CH2​. oxygen acylation accounts for preservation of the enol double bond and for the two newly installed carbonate groups. carbon attack would give a different carbon skeleton. The two oxygens are trapped because they were selectively unmasked by desilylation, rather than because every ether oxygen in the molecule is equally reactive.

HMPA coordinates Li+ through its phosphoryl oxygen and makes the anionic oxygen sites more available for acylation. Strong cation solvation changes contact ion pairing, chelation and aggregate structure. It can therefore accelerate trapping of both the enolate and phenoxide by the chloroformate, helping realize the observed O-acylation pathway.
It is too strong to assert that HMPA necessarily produces completely free, monomeric anions. In a primary study of a lithium enolate in THF, low-temperature multinuclear NMR and reaction kinetics identified an HMPA-coordinated dimer as the reactive form, even with excess HMPA.[ref] The useful conclusion here is altered lithium coordination and enhanced oxygen accessibility; the exact aggregate of this bifunctional substrate is not supplied.

The carbamate carbonyl oxygen attacks electrophilic sulfur of triflic anhydride, with cleavage of the anhydride S–O bond to release triflate. Donation of the nitrogen lone pair toward the carbonyl carbon then gives the indicated imidoyl-type cation: the former carbonyl oxygen now bears Tf, and the N–H nitrogen participates in a C=N+ bond. This converts a relatively unreactive carbamate carbonyl into a strongly electrophilic carbon bearing an excellent leaving group.
The aromatic pi bond para to ethoxy attacks that carbon. The C=N pi electrons move to nitrogen as the new aryl–carbon bond forms. Loss of the aromatic proton restores aromaticity; nitrogen donation and departure of the O-triflylated group restore the imine bond. proton transfers during the sequence and carbonate workup remove the remaining N–H proton. The surviving five-membered ring is an oxazoline: the product is 2-(4-ethoxyphenyl)-2-Oxazoline. An equivalent arrow order can eliminate the triflyloxy leaving group before arene capture; these are limiting descriptions of the activated electrophile, not a need to postulate an unactivated carbamate undergoing ordinary Friedel–Crafts acylation.
Ethoxy donates a lone pair into the ring, stabilizing the ortho and para Wheland intermediates. The para pathway avoids the crowding of an incoming heterocycle beside ethoxy, giving the observed position. Mechanistic experiments on this transformation include in situ carbon NMR of the activated reagent, competition experiments and isotope-effect measurements supporting electrophilic aromatic substitution; they do not directly image every intermediate.[ref]

Oxazolination occurs para to oxygen on the ring that does not bear bromine. The product is 2-[4-(4-bromophenoxy)phenyl]-2-Oxazoline, shown in the final panel above. The brominated ring retains its para bromine; the other ring receives the new oxazolinyl group at its open para position.

In the strongly acidic nitrating mixture, the dimethylamino nitrogen is predominantly protonated. The substituent is therefore −NMe2​H+ during aromatic substitution, rather than the neutral electron-donating −NMe2​ group. Its lone pair is unavailable for conjugation. Ortho or para attack would generate a sigma-complex contributor with positive ring charge at the carbon attached to an already positive nitrogen; meta attack avoids that especially unfavorable contributor.
Sulfuric acid protonates nitric acid, and loss of water produces the nitronium ion. Aromatic attack at a meta position forms a Wheland intermediate; deprotonation restores aromaticity. The bicarbonate workup then returns the amino group to its neutral form. A is 3-nitro-N,N-dimethylaniline.
There are six inequivalent aromatic carbons in this unsymmetrical meta-disubstituted ring and one carbon environment for the two equivalent N-methyl groups, giving seven carbon NMR signals. The para isomer would have only four aromatic environments plus the methyl environment. An ortho isomer could also give seven signals, so signal counting supports but does not alone establish the meta assignment; protonation-dependent directing effects are essential.

tert-Butoxide removes the acidic hydrogen from PhCH(OPh)CN. The resulting carbon anion is stabilized by the nitrile group and the phenyl substituent. It attacks the aromatic carbon bearing fluorine, giving a Meisenheimer addition intermediate. Ring pi bonds shift toward the para nitro group, where the negative charge can be delocalized onto oxygen.
Loss of fluoride restores aromaticity and gives p−NO2​C6​H4​−C(Ph)(OPh)CN. This is an addition–elimination nucleophilic aromatic substitution. Acid workup neutralizes residual anions; it is not a separate step that replaces the aryl fluoride.

ii OurBigBook.com
The same base first makes PhC−(OPh)CN. This time addition occurs at a carbon bearing hydrogen para to the nitro group, giving a sigma-H adduct. The attacked carbon temporarily bears both its original H and the new carbon substituent. Negative charge is again delocalized through the ring toward the nitro oxygens.
A further base removes the hydrogen on that aromatic addition site. Its C–H electrons form an exocyclic bond toward the incoming carbon, while that carbon expels phenoxide. This is a base-induced elimination across the new ring–side-chain bond. Redistribution of the nitronate/quinoid pi system restores an aromatic ring and gives the side-chain carbanion p−NO2​C6​H4​−C−(Ph)CN. protonation during acidic workup yields p−NO2​C6​H4​−CH(Ph)CN.
This is vicarious nucleophilic substitution: the ring loses hydrogen while the nucleophile sacrifices its phenoxy leaving group. hydrogen is not expelled as hydride, and the product's side-chain hydrogen can be supplied on workup rather than moving directly from the ring in a compulsory hydride shift. Base and isotope experiments in the primary mechanistic work support reversible addition followed by base-induced elimination, while not uniquely choosing every E2/E1cb limit.[ref] The displayed para product is consistent with access to the less crowded activated position.

Hydroxide initially deprotonates a little thiophenol, generating the reactive thiolate PhS−. Number the extended conjugated system O=C1−C2=C3−C4=C5, where C1–C2–C3 is the ring enone and C4=C5 is the attached vinyl group. sulfur attacks the terminal delta carbon C5. The pi bonds relay toward oxygen, generating an extended enolate.
proton transfer at C4, either directly from thiophenol or through solvent-mediated enolization, gives the product retaining the conjugated ring enone. The product is 3-[2-(phenylsulfanyl)ethyl]cyclohex-2-en-1-one, drawn below. Its side chain is −CH2​CH2​SPh; the ring C2=C3 double bond remains. This is net sulfur/hydrogen addition to the terminal part of the extended system, proceeding through 1,6-Conjugate addition and protonation of the extended enolate.
The terminal site is less crowded than the ring beta carbon, and the isolated product retains favorable carbonyl–alkene conjugation. Reversible proton transfers and conjugate addition under thiolate catalysis can favor that product over an unconjugated enone-derived alternative. Transfer of a proton from another PhSH molecule regenerates PhS−, explaining why a catalytic quantity of hydroxide suffices. The net balance is one thiophenol added, C8​H10​O+C6​H6​S=C14​H16​OS; carbonyl 1,2 addition would instead produce a different functional group and is not favored for this soft sulfur nucleophile.

Ph2​P(O)−C−H−C(SPh)=CH2​.

a OurBigBook.com
The depicted trans-fused A is chiral; cis-fused B is achiral as a configurational species. The unsaturated ring makes the two sides of the fused system constitutionally different. At the trans junctions of A, the mirror image cannot be superposed by a proper rotation; there is no surviving mirror plane or inversion center that exchanges the two distinct rings correctly. A twofold rotational relationship is compatible with chirality and does not remove it. This is why simply importing the chirality conclusion for fully saturated trans-decalin would be unsafe.
For B, interchange of the two junction ends relates the opposite local stereochemical senses. An individual folded cis conformer may look chiral, but ring inversion interconverts its mirror-related conformations without changing junction configuration. The configurations therefore describe a meso/achiral species rather than a persistent enantiomeric pair. The conformational sketches below distinguish the rigid trans arrangement from the two accessible cis folds; the alkene ring is represented schematically as a half-chair-like unit.

b OurBigBook.com
For both substrates, the alkene pi bond attacks bromine and polarizes/breaks Br–Br. A bromine lone pair completes a three-membered bromonium ion, and bromide subsequently attacks a carbon from the opposite face, cleaving that C–Br bridge bond. The two new C–Br bonds are anti. A free carbocation that rotates before capture is not needed.
In trans-fused A, the fixed conformation makes the alternative anti-opening pathways diastereomeric. A chair-like, trans-diaxial opening is favored over a geometrically strained alternative that requires a twist/boat-like arrangement of the six-membered ring. The Fürst-Plattner rule describes this conformational preference. One depicted enantiomer of A therefore gives essentially one dibromide diastereomer, with the two bromines occupying the trans-diaxial arrangement in its favored chair. If a racemic sample of A were used, its other enantiomer would of course give the mirror product as well.
For cis-fused B, the accessible mirror-related folds and the equivalent anti pathways produce mirror-related dibromides with equal rates in an achiral environment. Adding the two bromines destroys the symmetry that made the starting configuration achiral. B gives a racemic dibromide pair. The racemate follows from equivalent enantiomer-forming pathways, not from a loss of anti stereospecificity.

Number the dioxane O1–C2–O3–C4–C5–C6, with methyl at C2 and tert-butyl at C5. Their cis relationship is maintained on ring inversion, but because they occupy opposite ring positions, one is axial and the other equatorial in either chair. The two alternatives are C2–Me equatorial/C5–tert-butyl axial, and C2–Me axial/C5–tert-butyl equatorial.
The key signal is U, the C5 methine, with only small vicinal couplings, 3.7 and 1.3 Hz. An axial H5 would have large trans-diaxial couplings to axial hydrogens on C4 and C6. Their absence identifies H5 as equatorial, hence the preferred conformer has axial tert-butyl and equatorial methyl. It is not sufficient to guess from tert-butyl bulk. The tert-butyl singlet itself supplies no vicinal H–H coupling information about its orientation.

The axial penalties depend on the ring position. An axial methyl at C2 encounters the axial hydrogens on C4 and C6. The short C–O links and dioxane geometry make these 1,3-Diaxial interactions particularly unfavorable. In contrast, an axial substituent at C5 points toward ring oxygens O1 and O3 rather than toward axial C–H groups at those positions. Those oxygen sites present much less steric obstruction than axial hydrogens on carbon.
Avoiding the expensive axial C2 methyl outweighs the cost of axial tert-butyl at C5. Transferring cyclohexane tert-butyl preferences unchanged would therefore give the wrong answer. This is primarily a position-dependent steric comparison, not the usual anomeric preference for an axial electronegative substituent at an acetal carbon. Primary NMR work on substituted dioxanes documents this axial C5 tert-butyl conformer.[ref]

The alkene undergoes concerted oxygen transfer from the terminal peroxide oxygen of mCPBA. In the cyclic electron-flow description, the alkene pi bond begins one C–O bond, the peroxide oxygen supplies the second, O–O breaks, and proton transfer/carboxyl rearrangement gives m-chlorobenzoic acid. Both C–O bonds are installed from the same alkene face, so the alkene's relative substituent geometry is retained in the epoxide. There is no freely rotating carbocation intermediate.
The neighboring free OH hydrogen-bonds to the approaching peracid and favors a cyclic, organized transition-state geometry. oxygen is delivered from the OH-directed face, giving the indicated syn relationship between epoxide oxygen and the allylic hydroxyl. In the supplied projection, the epoxide C–O bonds and the original C–OH bond are on the rear/hashed face. Steric interactions still influence the preferred hydrogen-bonded conformer, but “attack from the least hindered face” alone misses the positive directing role of OH. The distant benzyl ether is not the directing free hydroxyl.
The classical experimental comparison of allylic alcohols and protected derivatives established this directing principle; it supports the mechanistic interpretation without itself measuring the selectivity of this acyclic substrate.[ref]

A: aldehyde oxygen is the best acceptor; there is no conventional strong O–H or N–H donor. The N-methylpyrrole nitrogen contributes its lone pair to aromatic bonding and is not an ordinary amine acceptor. Solution

ii OurBigBook.com
4-Fluorophenol probes acceptor strength because it donates its OH hydrogen. The qualitative order is C > D > B > A: the electron-rich lactam oxygen is the strongest acceptor here, the secondary amine is next, pyridine nitrogen is intermediate, and aldehyde oxygen is weakest. Resonance donation from amide nitrogen increases the lactam oxygen's basicity relative to an ordinary aldehyde or ketone oxygen. The N-methylpyrrole site in A does not add an independent amine-like acceptor.
N-Methylpyrrolidin-2-one is a carbonyl acceptor without N–H, so it instead probes the solute's donor ability. B > C ≫ A and D: B's phenolic OH is a strong donor, the lactam N–H of C is appreciable, A lacks a conventional donor, and D's neutral secondary-amine N–H is weak. The amine can be a good acceptor and a poor donor at the same time; the two properties are not opposites on one scale.
These are association equilibria in cyclohexane, where solvent competition for hydrogen bonds is weak. The quoted constants should be compared within each probe series, not treated as solvent-independent measures. At 298 K, as an illustrative common temperature, a tenfold increase in Ka​ corresponds to a binding free-energy advantage RTln10≃5.7 kJmol−1. This does not imply the experiment's temperature was specified, nor that every difference is the energy of one isolated hydrogen bond.

At ordinary physiological pH, the arginine side chain is predominantly guanidinium. Its N–H groups are donors, and its positive charge can reinforce favorable electrostatic interactions. guanine exposes adjacent O6 and N7 acceptors in the major groove, allowing two guanidinium N–H donors to make a paired interaction without breaking Watson–Crick pairing. It therefore cannot make the same donor-to-two-acceptors match with guanidinium. A single N7 contact would be less discriminating. The classic structural recognition analysis explicitly proposed paired arginine–guanine contacts in the major groove.[ref] protein geometry, hydration and phosphate contacts also affect sequence recognition; this local motif explains a preference rather than guaranteeing that every arginine-containing protein recognizes every G–C site.

View the backbone along N–Cα​ to display ϕ=∠(Ci−1′​,Ni​,Cαi​,Ci′​) in a Newman projection. The front amide nitrogen carries the preceding carbonyl and H, while the back Cα​ carries the next carbonyl, H and side chain. Rotation changes the contacts among these groups. A corresponding view along Cα​–C′ displays ψ. The peptide C′–N bond itself is approximately planar and is not the freely rotating bond in either definition.
glycine replaces the side chain by a second hydrogen. Removing Cβ​ removes many contacts that exclude backbone arrangements for other residues, so its allowed ϕ,ψ region is much broader and includes substantial positive-ϕ space. Its alpha carbon is also achiral, so the steric map is approximately mirror-symmetric between positive and negative torsions in the simple isolated-residue model.
proline closes a five-membered ring from Cα​ through three methylenes back to nitrogen. In the N–Cα​ projection, the nitrogen's side-chain arm and Cα​'s Cβ​ arm are tied together, preventing unrestricted relative rotation. Its ϕ is consequently confined near the usual negative-60∘ region rather than exploring the broad range of an acyclic amino acid. proline also lacks a backbone N–H donor when incorporated into an ordinary internal peptide. Ring closure restricts ϕ strongly, but does not freeze every backbone or ring degree of freedom. These differences are naturally represented by distinct Ramachandran plots; the original torsion/steric analysis established the basis for such comparisons.[ref]
