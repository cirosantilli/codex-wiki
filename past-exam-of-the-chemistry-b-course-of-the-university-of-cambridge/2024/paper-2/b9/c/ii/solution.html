oxidative addition inserts Pt into one B–Br bond. All three B–Br positions of the starting ring are equivalent, so there is no distinct choice among them before the first substitution. The product is the square-planar Pt(II) boryl bromide trans−[Pt(PCy3​)2​(Br)(B3​Br2​N3​H3​)], with the two phosphines trans, the Pt–B bond trans to Pt–Br, and the six-membered ring intact. The third panel shows this connectivity. This is a metal–boron sigma bond, not attachment of the whole ring as an η6 pi ligand. Formal electron counting gives Pt(II), d8, with 8+4P​+2Br​+2boryl​=16 electrons, appropriate for square-planar coordination. The primary synthesis establishes this trans-bromo(borazinyl) structure.[ref]
The electron-rich Pt(0) reacts at the polarized B–Br bond, whose electrophilic boron and departing bromide provide a favorable bond-activation pathway. This accounts for B functionalization without needing cleavage of a ring B–N bond or selection of a nitrogen-bound product. It does not imply that arbitrary N–H activation is impossible under every condition.
Replacing an electronegative bromine substituent by a metal fragment changes the boron's electron demand. Relative to the B–Br sites, the metal-bound boron is less effective at accepting N-to-B pi donation, so its adjacent B–N bonds are expected to have less multiple-bond character and be longer; donation toward the remaining B–Br sites produces compensating unequal ring bonds. The original threefold equivalence is lost. The detailed lengths depend on the full electronic structure; a localized drawing with three rigid alternating double bonds would conceal the redistribution that the unequal lengths reveal.
