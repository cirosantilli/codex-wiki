A glutamic acid residue supplies a carboxyl group whose protonation state can change with the active site environment. The following five schematic contributions are possible in a hypothetical enzyme; they are alternatives and overlapping physical effects, rather than five independently established reactions of this enzyme. In the diagrams E is the protein tether, and C6 is the reacting purine carbon.
general acid catalysis: E−CO2​H⋯N1→E−CO2−​+H−N1+. Donation of the carboxyl proton activates the amidine-like C6 center for water attack. The same acid could instead assist the amino leaving group: E−CO2​H+−NH2​→E−CO2−​+−NH3+​. These are two locations for general acid catalysis, rather than two different categories.
general base catalysis: E−CO2−​⋯H−OH→E−CO2​H+OH−, coupled to oxygen attack on C6. Accepting a proton makes water a more effective nucleophile. This does not require an appreciable pool of free hydroxide in bulk solution.
covalent catalysis: a glutamate oxygen can in principle attack activated C6 to form C6−O−C(O)−E. After addition/elimination replaces the amino group, water must hydrolyze that transient enzyme–substrate bond to release product and regenerate E−CO2​H/CO2−​. Such a pathway is a chemically distinct possibility, not established by the given structure.
electrostatic catalysis: the carboxylate can stabilize a developing positive charge, for example in a protonated purine or amino leaving group; a protonated carboxyl can also donate a hydrogen bond to an oxygen developing negative charge. This contributes only if the reacting configuration is stabilized relative to the bound starting state. A carboxylate placed next to an oxyanion without compensating geometry would instead be unfavorable.
proximity and orientation catalysis: donor and acceptor hydrogen bonds can hold the purine and attacking water in a productive arrangement, schematically E−CO2​H⋯N1−C6⋯OH2​. Binding reduces unproductive orientations and can distort the substrate toward a reactive geometry. This last contribution is sometimes described through substrate binding or strain; a binding interaction is not automatically catalytic unless it improves access to the transition state.
