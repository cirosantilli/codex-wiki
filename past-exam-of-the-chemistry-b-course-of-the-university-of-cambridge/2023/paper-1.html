The first reagent supplies a vinyl nucleophile, CH2​=CH−, rather than an allyl group. Two successive conjugate additions install vinyl at C2 and a 2-formylethyl chain at C1. Number the original nitro-bearing alkene carbon C1. Vinylmagnesium bromide attacks the beta carbon C2 of the nitroalkene, shifting the original C1=C2 electrons into a magnesium nitronate. The new C2–vinyl bond remains intact during the second stage; its terminal double bond is not the electrophilic site in that stage.
The carbon end of the nitronate at C1 then attacks the terminal beta carbon of acrolein. Move the acrolein C=C electrons towards its carbonyl and the C=O electrons onto oxygen. This forms the C1–CH2CH2CHO connectivity through an aldehyde enolate; protonation by methanol and tautomerization restore the aldehyde. The nitronate returns to a neutral nitro group as it makes the second C–C bond. Thus the chain is attached through the carbon formerly beta to the acrolein carbonyl, rather than by a direct addition to its aldehyde carbon.
After the first addition, C2 bears the vinyl group while the nitronate carbon C1 remains approximately planar. Approach of the second electrophile to the less crowded face, opposite the adjacent vinyl substituent, gives the new formylethyl chain trans to vinyl. Consequently nitro and vinyl are cis, as depicted: at C1 the chain and nitro group occupy opposite faces. This is a substrate-controlled diastereoselective tandem Michael addition. The reagents and starting material contain no chiral source, so the depicted product represents a racemic pair rather than an enantioselective synthesis. Steric facial selection explains the relative configuration without requiring a uniquely established magnesium aggregate or transition-state conformation.

A and C are chiral; B is achiral. In A, a quaternary ammonium stereocenter occurs because nitrogen has four different substituents: phenyl, methyl, ethyl, and the sulfinylethyl chain. It has no nitrogen lone pair available for ordinary pyramidal inversion of an amine. The sulfoxide sulfur is also stereogenic: its pyramidal arrangement distinguishes oxygen, methyl, the nitrogen-containing chain, and the lone-pair direction. There are two independent stereocenters and no internal symmetry, giving four configurations in two pairs of enantiomers. This is chirality of the cation; a bromide counterion does not remove it.
B can adopt a trans-1,4-dimethylcyclohexane chair with both methyl groups equatorial. An inversion centre at the chair centre exchanges its opposite carbon atoms and methyl groups. Its mirror image is therefore superposable: trans substituents in a flat ring drawing do not by themselves prove chirality. ring inversion gives the less favourable diaxial conformer without changing the trans configuration.
In C, tetrahedral phosphorus has four different substituent directions: oxo, methoxy, ethoxy, and phenoxy. C has a phosphorus stereocenter. Delocalization in the P–O bonds does not make these four ligand environments identical. A tetrahedral drawing and its mirror cannot be superposed without exchanging two ligands.

The bridged, sterically hindered biphenyl framework is twisted and does not rapidly interconvert the two faces of each methylene group on the NMR timescale. Label one hydrogen on each CH2 HA​ and the other HB​. Replacing HA​ versus HB​ by a test substituent gives diastereomeric environments, so these hydrogens are diastereotopic and need not have equal chemical shifts even in an achiral solvent. Molecular symmetry relates the two HA​ sites to each other and the two HB​ sites to each other.
Each methylene hydrogen undergoes geminal coupling to its partner, giving the two doublets, each accounting for two hydrogens overall. Their separation is 0.21 ppm; at operating frequency ν0​ in MHz it corresponds to 0.21ν0​ Hz. This separation is not the geminal coupling constant, which must be measured from the line spacing within a doublet. If the shift separation is not large compared with that coupling, an AB pattern with second-order distortion is expected rather than ideal first-order doublets. A racemic pair of twisted conformations still has these signals: enantiomers have the same spectrum in an achiral medium, while the two hydrogen environments within each molecule remain different.

In both cases the alkene undergoes concerted oxygen transfer from metachloroperbenzoic acid. The alkene pi bond forms one C–O bond to the terminal peroxide oxygen; electron redistribution forms the second C–O bond, breaks O–O, and transfers the peracid proton to its carbonyl oxygen as the carboxylic acid product forms. In a resonance description of ozone as O−−O+=O, the alkene pi pair bonds to one terminal oxygen while the other terminal oxygen forms the second C–O bond and the ozone pi bond shifts onto its central oxygen. Cleavage of this unstable primary ozonide produces a carbonyl compound and a carbonyl oxide, which recombine to form the secondary ozonide. Reductive workup breaks the peroxide bonds and gives the carbonyl fragments R−CH2​CHO and formaldehyde.
Excess sodium borohydride also reduces the newly formed aldehyde: hydride attacks its carbonyl carbon, shifting the pi electrons onto oxygen, and the final acidic workup protonates the alkoxide. Thus the retained chain becomes R−CH2​CH2​OH. The terminal alkene carbon becomes formaldehyde and can be reduced to methanol; it is no longer part of the main product. This accounts for the one-carbon shortening of the retained skeleton.
The original amide carbonyl is present in both H and I and is not reduced under the ordinary borohydride workup. The existing stereocenters and benzyl ether are retained. Stopping the mechanism at an aldehyde, or deleting the amide C=O when reading the smaller printed scheme, would give the wrong product. The new terminal alcohol carbon is CH2 and therefore adds no stereocenter.

The acceptor-strength trend is amide oxygen and amine nitrogen strongest, ketone oxygen weaker, hydrocarbon essentially nonaccepting. trimethylamine and isopropylamine each provide a relatively available nitrogen lone pair; adding N–H bonds changes donor ability much more conspicuously than it changes the acceptor ranking in this comparison. Alkyl substitution and solvation modify the precise values, so the matching amine parameters should be read as the stated empirical strengths rather than a universal equality of all amines.
In acetamide, nitrogen lone-pair donation contributes an O−−C=N+ form contributing to resonance in chemistry, increasing electron density at oxygen and making it a strong hydrogen bond acceptor. The amide acceptor is oxygen, not nitrogen: the nitrogen lone pair is delocalized into the carbonyl. acetone also accepts at oxygen, but lacks this nitrogen-to-carbonyl donation and has the smaller hydrogen-bond acceptor parameter. isobutane has neither a heteroatom lone pair nor a comparable acceptor site; its zero value is consistent with the absence of an ordinary directional hydrogen bond acceptor.

The clearest comparison is between the two amines, which have the same molecular formula and molar mass. isopropylamine can donate N–H hydrogen bonds to neighbouring nitrogen lone pairs; trimethylamine has no N–H donor. The resulting intermolecular association raises the primary amine's boiling point, allowing it to remain liquid at room temperature, whereas trimethylamine is gaseous. Independent phase-change data place their normal boiling points on opposite sides of room temperature.[ref]
acetone has a substantial carbonyl dipole and London dispersion forces sufficient for a room-temperature liquid, even though it cannot build a strong donor–acceptor hydrogen bond network by itself. isobutane has mainly London dispersion forces and a compact shape and remains gaseous. acetamide combines carbonyl acceptors with two N–H donors, allowing an extended, directionally bonded crystal lattice; favourable packing as well as intermolecular association stabilizes its solid state.
These compounds have similar molar masses, so mass alone cannot explain the differences. The hydrogen-bond acceptor parameter also cannot predict the phase on its own: a pure substance needs complementary donors to exploit strong acceptor sites, and freezing additionally depends on crystal packing and entropy. A stronger acceptor does not automatically imply a higher melting point for arbitrary molecules.

Transfer is favourable when new water–solute interactions compensate the cavity and reorganization costs of accommodating the solute. The Gibbs energy also includes the loss of solute–alkane interactions; it is not simply a count of hydrogen bonds. isobutane cannot compensate those costs with strong directional interactions, explaining its positive transfer Gibbs energy. acetone and both amines can accept hydrogen bonds from water and have favourable electrostatic solvation, making their transfer moderately favourable. isopropylamine can additionally donate to water, consistent with a somewhat greater preference for water than trimethylamine.
acetamide combines a strong carbonyl acceptor with N–H donors and has the most favourable hydration in the group, accounting for its much more negative transfer Gibbs energy. Its pure solid lattice is not an extra sublimation penalty to insert here: the stated process starts from a dilute alkane solution and ends in dilute water, rather than dissolving the pure crystal. The nonmonotonic relationship between hydrogen-bond acceptor parameter and transfer Gibbs energy is expected because donor strength, nonpolar surface, solvent cavity formation, and all other solvation contributions also enter.
