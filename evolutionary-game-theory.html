Evolutionary game theory

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization Game theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 4
Evolutionary game theory studies how a population's composition changes when the success of a pure strategy depends on which other pure strategies it encounters. The central feedback is a frequency-dependent payoff: becoming common changes the opponents a strategy meets, which can change whether it does well. In a coordination game, common behaviour can reinforce itself; in the coexistence example below, being rare is an advantage. This makes evolutionary game theory useful for asking which behaviours can persist and which initial compositions lead to different outcomes, without assuming that each participant solves an optimization problem.
In the basic model, a large, well-mixed population has finitely many inherited pure strategies; interactions sample opponents according to their population frequencies. Assume that a strategy's expected payoff determines its per capita net reproductive growth rate, with a common baseline allowed. These are modelling assumptions: reproduction must transmit the strategy, encounters must match the proposed sampling rule, and the conversion from payoff to growth must be justified for an application. The replicator equation below describes the resulting continuous-time frequencies. Taylor and Jonker's [ref] explicitly derive this growth model.
For learned behaviour, reproduction can instead be replaced by an assumed copying rule: an individual samples another individual uniformly and copies a better-performing pure strategy at a rate proportional to the positive payoff difference. At frequencies xi​,xj​, the net flow from j to i is xi​xj​(fi​−fj​), because the two directional positive parts subtract to fi​−fj​. Summing over j gives xi​(fi​−fˉ​), the replicator equation. This derivation states a particular mechanism; an arbitrary rule for learning or imitation need not give the same dynamical system.
Three questions must be distinguished. A symmetric Nash equilibrium tests whether an individual can improve its expected payoff against the resident composition. An evolutionarily stable strategy tests whether each distinct rare mutant is outperformed after it is introduced. Dynamical stability tests the response of an actual dynamical system to small changes of composition, and asymptotic stability additionally requires nearby trajectories to converge. For a finite symmetric bimatrix game under the continuous-time replicator equation, every evolutionarily stable strategy is a symmetric Nash equilibrium and a locally asymptotically stable rest point; the converse from asymptotic stability to an evolutionarily stable strategy fails in general. See Ross Cressman and Yi Tao, The replicator equation and other game dynamics (2014), Theorems 1–2 and Example 1. These statements refer to stability on the full probability simplex, including perturbations introducing currently absent pure strategies.
A concrete experiment makes the frequency feedback visible. In 2009, Gore, Youk and van Oudenaarden competed two yeast strains: a producer of the enzyme invertase, which enables sucrose hydrolysis, and a mutant lacking the corresponding SUC2 gene. Released sugars can feed neighbours, so avoiding production can be advantageous. They used shaken 5 mL cultures with 5% sucrose at 30∘C, diluted daily after 23 hours of growth. Different labelled proteins allowed the two strains to be counted separately by flow cytometry; selective plating periodically checked the counts. The cooperator required externally supplied histidine, so restricting that amino acid imposed an adjustable growth disadvantage. See Snowdrift game dynamics and facultative cheating in yeast, pp. 253 and 255–256, Methods Summary.
Starting with rare producers and, separately, rare nonproducers tested both invasion directions. Under the reported coexistence conditions each rare strain increased; changing histidine or added glucose shifted the eventual composition. Their explanation used nonlinear growth benefits, rather than a fixed pairwise payoff matrix. This motivates testing both rarity regimes before inferring an evolutionarily stable strategy or fitting a replicator equation; it does not identify the illustrative tables below with measured payoffs. See Gore, Youk and van Oudenaarden, Figures 1–3.
An early motivation was understanding why animal contests can involve restraint even when escalation might win an individual encounter. Maynard Smith and Price's [ref] introduced the evolutionarily stable strategy in this setting. Taylor and Jonker's [ref] then studied explicit continuous and discrete dynamics. Static resistance to invasion and the paths populations follow are related questions, but they require separate definitions.
