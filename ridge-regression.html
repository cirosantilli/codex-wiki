Ridge regression (RSS+λ∥β∥22​)

 ... Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Statistical model Statistical modelling Normal linear model Linear regression
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 9
Ridge regression fits a linear regression model while penalizing the size of its slope regression coefficients. For a fixed n×p design matrix X containing the slope predictors, observed responses Y, and an unpenalized regression intercept α, the objective is
(αλ​,β​λ​)=α∈R,β∈Rpargmin​{∥Y−α1−Xβ∥22​+λ∥β∥22​},λ≥0.
(7)
Here 1 is the all-ones vector, and ∥β∥22​=∑j​βj2​ is the squared Euclidean norm of the slopes alone. The first term is the residual sum of squares; the second implements regularization. No random-error assumption is needed to define this fit. The convention is a summed squared-error loss: writing the objective as n−1RSS+λavg​∥β∥22​ gives the same fit when λ=nλavg​.[ref]
For an exact example, reuse the constructed pairs (xi​,Yi​)=(0,1),(1,2),(2,2),(3,4),(4,5) from linear regression. Their sample means are xˉ=2 and Yˉ=14/5, and the centered cross-products are ∑i​(xi​−2)2=10 and ∑i​(xi​−2)(Yi​−14/5)=10. Thus
β​λ​=10+λ10​,αλ​=514​−2β​λ​,Yi​=514​+β​λ​(xi​−2).
(8)
The ordinary least squares line has slope 1 and residual sum of squares 4/5. At λ=10, ridge regression has slope 1/2, regression intercept 9/5, and residual sum of squares 33/10; at λ=30 these become 1/4, 23/10, and 257/40. Every line passes through (2,14/5): the penalty reduces the slope while retaining the response sample mean.
The computational benefit is visible after predictor centering. Write Xc​ for the design matrix with each column's sample mean subtracted, and Yc​=Y−Yˉ1. The unpenalized intercept in ridge regression gives αλ​=Yˉ−xˉTβ​λ​, and the closed-form ridge regression estimator solves
(XcT​Xc​+λIp​)β​λ​=XcT​Yc​.
(9)
For any nonzero vector v and λ>0, vT(XcT​Xc​+λIp​)v=∥Xc​v∥22​+λ∥v∥22​>0. This positive-definite matrix is therefore invertible even when Xc​ lacks full column rank, including repeated columns or p≥n. The unique penalized slopes resolve the ambiguity of rank-deficient ordinary least squares; they do not supply evidence about slope directions absent from the data. This makes ridge regression useful for multicollinearity.[ref]
Predictor centering and scaling serve different purposes. Centering handles the free regression intercept. Scaling determines what counts as a large slope: replacing xj​ by cxj​ changes its coefficient to βj​/c for the same predictions, changing its contribution to an unweighted penalty. A common convention forms standard scores zij​=(xij​−xˉj​)/sj​ using each nonconstant predictor's sample standard deviation sj​, then penalizes slopes θj​ in those coordinates. Since θj​=sj​βj​, this is the weighted penalty λ∑j​sj2​βj2​ in the original units. Constant columns have sj​=0 and must be removed before this scaling. Scaling is a modelling choice; equal penalties in original units and in standardized units define different fits.[ref]
The singular value decomposition explains the shrinkage. If Xc​=UDVT is the compact decomposition using the r positive singular values dj​, with columns uj​ of U, then
Yλ​=Yˉ1+∑j=1r​dj2​+λdj2​​uj​ujT​Yc​.
(10)
The principal-component shrinkage by ridge regression factors are between 0 and 1: directions with small singular values are damped most. As λ→∞, the fitted values approach Yˉ1; the vanishing-penalty ridge limit recovers the ordinary least squares fitted vector as λ↓0, even if the slope estimates at zero penalty are nonunique. The covariance and bias of a ridge regression estimator describes the resulting bias-variance tradeoff. A penalty can reduce variance of an estimator while introducing bias of an estimator; the unbounded directional risk of fixed ridge shrinkage rules out a uniform improvement for a fixed positive penalty over all possible signals.[ref]
For a second exact construction, choose a centered orthonormal set of three vectors,
u1​=21​(1,−1,1,−1)T,u2​=21​(1,1,−1,−1)T,u3​=21​(1,−1,−1,1)T,
(11)
and set X=(3u1​,u2​) and Y=21+3u1​+u2​+21​u3​. The squared singular values are 9 and 1. At λ=1, the fitted coordinates along u1​,u2​ are (27/10,1/2), retaining 9/10 and 1/2 of their ordinary least squares values (3,1). At λ=9 they become (3/2,1/10), retaining 1/2 and 1/10. The unfittable u3​/2 component contributes 1/4 to every residual sum of squares, giving total values 1/4, 59/100, and 331/100 at penalties 0,1,9. These are constructed fits, not measured prediction results.
The penalty λ is a hyperparameter, so fitting slopes at a fixed value and selecting that value are separate tasks. Use a validation set or K-fold cross-validation to compare candidate penalties by held-out squared-error loss. In each split, compute predictor sample means and scales from that split's training data, apply the same transformation to its validation set, and refit the slopes; using held-out information in preprocessing introduces data leakage. Minimum training residual sum of squares cannot select useful shrinkage: ordinary least squares already attains the smallest possible value.[ref] After model selection, refit on all training data with the selected penalty. A separate test set or nested cross-validation assesses the entire selection procedure; a low selected validation score alone is no performance guarantee.[ref]
