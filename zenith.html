Zenith

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Horizontal coordinate system
OurBigBook.com Words: 238 Articles: 1
The zenith is the point on the celestial sphere in the direction of the observer's local upward vertical. In the horizontal coordinate system, its unit vector is eU​ and its altitude is 90∘. The zenith belongs to the observer's site; it is not generally the north celestial pole, whose altitude depends on geographical latitude. A direction's zenith distance is its nonnegative angle from the zenith, so z=90∘−γ. A direction of altitude 30∘ therefore has zenith distance 60∘. [ref].
At the zenith, every value of azimuth produces (E,N,U)=(0,0,1). There is no horizontal component to identify a bearing: atan2(0,0) cannot provide a geometrically meaningful azimuth, even if software returns a number. This coordinate singularity is a defect of the angular description; the viewing direction itself remains regular. The same ambiguity occurs at the opposite downward vertical. Near the zenith, two directions at fixed altitude separated by a small azimuth increment dη have separation cosγ∣dη∣ to first order, with angles in radians. At altitude 89∘, a 1∘ azimuth increment spans only about 0.01745∘ on that small arc. An optical telescope can consequently require a large bearing change for a small change of pointing near the zenith; working with unit vectors avoids inventing a preferred bearing at the singular point.
