Galton-Watson process (Zn+1​=∑j=1Zn​​ξn,j​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 20
A Galton-Watson process is a branching process that counts individuals by generation. Start with one ancestor, Z0​=1, and let all offspring counts ξn,j​ be independent and identically distributed random variables taking nonnegative integer values with a common offspring distribution. Then
Zn+1​=∑j=1Zn​​ξn,j​,P(ξn,j​=k)=pk​,k≥0.
(1)
The empty sum is zero: zero is an absorbing state, so once a generation is empty every later generation is empty. The same offspring distribution applies to every individual in every generation. [ref]
For a concrete offspring distribution, take p0​=1/4, p1​=1/4, p2​=1/2, and pk​=0 for k≥3. An individual has no children with probability 1/4, one with probability 1/4, and two with probability 1/2. The offspring expected value is μ=5/4. A possible sequence of generation sizes is 1,2,1,0,0,…: two children need not lead to a permanently growing family.
The probability generating function F(s)=1/4+s/4+s2/2 gives the exact generation probability distributions through branching-process generating-function iteration. With G0​(s)=s and Gn+1​(s)=F(Gn​(s)), the second generation has
G2​(s)=3211​+81​s+329​s2+81​s3+81​s4.
(2)
Thus P(Z2​=0)=11/32 and E[Z2​]=25/16. The figure shows full generation probability mass functions, computed by polynomial function composition, through generation six; no simulated frequencies are used.
The contrast is striking: the expected generation size in a Galton-Watson process grows as (5/4)n, while the branching extinction probability is 1/2. By generation six, P(Z6​=0)≈0.462186 although E[Z6​]≈3.814697. Larger surviving families carry the rising expected value; that mean is an average over possible families, not a forecast of one family's path. The Galton-Watson extinction fixed point turns an unbounded number of future generations into a single equation for this probability.
The historical question was the disappearance of surnames passed through male descendants. In Watson and Galton, On the Probability of the Extinction of Families, pages 139–140, Francis Galton formulates a constant-law model counting sons who reach adulthood, and Watson computes later generation probability distributions by composing generating functions. Those are assumptions of their model, rather than a claim that actual human reproduction follows this offspring distribution.

Offspring distribution of a branching process (pk​=P(ξ=k),F(s)=∑k≥0​pk​sk)

The offspring probability distribution of a Galton-Watson process is the law of one individual's number of children: pk​=P(ξ=k) for k≥0, with pk​≥0 and ∑k≥0​pk​=1. Its probability generating function and, when finite, its expected value are
F(s)=E[sξ]=∑k≥0​pk​sk,0≤s≤1,μ=E[ξ]=∑k≥0​kpk​=F′(1−).
(3)
Use 00=1 when evaluating the probability generating function at zero: F(0)=p0​ and F(1)=1.
For p0​=p1​=1/4 and p2​=1/2, F(s)=1/4+s/4+s2/2, μ=5/4, and the offspring variance is 11/16: E[ξ2]=9/4 and Var(ξ)=9/4−(5/4)2. The generation probability mass functions pictured under Galton-Watson process come from repeatedly composing this probability generating function. They describe the whole generation, whereas (pk​) describes one individual's reproduction. [ref]
The whole offspring distribution matters for extinction. Another law, p0​=3/8 and p2​=5/8, has the same offspring mean 5/4 but branching extinction probability 3/5, since 3/8+5s2/8=s has roots of a polynomial 3/5 and 1. Consequently the two laws have the same expected generation size in a Galton-Watson process at each generation but different survival chances. A binomial branching process and a Poisson branching process likewise specify different offspring distributions; a common mean does not make their probability generating functions identical.

Fractional-power offspring generating function (G(s)=1−α(1−s)β)

Words: 70
Offspring distribution of a branching process OurBigBook.com
For 0<α,β<1, this probability generating function has probabilities p0​=1−α and pk​=αβ(1−β)⋯(k−1−β)/k! for k≥1, where the product after β is empty for k=1. All probabilities are positive and sum to one, while the expected value is infinite. In a Galton-Watson process starting with one individual, the generation-n probability generating function is Gn​(s)=1−α(1−βn)/(1−β)(1−s)βn. The branching extinction probability is 1−α1/(1−β), obtained by taking the limit of Gn​(0) and using that extinction by generation n is an increasing sequence of events.

Branching process conditioned on extinction (fq​(s)=f(qs)/q)

Words: 65
Galton-Watson process OurBigBook.com
Let q∈(0,1] be the branching extinction probability of a Galton-Watson process with offspring probability generating function f. Conditional on extinction, the probability of j children is multiplied by qj/q: every child's descendants must also become extinct. Hence the new probability generating function is fq​ and its mean is f′(q). If f′(q)<1, the total progeny of a branching process has conditional expected value 1/(1−f′(q)), and unconditionally E[T;T<∞]=q/(1−f′(q)). The Markov inequality gives a finite-total-progeny tail bound without discarding the survival mass.

Poisson branching process (f(s)=eμ(s−1))

Words: 105 Articles: 1
Galton-Watson process OurBigBook.com
A Galton-Watson process with a Poisson distribution of offspring of mean μ has this probability generating function. For μ=1+ε>1 its branching survival probability r satisfies −log(1−r)=(1+ε)r, and 2ε/(1+2ε)≤r≤2ε. Indeed ε=∑j≥1​rj/(j+1) lies between r/2 and r/[2(1−r)]. These exact inequalities belong to the Poisson branching process, not to every finite binomial branching process.

A Galton-Watson process whose offspring law is a binomial distribution Bin(n,p) has mean np and the displayed offspring probability generating function. Its branching extinction probability is the smallest root of q=f(q) in [0,1]. It dominates a breadth-first exploration of a binomial random graph by allowing each explored vertex up to n independent possible children, including previously exposed or already discovered vertices as phantom children.

Binomial branching survival correction (ρ=2ε+O(ε2+ε/n))

For p=(1+ε)/n, the branching survival probability satisfies 1−ρ=(1−pρ)n. If (1+ε)2<n, comparison with a Poisson branching process and expansion of the logarithm give
1+2ε2ε​≤ρ≤1−(1+ε)2/n2ε​.
(4)
For the upper bound use ε=∑j≥1​(1−npj+1)ρj/(j+1) and retain its first nonnegative term. For fixed n>1, the leading term is instead 2nε/(n−1). In particular n=2 gives ρ=4ε/(1+ε)2, showing that the uncorrected exact bound ρ≤2ε is false for small positive ε.
For 0<ε≤1/4 and nε≥2, the uncorrected exact upper bound does hold for the binomial branching process. Here np2≤ε and np3≤1/4, so at x=2ε the first two terms of the nonnegative series give ε(1−np2)+(4/3)ε2(1−np3)≥ε. Monotonicity of that series yields ρ≤2ε. This recovers the intended large-n bracket without claiming it for every finite reproduction law.

Galton-Watson extinction fixed point (q=min{s∈[0,1]:F(s)=s})

Words: 637 Articles: 3
Galton-Watson process OurBigBook.com
For a Galton-Watson process with one ancestor and offspring probability generating function F, the branching extinction probability q is the smallest fixed point of F in [0,1]. A direct way to find it is to compute extinction by generation n:
q0​=0,qn​=P(Zn​=0),qn+1​=F(qn​),qn​↑q.
(6)
Zero is an absorbing state, so extinction by generation n is exactly the event Zn​=0. [ref]
For F(s)=1/4+s/4+s2/2, the equation is
F(s)−s=21​(s−21​)(s−1)=0.
(7)
Both 1/2 and 1 are fixed points, but iteration from zero selects q=1/2. The first values are q1​=1/4, q2​=11/32, q3​=809/2048≈0.395020, and q6​≈0.462186. Iteration started at 1 would stay at 1 forever and would give the wrong extinction answer for this offspring distribution.
The vertical steps in the figure apply the offspring probability generating function; horizontal steps return to the diagonal to start the next iteration. This gives a useful numerical calculation even when solving F(s)=s explicitly is difficult. For the displayed law, every finite iteration is a lower bound for the eventual branching extinction probability; at generation six, about 0.037814 additional extinction probability remains beyond the current empty families.
Why the smallest fixed point? The ancestor's children's descendant families are independent; given k children, all are empty n generations later with probability qnk​. Averaging gives qn+1​=∑k​pk​qnk​=F(qn​). The increasing extinction events give the limit q, and continuity of the probability generating function yields F(q)=q. For any other fixed point r∈[0,1], q0​≤r and monotonicity of F imply qn​≤r at every step; hence q≤r. [ref]

Laziness invariance of branching extinction (Gq​(z)=qz+(1−q)G(z),q<1)

Words: 65
Galton-Watson extinction fixed point OurBigBook.com
For a Galton-Watson process, replacing an individual's reproduction by a one-descendant uneventful step with probability q<1 changes its offspring probability generating function to the displayed mixture. Its fixed-point equation reduces to G(z)=z, so the smallest fixed point and hence the extinction probability of a branching process are unchanged. The mean is q+(1−q)G′(1), which also preserves the subcritical/critical/supercritical classification. The excluded case q=1 never reproduces or dies and need not share the extinction probability.

Extinction probability of a branching process (q=P(∃j:Zj​=0))

Words: 318 Articles: 1
Galton-Watson extinction fixed point OurBigBook.com
For a Galton-Watson process with one ancestor, the branching extinction probability is the probability that some generation is empty:
q=P(⋃n≥0​{Zn​=0})=limn→∞​P(Zn​=0).
(8)
The equality uses the absorbing state at zero. The complementary branching survival probability 1−q is the probability that every generation is nonempty. The Galton-Watson extinction fixed point computes q from the offspring distribution.
For p0​=p1​=1/4, p2​=1/2, one ancestor has q=1/2. Starting instead from m ancestors with independent descendant families gives
P(eventual extinction∣Z0​=m)=qm,P(Zn​=0∣Z0​=m)=qnm​.
(9)
All m families must die out. With three ancestors, eventual extinction has probability 1/8 and survival has probability 7/8; by generation two, extinction has probability (11/32)3=1331/32768≈0.040619. Extra ancestors change the extinction probability without changing the offspring distribution. [ref]
For finite offspring mean μ, the extinction classification is:
  • If μ<1, extinction has probability 1. For example p0​=3/4, p2​=1/4 gives μ=1/2 and F(s)=3/4+s2/4; its only fixed point in [0,1] is 1.
  • If μ=1 and p1​=1, extinction also has probability 1. For example p0​=p2​=1/2 gives F(s)−s=(s−1)2/2 and q=1, despite expected generation size in a Galton-Watson process remaining one.
  • If 1<μ<∞, extinction has probability q<1. The value can be zero: if p0​=0, every individual has at least one child, so an initially nonempty family never becomes empty. In particular p2​=1 gives Zn​=2n and q=0.
The critical exception is p1​=1: every individual has exactly one child, F(s)=s, and Zn​=1 forever from one ancestor. Thus μ=1 but q=0; a blanket claim that every critical Galton-Watson process becomes extinct would be false. These cases concern the Galton-Watson process with its common offspring distribution and independence assumptions. [ref]
This probability answers whether any lineage persists, which the offspring mean alone cannot quantify in the supercritical case. A branching process conditioned on extinction uses the same fixed point to reweight reproduction and describe families known eventually to die out.

The expected value is useful for computing average population counts and, in the subcritical case, the expected value of the total progeny of a branching process. (11)
The tower property of conditional expectation gives the second equality; starting from E[Z0​]=1 gives the formula. It describes an average over the generation probability distribution, not deterministic growth of an individual family. [ref]
