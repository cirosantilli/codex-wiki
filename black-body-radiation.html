Black-body radiation (u=arad​T4,p=u/3)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Planck's law
OurBigBook.com Words: 426 Articles: 2
Black-body radiation is the electromagnetic radiation in thermal equilibrium with an ideal blackbody. It can be described as a photon gas: a cavity whose walls are all at the same temperature contains radiation with the universal Planck's law spectrum. A small opening in such a cavity approximates a blackbody emitter because incoming radiation undergoes repeated absorption inside. The radiation's equilibrium spectrum depends on temperature, rather than the wall material. [ref]
There are three useful quantities to keep distinct: directional spectral radiance Bν​ or Bλ​, total energy density u in the cavity, and outward radiant exitance j∗ from its opening. Planck's law supplies the first; integrals over frequency and directions supply the others. For isotropy, every direction has the same Bν​, so the full 4π solid angle gives
u(T)=c4π​∫0∞​Bν​(T)dν=arad​T4,arad​=15h3c38π5kB4​​=15ℏ3c3π2kB4​​.
(100)
Here ℏ=h/(2π) is the reduced Planck constant, and the radiation constant is arad​≈7.565733250×10−16Jm−3K−4. The explicit Boltzmann constant makes this an SI formula; adopting natural units with kB​=1 recovers u=π2T4/(15ℏ3c3).
For a short integral outline, substitute x=hν/(kB​T) and use
∫0∞​ex−1x3​dx=∑n=1∞​∫0∞​x3e−nxdx=6∑n=1∞​n41​=15π4​.
(101)
The expansion is a geometric series with nonnegative terms; repeated integration by parts evaluates each integral, and ζ(4)=π4/90 is a value of the Riemann zeta function. This gives the coefficient above.
The photon gas also exerts radiation pressure, p=u/3. The factor 1/3 comes from the angular average of the squared directional component of photon momentum, ⟨cos2θ⟩=1/3. The thermodynamic derivation of blackbody radiation pressure gives the same result. In contrast, outgoing power involves one projected-direction factor over an outward hemisphere, yielding radiant exitance
j∗=π∫0∞​Bν​(T)dν=4cu​=σT4,σ=15h3c22π5kB4​​.
(102)
This is the Stefan–Boltzmann law with unit emissivity; σ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant. The factor π follows from Lambert's cosine law, rather than the 4π full-sphere factor used for energy density.
For example, an ideal 6000K cavity has u≈0.980519Jm−3, radiation pressure p≈0.326840Pa, and outward radiant exitance j∗≈7.34881×107Wm−2. A stipulated 3000K cavity has one-sixteenth each value because all three scale as T4. These describe stored energy, mechanical pressure, and outgoing power per area, respectively. In a closed equilibrium cavity, equal opposing radiation balances the directional power flow even though u and p are nonzero.
The usefulness of black-body radiation is this common reference: Planck's law, Wien's displacement law, and the Stefan–Boltzmann law consistently connect spectral measurements, temperature, and total emission. The three illustrative curves in Planck's law are ideal equilibrium spectra; applying them to a material requires considering its emissivity and whether thermal equilibrium is a suitable approximation.
