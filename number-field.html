Number field ([K:Q]<∞)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Algebraic number theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 594 Articles: 9
A number field is a finite field extension K/Q of the rational numbers. Its degree of a field extension [K:Q] is its dimension as a vector space over Q. Every element is an algebraic number; the primitive element theorem also says that K=Q(θ) for some single algebraic number θ.
For example,
Q(2​)={a+b2​:a,b∈Q},[Q(2​):Q]=2.
(4)
The basis 1,2​ makes arithmetic explicit: use (2​)2=2 to reduce products. Every nonzero element has its inverse in the same field:
(a+b2​)−1=a2−2b2a−b2​​.
(5)
The denominator cannot vanish unless a=b=0, since 2​ is not a rational number. For instance, (3+2​)−1=(3−2​)/7 belongs to the number field, although it is not an algebraic integer.
A degree-n number field has exactly n distinct field embeddings into the complex numbers fixing Q, because every finite field extension in characteristic zero is a separable field extension. For Q(2​) these are the two real maps
σ+​(a+b2​)=a+b2​,σ−​(a+b2​)=a−b2​.
(6)
Both preserve addition and multiplication. Keeping both values, rather than just the positive choice of 2​, is what makes the field norm and the Minkowski embedding of a number field useful.
