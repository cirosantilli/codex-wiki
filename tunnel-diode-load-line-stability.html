Tunnel-diode load-line stability (1/R+I′(V)>0)

 Home Physics Semiconductor P-n junction Tunnel diode
OurBigBook.com Words: 447
A current-voltage characteristic determines possible steady operating points; it does not alone determine their linear stability. Consider an ideal voltage source Vs​ feeding a tunnel diode through a resistor R>0, with a positive constant capacitance C across the junction. Assume a quasistatic device relation I(V) and neglect inductance, transit delays, noise and heating. Kirchhoff's current law gives the original one-state model
CV˙=RVs​−V​−I(V).
(14)
A steady operating point V∗​ satisfies I(V∗​)=(Vs​−V∗​)/R: the device curve intersects the descending supply line. This is the source-side counterpart of a resistive load line. A small perturbation V=V∗​+δV obeys
CδV˙=−(R1​+I′(V∗​))δV,λ=−C1/R+I′(V∗​)​.
(15)
Thus the equilibrium point is locally attracting if 1/R+I′(V∗​)>0 and unstable if the inequality reverses. Equality requires higher-order analysis. Even on a negative differential resistance segment, a sufficiently small supply electrical resistance can stabilize this model. The assumption about the electrical circuit matters: an inductive or delayed circuit has additional state variables and may behave differently.
Choose an original toy forward characteristic, with no fitted device parameters:
x=V0​V​,I(V)=I0​i(x),i(x)=x3−6x2+10x=x((x−3)2+1),
(16)
where V0​=0.10V, I0​=1.0mA and 0≤x≤3.6. For every x>0, i(x)>0, so this entire forward curve absorbs positive total power. Nevertheless
I′(V)=V0​I0​​(3x2−12x+10)<0⟺2−36​​<x<2+36​​.
(17)
The model therefore separates negative differential resistance from negative total V/I. The corresponding peak and valley are at approximately 0.1184V and 0.2816V; these voltages deliberately differ from the historical peak.
With R=100Ω and Vs​=0.60V, the supply line is I/I0​=6−x. The intersection equation factors exactly:
i(x)+x−6=(x−1)(x−2)(x−3)=0.
(18)
The three operating points are (0.10V,5mA), (0.20V,4mA) and (0.30V,3mA). Their values of 1/R+I′(V∗​) are respectively 20, −10 and 20mS. The outer two attract nearby trajectories; the middle one repels them. This is bistability in the specified one-state electrical circuit.
For the second panel, choose R=25Ω and Vs​=0.30V. Now I/I0​=12−4x and
i(x)+4x−12=(x−2)((x−2)2+2)=0.
(19)
There is exactly one real operating point, again (0.20V,4mA). Here 1/R+I′(V∗​)=20mS>0, despite the device's own slope I′(V∗​)=−20mS. With C=10nF, the perturbation decays with time constant C/(20mS)=0.50μs; the unstable middle point of the first panel has growth time 1.0μs.
The one-state autonomous model supports switching between attracting operating points after a perturbation or parameter change; it cannot produce a nonconstant periodic orbit. A proposed oscillator needs additional dynamics. This limitation is useful: a load-line intersection establishes a possible steady reading, the slope test establishes local attraction in a specified model, and neither calculation alone predicts an oscillation frequency for a real tunnel diode.
