Central limit theorem (σn​Sn​−nμ​d​N(0,1))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Convergence of random variables
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 13
Let X1​,X2​,… be real independent and identically distributed random variables with expected value μ=E[X1​] and finite, strictly positive variance σ2=E[(X1​−μ)2]. Write Sn​=∑j=1n​Xj​ and Xn​=Sn​/n for their sum and sample mean. The central limit theorem states
Zn​=σn​Sn​−nμ​=σn​(Xn​−μ)​d​N(0,1).
(24)
Precisely, for every real z,
P(Zn​≤z)⟶Φ(z),Φ(z)=2π​1​∫−∞z​e−u2/2du,
(25)
where Φ is the standard normal distribution function. The conclusion is convergence in distribution to the standard normal distribution. If the variance is zero, every observation equals μ almost surely; the displayed normalization is then undefined and there is no fluctuating error to describe.
The striking feature is that the observations need not have a normal distribution. They may be binary, bounded, or strongly skewed. Their detailed probability distribution disappears from this limit except through the expected value and variance. The subtraction removes predictable growth: E[Sn​]=nμ. The division implements variance normalization: variance additivity for independent random variables gives Var(Sn​)=nσ2, so every Zn​ has expected value zero and variance one. For nonzero μ, an uncentered sum drifts; without rescaling its spread grows.
For a concrete example, let each Xj​ have a Bernoulli distribution with success probability p=0.2. Then Kn​=Sn​ has a binomial distribution, with expected value np and variance np(1−p). Its exact probability mass function and its normalized count are
P(Kn​=k)=(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k,k=0,…,n,Zn​=np(1−p)​Kn​−np​.
(26)
Here (kn​) is a binomial coefficient. Individual trials are conspicuously skewed, yet their centered, rescaled counts approach the same standard normal distribution as sums of fair trials.
At n=100, Kn​ has expected value 20 and standard deviation 4. The exact binomial distribution gives P(16≤Kn​≤24)=0.740141…. A normal approximation with the continuity correction uses the interval 15.5 to 24.5:
P(16≤Kn​≤24)≈Φ(424.5−20​)−Φ(415.5−20​)=0.739411….
(27)
The small discrepancy is an approximation error, not an equality supplied by the central limit theorem.
The strong law of large numbers and weak law of large numbers locate the average: Xn​→μ, respectively by almost sure convergence and convergence in probability. The central limit theorem magnifies that shrinking error and describes its shape. In terms of the sample mean, the resulting normal approximation is N(μ,σ2/n), with standard error σ/n​. This explains why four times as many independent observations halve the standard error. When σ is known, Xn​±1.96σ/n​ has approximately 95% coverage for μ at large n, giving a confidence interval. This coverage refers to repeated samples, not a guarantee for an individual interval.
A useful application is the Monte Carlo method: estimate a success probability by the proportion of successful simulated trials. Nicholas Metropolis and S. Ulam's original paper, "The Monte Carlo Method" (1949), illustrates this with games of solitaire and estimation of a region's volume by randomly selected points, and discusses why estimates require probabilistic error control. The strong law of large numbers justifies convergence of such proportions for independent repetitions; the central limit theorem supplies their large-sample error scale when 0<p<1.[ref]
There are three useful limits to this picture:
  • A heavy-tailed distribution can have infinite variance, so the displayed hypothesis must be checked. A Pareto distribution with minimum one and shape 3/2 has finite expected value three but infinite variance. The strong law of large numbers still applies, but the stated central limit theorem does not. More dramatically, the Cauchy sample mean has an unchanged distribution: even averaging need not concentrate when the expected value is undefined. A heavy-tailed distribution with finite variance is allowed by the stated central limit theorem.
  • Independence matters. If all Xj​ equal the same nonconstant Bernoulli random variable, then Sn​=nX1​ and Xn​=X1​. There is no accumulation of new information, and the variance of the sum grows like n2, not n. Other versions of the central limit theorem can allow dependence, but require hypotheses controlling it.
  • Convergence in distribution is a limiting assertion, with no universal sample size or error rate under finite variance alone. It does not automatically give accurate relative estimates of very small tail probabilities. Nor does it mean that discrete point masses converge as probability density functions: a binomial distribution remains discrete for every finite n. If the success probability changes with n so that npn​→λ and pn​→0, the relevant result is the Poisson limit theorem, rather than the fixed-p argument above.
A historical original-paper reference is J. W. Lindeberg's "Eine neue Herleitung des Exponentialgesetzes in der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung" (1922), published in Mathematische Zeitschrift 15, pp. 211–225. The Lindeberg condition and Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem below describe a version for sums whose summands need not share a probability distribution.[ref]
A brief proof outline uses the characteristic function of Y=(X1​−μ)/σ. Its expected value is zero and its variance is one, so the finite second moment gives
φY​(u)=1−2u2​+o(u2)(u→0).
(28)
The Taylor expansion of the complex exponential, with its remainder controlled by Y2, and the dominated convergence theorem justify this step without a third-moment assumption. Independence then gives the characteristic function of Zn​:
φZn​​(t)=(φY​(t/n​))n=(1−2nt2​+o(n−1))n⟶e−t2/2.
(29)
The limit is the characteristic function of the standard normal distribution; the Lévy continuity theorem turns this into convergence in distribution.
