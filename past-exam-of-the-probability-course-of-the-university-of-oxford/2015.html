The sample maximum has cumulative distribution function
FMn​​(x)=P(X1​≤x,…,Xn​≤x)=⎩⎨⎧​0,xn,1,​x<0,0≤x≤1,x>1.​
(1)
The product xn follows from independence of random variables and the continuous uniform distribution of each observation. For every x<1, this cumulative distribution function tends to zero; for x>1 it equals one. These are exactly the values of the cumulative distribution function of the constant random variable 1 at its continuity points. Therefore, by the definition of convergence in distribution,
Mn​d​1.​
(2)
At x=1 the limiting cumulative distribution function is discontinuous, so the definition does not require checking that point, although FMn​​(1)=1 also holds.

The moment-generating function of a real random variable X is
MX​(t)=E[etX]​
(5)
for real t for which this expected value is finite. Because the integrand is nonnegative, the extended expected value is always defined, but it may equal +∞. We always have MX​(0)=1; the usual uniqueness and convergence theorems require finiteness on an open interval containing zero. When this condition holds, derivatives at zero recover the moments, MX(r)​(0)=E[Xr].

The Poisson process gives Nk​∼Pois(k), with expected value and variance both equal to k. Consequently Yk​=(Nk​−k)/k​. Scaling and translating the argument in the moment-generating function of a Poisson distribution gives, for every real t,
MYk​​(t)​=e−tk​MNk​​(t/k​)=exp{k(et/k​−1−k​t​)}.​​

The joint probability density of the two independent standard normal random variables is (2π)−1e−(x2+y2)/2. The polar coordinates map has absolute Jacobian determinant r, so the change of variables for probability densities gives
fR,Θ​(r,θ)=2πr​e−r2/21{r>0, 0≤θ<2π}​.​
(15)
At the origin the angle may be assigned arbitrarily: that point has probability zero. The joint probability density factorizes as a Rayleigh distribution density re−r2/2 and a continuous uniform distribution density 1/(2π). Hence R and Θ are independent.
For u>0, substitute s=r2/2 to find
P(R2≤u)=∫0u​​re−r2/2dr=1−e−u/2.​
(16)
Therefore R2∼Exp(1/2), equivalently χ22​, and applying a measurable function to R preserves its independence from Θ. Explicitly, with S=R2,
fΘ,S​(θ,s)=4π1​e−s/21{0≤θ<2π, s>0}​.​
(17)
Thus Θ∼Unif[0,2π) and S∼Exp(1/2) independently. This polar decomposition of a planar standard normal vector separates the uniformly distributed direction from its random squared distance.

Put q=1−p and ξi​=Yi​−Yi−1​. The Markov property and the state-independent transition probabilities show that, conditional on the whole previous history, ξi​ is +1 with probability p and −1 with probability q. Multiplying these conditional probabilities for any finite step sequence proves that the ξi​ are independent and identically distributed random variables, with
Eξi​=p−q=2p−1>0.
(28)
Because Yn​=k+∑i=1n​ξi​, the strong law of large numbers gives
nYn​​=nk​+n1​i=1∑n​ξi​a.s.​μ=2p−1.​
(29)
In particular, Yn​→+∞ almost surely. This stronger pathwise consequence will determine both eventual crossings and the asymptotic first-passage times.

For k=0, the target is reached at time zero, so h0​=1. For k<0, the preceding almost sure convergence gives Yn​→+∞ with probability one. A sample path with unit upward and downward steps must visit every intermediate integer when it crosses from a negative value to a positive value. It must therefore visit zero at a finite time. Thus
hk​=1for every k≤0.​

The unit-step property matters: positive long-run displacement alone would not force a walk with larger upward jumps to land exactly on zero.

iii OurBigBook.com
The first-step analysis gives h0​=1 and, for k≥1,
hk​=phk+1​+qhk−1​.
(31)
The minimal nonnegative solution for hitting probabilities theorem states that the probability of ever hitting a target is the pointwise smallest nonnegative solution of this transition recurrence with value one at the target. It follows by comparing any such solution with the probabilities of hitting within n steps, then taking their increasing limit.
The characteristic equation pr2−r+q=0 has distinct roots 1 and β=q/p∈(0,1). Hence every solution on the nonnegative integers with boundary value one has the form
gk​=A+(1−A)βk.
(32)
If it is nonnegative for all k, taking k→∞ forces A≥0, and therefore gk​−βk=A(1−βk)≥0. The candidate gk​=βk is itself nonnegative and satisfies the recurrence and boundary value. It is consequently the minimal solution, giving the biased random walk hitting probability
hk​=(p1−p​)k,k>0;β=p1−p​.​
(33)
On the negative half-line the already established values hk​=1 satisfy the same recurrence away from zero. No unproved boundary condition at infinity has been imposed.

Since Yn​→+∞ almost surely, only finitely many early values can attain the global minimum; it is a finite integer. Also W≤Y0​=0. For every nonnegative integer m, the unit-step property and translation invariance of the increments give
P(−W≥m)=P0​(ever visit −m)=Pm​(ever visit 0)=βm,β=p1−p​.

1−β=p2p−1​.​

Work on the single event of probability one where Yn​/n→μ=2p−1>0. On every sample path in that event, Yn​→+∞ and the unit-step property ensures that every positive integer j is reached, so Tj​<∞. Starting from zero requires at least j steps to reach j, hence Tj​≥j and Tj​→∞.
The convergent numerical sequence Yn​/n on this fixed sample path also converges along its possibly random subsequence n=Tj​. Since YTj​​=j,
Tj​j​=Tj​YTj​​​⟶μ.
(37)
Taking the reciprocal is valid because μ>0. We obtain
P(jTj​​⟶α)=1,α=2p−11​.​
(38)
This is a first-passage law of large numbers for an upward skip-free random walk. No independence between the hitting times and the path is needed: almost sure convergence already supplies convergence along every diverging subsequence on the same event.
