Separate a long future count from the early Poisson arrival time. Let Ft​=σ(Ns​:0≤s≤t) be the natural filtration, and define
An​=n2Nn4​−Nn2​−(n4−n2)​,Bn​=n​min(Tn​,n2)−n​,Cn​=n2Nn2​−n2​.
(12)
The truncated Poisson arrival time in Bn​ is Fn2​-measurable. The independent increments property makes An​ independent of Fn2​, and hence of Bn​.
The numerator of An​ is a centered Poisson random variable of parameter n4−n2. Apply the central limit theorem to the n4−n2 unit-time increments, then use n4−n2​/n2→1 and Slutsky's theorem. This proves An​d​N(0,1). Also, Markov's inequality and ETn​=n give
P(Tn​>n2)≤n1​⟶0.
(13)
Thus Bn​ equals (Tn​−n)/n​ outside an event of vanishing probability, so part (i) and Slutsky's theorem give Bn​d​N(0,1). Exact independence of random variables for each n now gives the joint limit (An​,Bn​)d​(Z1​,Z2​) with independent standard normal random variables.
Finally, ECn​=0 and Var(Cn​)=n−2, so Chebyshev's inequality gives Cn​→0 in probability. The first coordinate in the question is An​+Cn​. The second differs from Bn​ only on {Tn​>n2}, and so its difference tends to zero in probability. The vector version of Slutsky's theorem proves the required joint convergence in distribution. The boundary of (−∞,x]×[y,∞) has zero limiting probability, since both marginal distributions have continuous cumulative distribution functions. The Portmanteau theorem therefore yields
P(n2Nn4​−n4​≤x,n​Tn​−n​≥y)⟶Φ(x)(1−Φ(y)).​
(14)
This is P(Z1​≤x,Z2​≥y) by independence of random variables.
