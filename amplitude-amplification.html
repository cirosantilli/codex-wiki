Amplitude amplification

 Home Physics Branches of physics Quantum theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 648 Articles: 6
Amplitude amplification increases the probability of obtaining an answer accepted by a specified Boolean function. It requires a coherent preparation A, its inverse, and a marked-state phase oracle that negates accepted components. Alternating that sign change with a reflection operator about the prepared state directs interference toward the accepted subspace. Brassard, Høyer, Mosca, and Tapp developed the general framework, extending Grover's algorithm beyond a uniform quantum superposition. [ref].
If a normalized quantum state has good amplitude sinθ, the product of the reflection in the bad axis and the reflection in the state line rotates its good-bad plane by 2θ. After k iterations the good probability is
sin2((2k+1)θ),
(9)
so O(1/sinθ) iterations raise a small good amplitude close to one. Success subsequently oscillates, making the stopping time part of the algorithm.
Compute the exact Grover search on four entries directly. Order the computational basis as 00,01,10,11 and mark 11. The Hadamard transform prepares amplitudes (1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2). The marked-state phase oracle changes these to (1/2,1/2,1/2,−1/2), whose arithmetic mean is a=1/4. The Grover diffusion operator performs inversion about the mean:
ax​⟼2a−ax​,(21​,21​,21​,−21​)⟼(0,0,0,1).
(10)
The three unmarked amplitudes become 1/2−1/2=0, while the marked amplitude becomes 1/2−(−1/2)=1. All three stages have squared norm one. Here sinθ=1/2, so θ=π/6 and one iteration gives sin2(3θ)=1, agreeing with the explicit calculation.
Grover's original search paper was submitted on 29 May 1996; its algorithm combines selective sign changes with inversion about the mean. The example exposes the beauty of that construction: a change initially invisible to computational-basis measurement becomes complete cancellation of every wrong answer. [ref].
The marked-state phase oracle is an assumed operation, whose implementation cost must be counted. Computational-basis measurement yields a candidate label, here 11; using it to retrieve a record requires the relevant data access. Preparing a quantum superposition does not make all its entries available as classical outputs. The improvement concerns the number of oracle queries needed to obtain an accepted label.
