Each simple pendulum moves in the vertical plane containing the pivots. Ignoring contact between the ideal rods and point masses, each rod can turn through a full circle. Thus the configuration space is the torus
Q=S1×S1.​
(1)
Choose generalized coordinates θ1​,θ2​, measured from the downward vertical, positive towards the right and understood modulo 2π. These angles are local coordinates on the torus, rather than a single globally one-to-one real coordinate chart. With upward Cartesian coordinate y, the bob positions are
r1​=(lsinθ1​,−lcosθ1​),r2​=(r+lsinθ2​,−lcosθ2​).
(2)
Their velocities satisfy ∣r˙j​∣2=l2θ˙j2​, so kinetic energy from Cartesian position vectors gives
T=2ml2​(θ˙12​+θ˙22​).
(3)
The spring length is the Euclidean distance
d=∣r2​−r1​∣=(r+l(sinθ2​−sinθ1​))2+l2(cosθ2​−cosθ1​)2​.
(4)
Taking gravitational potential energy zero at pivot height gives Vg​=−mgl(cosθ1​+cosθ2​). The spring potential energy is Vs​=k(d−r)2/2, since r is its natural length. Consequently the Lagrangian is
L=T−Vg​−Vs​=2ml2​(θ˙12​+θ˙22​)+mgl(cosθ1​+cosθ2​)−2k​(d−r)2.​
(5)
In the paper's notation t(θ1​,θ2​)=Vs​; this function is distinct from the time variable. This is the exact Lagrangian of two spring-coupled pendulums.
One alternative Lagrangian is
L=L−2mgl.​
(6)
It corresponds to setting gravitational potential energy zero at the downward position. The constant disappears from every Euler-Lagrange equation. More generally, total-time-derivative invariance of a Lagrangian allows L=L+dF(θ1​,θ2​,t)/dt for any smooth F.

Set l=m=g=1. At (θ1​,θ2​)=(0,0) the spring has d=r, so its potential energy has zero gradient: ∂j​Vs​=k(d−r)∂j​d=0. The gravitational potential energy has ∂j​Vg​=sinθj​=0 there. Zero velocities therefore satisfy the Euler-Lagrange equations, proving this is an equilibrium.
For fixed r>0, let ε=max(∣θ1​∣,∣θ2​∣) and δ=θ2​−θ1​. The Taylor series give
sinθ2​−sinθ1​=δ+O(ε3),cosθ2​−cosθ1​=O(ε2).
(7)
Thus, near the downward equilibrium, the horizontal separation is positive and
d=(r+δ+O(ε3))2+O(ε4)​=r+δ+O(ε3).
(8)
In particular, Vs​=kδ2/2+O(ε4) and Vg​=−2+(θ12​+θ22​)/2+O(ε4). Dropping the additive constant and terms beyond quadratic order gives the harmonic approximation
Lquadratic​=21​(θ˙12​+θ˙22​)−21​(θ12​+θ22​+k(θ1​−θ2​)2).​
(9)
For physical spring constant k≥0, the quadratic potential energy is a positive-definite quadratic form. The downward position is therefore a stable equilibrium in Lagrangian mechanics, and its nearby motion admits small oscillations.

The Euler-Lagrange equations of the quadratic Lagrangian are
θ¨+Kθ=0,K=(1+k−k​−k1+k​).
(10)
The mechanical mass matrix is the identity. A normal mode θ=acos(ωt+ϕ) therefore satisfies the eigenvalue problem Ka=ω2a. Direct multiplication gives
in phaseopposite phase​angular frequencyω+​=1ω−​=1+2k​​mode vector(1,1)(1,−1)​​​
(11)
These are normal modes of two spring-coupled equal pendulums. The corresponding normal mode coordinates u=θ1​+θ2​ and v=θ1​−θ2​ obey u¨+u=0 and v¨+(1+2k)v=0.
For the in-phase normal mode, the bobs undergo identical displacements, leaving their separation exactly r. The spring stays at its natural length and exerts no force, so this angular frequency is independent of k. The value 1 is the individual simple pendulum angular frequency g/l​ in the chosen units.

Write w=1+2k​. The initial conditions on the normal mode coordinates are u(0)=v(0)=0 and u˙(0)=v˙(0)=k. Solving the two harmonic oscillator equations and recovering the original angles gives the exact solution within the harmonic approximation:
θ1​(t)=2k​(sint+wsinwt​),θ2​(t)=2k​(sint−wsinwt​).​
(12)
Their angular velocities are θ˙1​=k(cost+coswt)/2 and θ˙2​=k(cost−coswt)/2, which verify all four initial conditions.
To expose the beat, define Δ=w−1 and Ω=(w+1)/2. Replacing 1/w by 1 changes each angle by O(k2) uniformly in time. The sum-to-product identities then give
θ1​θ2​​=ksin(Ωt)cos(Δt/2)+O(k2),=−kcos(Ωt)sin(Δt/2)+O(k2),​θ˙1​θ˙2​​=kcos(Ωt)cos(Δt/2),=ksin(Ωt)sin(Δt/2).​
(13)
The displayed angular velocities are exact in the linear system. The absolute and signed beat envelopes have leading amplitudes k∣cos(Δt/2)∣ and k∣sin(Δt/2)∣. Because Δ=k+O(k2), at τ=π/k we have Δτ/2=π/2+O(k) and Ωτ=τ+π/2+O(k). Hence
θ1​(τ)θ2​(τ)​=O(k2),=ksinτ+O(k2),​θ˙1​(τ)θ˙2​(τ)​=O(k2),=kcosτ+O(k2).​​
(14)
The first simple pendulum has negligible oscillation amplitude. Its kinetic energy is T1​(τ)=O(k4), while the second has T2​(τ)=k2cos2τ/2+O(k3). Thus, to the retained order, all instantaneous kinetic energy belongs to the second simple pendulum. The fast phase can make that kinetic energy small: the whole initial energy k2/2 is transferred as the second simple pendulum's combined kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy, since
21​(θ˙2​(τ)2+θ2​(τ)2)=2k2​+O(k3),Vs​(τ)=O(k3).
(15)
This is energy transfer after an impulse in two weakly coupled oscillators. It does not require cosτ=1.
At 2τ, Δ(2τ)/2=π+O(k), so the beat has returned the oscillation to the first simple pendulum:
θ1​(2τ)θ2​(2τ)​=ksin(2τ)+O(k2),=O(k2),​θ˙1​(2τ)θ˙2​(2τ)​=kcos(2τ)+O(k2),=O(k2).​​
(16)
The energy localization returns, although the fast oscillation phase need not equal its initial phase. The exact first minimum of the leading beat envelope is at π/Δ, whose leading value is π/k. Keeping the slow phase Δt is essential: expanding sin(wt) at fixed t and then using t=O(1/k) would lose the energy exchange. Although angles are retained only to order k, their leading energies are of order k2.

Take the origin at the centre of mass, so ∫B​ρrd3r=0, and write ri​=r⋅ei​ in the specified orthonormal basis. The inertia tensor is
Iij​=∫B​ρ(r)(∣r∣2δij​−ri​rj​)d3r.​
(17)
Here δij​ is the Kronecker delta. For a rigid body with angular velocity ω, this tensor gives L=Iω and rotational kinetic energy T=ω⋅Iω/2.
The inertia tensor is a real symmetric matrix, so it admits an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. The lines in these eigenvector directions through the centre of mass are its principal axes; the corresponding eigenvalues I1​,I2​,I3​ are its principal moments of inertia:
Iei​=Ii​ei​,I=diag(I1​,I2​,I3​)in principal axes.​
(18)
If an eigenvalue repeats, the corresponding principal axes need not be unique.

Put r=(x,y,z) with e3​ along z, and interpret the symmetry as axisymmetry of the whole mass density. A half-turn about e3​ sends (x,y,z) to (−x,−y,z) and leaves the integration domain and mass density invariant. Therefore ∫ρxzd3r=∫ρyzd3r=0. A quarter-turn sends (x,y,z) to (−y,x,z); it gives
∫ρxyd3r=−∫ρxyd3r=0,∫ρx2d3r=∫ρy2d3r.
(19)
All off-diagonal entries of the inertia tensor consequently vanish. Writing s2=x2+y2, the principal moments of inertia satisfy
I1​=I2​=∫B​ρ(2s2​+z2)d3r,I3​=∫B​ρs2d3r.​
(20)
Thus every orthonormal basis with third vector along the symmetry axis is a principal body frame. This is axisymmetry of the inertia tensor. In general there is no requirement that I3​=I1​.

(dtdA​)S​=(dtdA​)S​+ω×A.
(21)
Apply this to angular momentum L=∑i​Ii​ωi​ei​. The principal moments of inertia are constant in the principal body frame, so external torque balance becomes
τ=Iω˙+ω×(Iω).
(22)

z(t)=ω1​(t)+iω2​(t),ν=J(I3​−J)c​.

z˙=iνz+Jτ0​​(1+i).​

ω(t)=(bτ0​​[cos(I1​bt​)−1],bτ0​​[sin(I1​bt​)+1],I3​−I1​b​).​

Now take zero torque and ω(0)=(1,0,1). The axial angular velocity is ω3​=1 and the complex transverse component obeys z˙=iνz with z(0)=1 and ν=(I3​−I1​)/I1​. Hence the angular momentum components in the principal body frame are
LS​(t)=(I1​cosνt, I1​sinνt, I3​),ν=I1​I3​−I1​​.​

The transverse projection therefore traces a circle with signed angular frequency ν. Its magnitude is constant, L=I12​+I32​​.
In the inertial frame, zero torque means that L is fixed. Using
L=I1​ω+(I3​−I1​)ω3​e3​
(31)
and the derivative of a body-fixed basis vector, the symmetry axis satisfies
e˙3​=ω×e3​=I1​L​×e3​.
(32)
Thus there is uniform torque-free precession of a symmetric top about the fixed angular momentum, at a constant cone angle β and space angular frequency
cosβ=I12​+I32​​I3​​,Ωspace​=I1​I12​+I32​​​.​
(33)
The cone angle stays constant, with no nutation. The body-frame and space-frame frequencies of free symmetric-top precession differ: ν describes rotating components, whereas Ωspace​ describes the symmetry axis in space. The formulas also cover I3​=I1​, where the angular momentum components in the principal body frame are constant.

Define the canonical momenta by
pi​=∂q˙​i​∂L​(q,q˙​,t),i=1,…,n.​
(34)
To obtain an ordinary unconstrained Hamiltonian, assume a regular Lagrangian: the velocity Hessian matrix (∂2L/∂q˙​i​∂q˙​j​) is invertible. The inverse function theorem then lets us solve locally for q˙​=v(q,p,t). The phase space is the cotangent bundle T∗Q, with local canonical coordinates (q,p) and dimension 2n. The Legendre transform in mechanics defines
H(q,p,t)=i=1∑n​pi​vi​(q,p,t)−L(q,v(q,p,t),t).​
(35)
The velocities in this formula have been eliminated in favour of phase space variables. A hyperregular Lagrangian makes this construction global; regularity alone guarantees it locally. Hamilton's equations are
q˙​i​=∂pi​∂H​,p˙​i​=−∂qi​∂H​,i=1,…,n.​
(36)

In canonical coordinates on phase space, the Poisson bracket is
{f,g}=i=1∑n​(∂qi​∂f​∂pi​∂g​−∂pi​∂f​∂qi​∂g​).​
(37)
It treats t as a parameter. Along a solution of Hamilton's equations, the chain rule gives
dtdf​=∑i​(fqi​​q˙​i​+fpi​​p˙​i​)+ft​=∑i​(fqi​​Hpi​​−fpi​​Hqi​​)+ft​.
(38)
Therefore the Hamiltonian conservation law is
dtdf​={f,H}+∂t∂f​.​
(39)
The total derivative follows the trajectory; the explicit partial derivative holds (q,p) fixed.

The Poisson bracket is antisymmetric, so {H,H}=0. The Hamiltonian energy balance consequently gives
dtdH​=∂t∂H​=0.​
(40)
For a regular Lagrangian the Legendre transform in mechanics gives Ht​=−Lt​, evaluated at the corresponding velocity. In particular, a Lagrangian with no explicit time dependence has time-translation symmetry. Noether theorem associates this continuous symmetry of the action with the conserved quantity ∑i​q˙​i​∂L/∂q˙​i​−L=H. For an ordinary natural mechanical Lagrangian L=T−V, with T quadratic in the velocities, this quantity is the total energy T+V.

Set D=∂t​+{⋅,H}. The hypotheses mean Df=Dg=0 as identities on phase space. By the Jacobi identity for the Poisson bracket,
{{f,g},H}={{f,H},g}+{f,{g,H}}.
(41)
Also, the product rule applied to the defining coordinate formula gives
∂t​{f,g}={ft​,g}+{f,gt​}.
(42)
Adding these identities proves
D{f,g}={Df,g}+{f,Dg}.
(43)
Consequently
dtd​{f,g}=0.​
(44)
This proves the Poisson bracket of time-dependent conserved quantities theorem, including explicit time dependence of f and g.

Since H is a conserved quantity, the Hamiltonian energy balance gives Ht​=0. Since f is a conserved quantity, ft​+{f,H}=0. Take an explicit partial derivative with respect to time, holding the phase space coordinates fixed:
ftt​+{ft​,H}+{f,Ht​}=0.
(45)
The last Poisson bracket vanishes because Ht​=0. Thus
dtd​(∂t∂f​)=ftt​+{ft​,H}=0.​
(46)
This is the time derivative of a conserved quantity for an autonomous Hamiltonian. The conservation hypotheses are understood to hold for every trajectory, as identities on phase space.

Work on I>0, with a local choice of the periodic angle θ. The inverse formulas are
I=21​(αq2+αp2​),θ=atan2(αq,p)(mod2π).
(48)
In particular, θq​=p/(2I), θp​=−q/(2I), Iq​=αq and Ip​=p/α. Their Poisson bracket is
{θ,I}=2Iαp2​+2Iαq2​=1.​
(49)
The other fundamental Poisson brackets vanish by antisymmetry. Thus this is a canonical transformation for every α>0, on local charts away from the origin. At I=0 the angle is undefined.
In the new coordinates the harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian is
H0​(θ,I)=I(mα​cos2θ+αmω2​sin2θ).
(50)
For I to be a conserved quantity throughout phase space, Hamilton's equations require I˙=−∂θ​H0​=0 identically. The two coefficients must coincide. For the usual m>0 and ω>0, positivity of α selects
α=mω,H0​=ωI,I(t)=I(0),θ(t)=θ(0)+ωt(mod2π).​
(51)
These are harmonic oscillator action-angle variables; the action variable is I=H0​/ω.

A type-two generating function for a canonical transformation uses the mixed variables (q,P) and defines
p=∂q∂F2​​,Q=∂P∂F2​​.​
(52)
For a local invertible canonical transformation, require F2,qP​=0 so that p=F2,q​(q,P) can be solved for P. The differential identity dF2​=pdq+QdP gives d(F2​−PQ)=pdq−PdQ, expressing preservation of the canonical symplectic form.

On the interior of the principal inverse-cosine branch, 0<q<π, the given canonical transformation has
Q=cosqcosP,1−Q2sec2P​=sinq,p=−sinqsinP.
(53)
Integrating Q=∂P​F2​ suggests F2​=cosqsinP+C(q). Its other derivative is ∂q​F2​=−sinqsinP+C′(q); comparison with p requires C′(q)=0. Hence a suitable type-two generating function for a canonical transformation is
F2​(q,P)=cosqsinP.​
(54)
Both derivative equations reproduce the specified transformation exactly. Its mixed derivative is F2,qP​=−sinqcosP, so it defines a local canonical transformation where 0<q<π and cosP=0, equivalently where ∣Q/cosP∣<1. At q=0,π or cosP=0, the stated formulas fail to give a regular coordinate chart. These restrictions explain the square-root branch and the local meaning of canonicity. This is a trigonometric type-two canonical transformation.

For a time-dependent type-two generating function for a canonical transformation, the differential identity
pdq−Hdt=PdQ−Kdt+d(F2​−PQ)

gives K=H+∂t​F2​, with the partial derivative taken at fixed (q,P). Here F2​=cosqsinP has no explicit time dependence, so the Hamiltonian correction is
