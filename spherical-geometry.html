Spherical geometry

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 9
Spherical geometry studies lengths and angles measured along the surface of a sphere, rather than through its interior. It is a useful model whenever travel is constrained to a round surface. Its straightest paths are great circle arcs: they are geodesics, even though they appear curved in the surrounding Euclidean space. Two distinct great circles meet at an antipodal pair, so the parallel-line picture of Euclidean geometry does not apply.
For a sphere of radius R>0, write two positions as Ru,Rv, with u,v unit vectors. Their shorter surface route has great-circle distance
d=Rarccos(u⋅v),
(2)
where the dot product gives the central angle in radians. The straight chord through the surrounding Euclidean space instead has length 2Rsin(d/(2R)). For a quarter-circle separation these are πR/2≈1.571R and 2​R≈1.414R: specifying which distance we mean has a measurable effect. On the unit sphere, R=1 and great-circle distance equals the central angle.
Consider the spherical triangle with vertices (R,0,0), (0,R,0) and (0,0,R), joined by the shorter great circle arcs. Each side has length πR/2. At each vertex the two outgoing tangent vectors point along the other coordinate axes and are orthogonal, so all three interior angles are π/2. This is the positive coordinate octant of the sphere, not a flat triangle drawn inside it.
For a convex minor spherical triangle with interior angles α,β,γ in radians, the spherical excess formula states
A=R2(α+β+γ−π).
(3)
Our octant therefore has area R2(3π/2−π)=πR2/2. Independently, eight congruent octants partition the sphere, giving 4πR2/8 again. Thus angles alone determine area when R is fixed, unlike Euclidean geometry, where similar triangles can have arbitrarily different areas. The Gauss-Bonnet theorem explains the connection: the sphere's constant Gaussian curvature 1/R2 times the area equals this excess. For spherical triangles whose area is small compared with R2, the excess is small, recovering the familiar flat picture.
