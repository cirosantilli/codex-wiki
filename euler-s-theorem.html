Euler's theorem (aφ(n)≡1(modn))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 753 Articles: 3
For an integer modulus n≥2 and an integer a with greatest common divisor gcd(a,n)=1, Euler's theorem, also called the Fermat-Euler theorem, states
aφ(n)≡1(modn).
(151)
Here Euler's totient function φ(n) counts the units in the unit group U(n)=(Z/nZ)×. These congruence classes have modular multiplicative inverses, equivalently their representatives are coprime to n. The guaranteed return to one permits exponent reduction for units.[ref]
For a prime number p, φ(p)=p−1, recovering Fermat's little theorem in the form ap−1≡1(modp) when p∤a. Multiplying by a and separately handling p∣a gives ap≡a(modp) for every integer a. For a composite number used as the integer modulus, a nonzero congruence class can be a nonunit.
Modulo twelve, the units have representatives 1,5,7,11, so Euler's totient function gives φ(12)=4. Multiplication by five produces
1⟼5⟼1,7⟼11⟼7(mod12).
(152)
Thus this permutation has two permutation cycles, each of length two. In particular 52≡1(mod12), and the guaranteed return 54≡1 follows. The number of units need not be the length of one permutation cycle. Modulo fifteen, multiplication by seven similarly gives the two permutation cycles (1,7,4,13) and (2,14,8,11), covering all eight units. The powers of seven follow the permutation cycle containing one; the other permutation cycle shows that these powers do not visit every unit.
There is a stronger obstruction for a nonunit than just an occasional failed calculation. If a positive integer d>1 divides both a and n, then ak≡0(modd) for every k≥1, so ak cannot be congruent to one modulo n. For example, gcd(3,15)=3 and 3φ(15)=38≡6(mod15), rather than one. Such a base has no multiplicative order modulo that integer modulus.[ref]
