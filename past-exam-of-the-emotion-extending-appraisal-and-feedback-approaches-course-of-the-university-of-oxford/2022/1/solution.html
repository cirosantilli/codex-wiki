Autonomic recordings can support probabilistic discrimination of emotional states under specified conditions; they do not establish a person's experienced emotion with certainty. The distinction matters because a physiological response, an experimenter's induction label and a subjective feeling are different things. A useful psychophysiology study must justify how its labels relate to experience before assessing how well a bodily measure predicts them.
The autonomic nervous system regulates organs and glands through interacting pathways, including the sympathetic nervous system and parasympathetic nervous system. An electrocardiogram supplies cardiac timing and hence heart rate; heart rate variability describes variation in successive intervals. Electrodermal activity can be recorded with skin electrodes, while a temperature sensor supplies peripheral temperature. Breathing and blood-pressure measurements add other information. These measurements differ in what generates them and in their temporal response. In particular, sweat-related skin conductance provides information about sympathetic sudomotor activity, whereas a faster heart rate can reflect several autonomic influences and physical demands. Facial electromyography is useful complementary evidence, but records skeletal muscle activity through the somatic nervous system; it should not be counted as another autonomic channel.
Historically, the question was not simply whether the body changes during emotion, but whether bodily patterns distinguish categories. Paul Ekman and colleagues in 1983 used muscle-by-muscle facial instructions and reliving of past episodes. They reported autonomic differentiation among some negative states as well as between positive and negative states. This challenges the claim that autonomic activity is entirely undifferentiated. It does not show that the same pattern is a necessary and sufficient marker in every spontaneous episode: posing, recollection and the selection of categories constrain the result.[ref]
Joint patterns can carry information that a single measure misses. Philip A. Kragel and colleagues combined electrodermal, cardiac, respiratory and gastric measures during film- and music-induced states. Their 2013 study reported 58.0% autonomic classification accuracy across six induced categories and a neutral condition, with training and testing on separate participant subsets, above its chance benchmark. This supports multivariate information about the tested states. It does not establish invariant bodily essences: a classifier can exploit overlapping distributions whose average patterns differ.[ref]
Conversely, Erika H. Siegel and colleagues' 2018 synthesis found substantial within-category variation, and mean autonomic patterns that did not clearly separate the categories. Its laboratory adult samples do not exhaust every possible real-world measurement. Nevertheless, a universal fingerprint claim should generalize across episodes, and the observed variation is a substantial problem for that strong claim. Failure to establish a fingerprint does not imply that physiology contains zero predictive information; distributions can overlap without being identical.[ref]
Several alternative explanations must therefore be tested. A film may make participants breathe differently because of its pacing; imagined confrontation may prepare action; an emotional task may differ from a neutral task in movement or attention. Exercise, room temperature, medication and individual baselines can change the recorded variables without a change in discrete emotional category. A classifier that recognizes the stimulus or task is not necessarily recognizing the feeling. Splitting trials while retaining the same people and clips in training and test sets can also overstate generalization. Appropriate cross-validation should hold out the people or inductions required by the intended claim, with preprocessing and feature selection fitted only to training data. Mixed feelings and changing episodes should be allowed rather than forced into one mutually exclusive label.
Even genuine conditional discrimination does not determine the reliability of a particular classification. Let E denote one independently defined target state and + a positive classifier result. Partitioning the positive results into target and nontarget cases and applying Bayes' theorem gives

For an explicitly hypothetical classifier with true-positive probability 0.8 and false-positive probability 0.2, a target base rate of 0.1 produces 0.08/(0.08+0.18)≈0.308. At a base rate of 0.5, the same conditional performance produces 0.8. The positive predictive value therefore depends on the setting as well as the instrument. This calculation illustrates a limit on inference; it is not an estimate from either study.
The strongest conclusion combines measures while respecting their different roles. Repeated self-report helps establish experienced feeling, independently coded behavior establishes action, and autonomic recording establishes bodily regulation. None is error-free, and agreement must be demonstrated. Psychophysiologists can estimate how much an autonomic pattern changes the probability of a reported or independently characterized state, with an explicit uncertainty and domain of validation. They can establish useful evidence about experienced emotion, but autonomic activity alone supplies a conditional inference rather than a uniquely identifying emotional signature.
