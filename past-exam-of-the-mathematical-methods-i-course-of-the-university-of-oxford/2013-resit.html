Put z=x+iy. The expression whose complex argument is constrained becomes
z2+1=(x2−y2+1)+2ixy.
(4)
Its principal argument is π/2 exactly when its real part is zero and its imaginary part is strictly positive. Hence
y2−x2=1,xy>0.​
(5)
The locus consists of the upper-right half of the upper hyperbola branch, y=x2+1​ with x>0, and the lower-left half of the lower branch, y=−x2+1​ with x<0. The vertices z=±i are excluded because z2+1=0 there and zero has no complex argument. The remaining halves have negative imaginary part and give argument −π/2, not π/2. A real square orthogonal matrix satisfies QTQ=I. A complex square unitary matrix satisfies U†U=I, where † denotes conjugate transpose. A Hermitian matrix satisfies H†=H. The distinction between transpose and conjugate transpose matters for imaginary entries.
For the three displayed matrices, in their source order:
  • First: a unitary matrix, but neither an orthogonal matrix nor a Hermitian matrix.
  • Second: an orthogonal matrix, a unitary matrix, and a Hermitian matrix.
  • Third: a Hermitian matrix, but neither an orthogonal matrix nor a unitary matrix.
For the first matrix, A†=−A and A2=−I, so A†A=I but A†=A. It also fails ATA=I, even if complex orthogonality is considered. The second matrix is real diagonal with entries 1,−1: its transpose, conjugate transpose and inverse all equal itself. In the third matrix the imaginary off-diagonal entries are conjugate pairs, establishing the Hermitian matrix condition. Its first column has squared norm 1+3=4, so it is not a unitary matrix; it is not real orthogonal, and the complex bilinear first-column square is 1−3=−2=1 as well.

Let D=a⋅(b×c)=0 be the scalar triple product. The reciprocal basis satisfies a⋅a′=1 and all mismatched dot products vanish, with cyclic analogues. Apply the given vector triple product identity to obtain
b′×c′=D2(c×a)×(a×b)​=D2Da​=Da​.
(13)
Dotting with a′ gives
a′⋅(b′×c′)=D1​.​
(14)
The sign is retained: a negatively oriented original basis has a negatively oriented reciprocal basis. Equivalently, if the original vectors are columns of B, their reciprocals are columns of B−T, whose determinant is 1/detB.

Dot the reciprocal-basis expansion with each member of the original basis. All mismatched dot products vanish and the matching one equals one. Consequently
α=r⋅a,β=r⋅b,γ=r⋅c.​
(16)
These are coefficients in the reciprocal basis. They would instead involve dot products with the reciprocal vectors if r were being expanded in the original basis. The two coefficient conventions should not be interchanged for a nonorthonormal basis.

r=sa′+tb′+uc′.​

sin5θ=5c4s−10c2s3+s5=5s(1−s2)2−10s3(1−s2)+s5=16s5−20s3+5s.
