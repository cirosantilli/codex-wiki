The displayed orthogonal matrix transforms the coordinates of a fixed vector, rather than actively moving that vector. Write θ=arctan(1/2). Then P=R(−θ), so the new unit basis vectors, expressed in the old orthonormal basis, are the columns of PT:
e1′​=5​(2,1)​,e2′​=5​(−1,2)​.​
(8)

Reconstruction gives (2e1′​−e2′​)/5​=(1,0), confirming that the point is unchanged. The middle panel of the figure in part 1 shows these axes and the signed projections. Since PTP=I and detP=1, lengths and orientation of the basis are preserved.

dXdY​=2X−Y4X+2Y​.

The matrix exponential power series is absolutely convergent for a finite matrix, so taking the conjugate transpose term by term is valid. Since M†=M,
U†=(∑n=0∞​n!inMn​)†=∑n=0∞​n!(−i)nMn​=e−iM.

An integrating factor directly solves a linear first-order equation; a general second-order equation needs an additional reduction. There are several sufficient cases, with all divisions restricted to intervals where their denominators are nonzero.
If A=0 and B=0, divide by B to obtain y′+(C/B)y=D/B. The integrating factor is μ=exp(∫C/Bdx) and
y=μ−1(K+∫μD/Bdx).
(28)
If C=0 and A=0, put u=y′. Then u′+(B/A)u=D/A is first order, with integrating factor ν=exp(∫B/Adx); solve for u and integrate once more for y.
A further reducible class has C=B′−A′′, for then
Ay′′+By′+Cy=dxd​[Ay′+(B−A′)y].
(29)
One integration produces a first-order equation for y, whose integrating factor after division by A is exp(∫(B−A′)/Adx).
Multiplication can always put a regular second-order equation into self-adjoint form by choosing w=exp(∫(B−A′)/Adx), since
w(Ay′′+By′+Cy)=(wAy′)′+wCy.
(30)
However, the remaining wCy term generally prevents one integration from solving it. The limitations of integrating factors for second-order equations explain why this formal transformation alone is not a general solution method. These sufficient classes do not purport to classify every specially solvable coefficient choice.
