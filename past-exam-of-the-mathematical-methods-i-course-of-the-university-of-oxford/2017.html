An integrating factor for u′+P(x)u=Q(x) is a nonzero function μ(x)=exp(∫P(x)dx), chosen so that (μu)′=μQ. Integrating this total derivative reduces the first-order linear ordinary differential equation to one quadrature.
For the given nonlinear equation, let u=y2, so u′=2yy′. Multiplication by 2y gives u′+u=x2+2x. Using the integrating factor ex, or recognizing the particular solution u=x2, yields u=x2+Ce−x. The initial value gives C=1 and selects the positive branch:
y(x)=x2+e−x​.​
(5)
The radicand is strictly positive for every real x. This solution never reaches the singular denominator y=0 of the original equation, so multiplication by y has introduced no forbidden crossing. The negative branch would violate y(0)=1.

Let Hv=λv with v=0 and H∗=H. Then v∗Hv=λv∗v, while taking the complex conjugate gives v∗Hv=λv∗v. Since v∗v>0, every eigenvalue of a Hermitian matrix is real.
If Hv=λv and Hw=μw, then
λv∗w=(Hv)∗w=v∗Hw=μv∗w.
(6)
Distinct eigenvalues therefore force v∗w=0: their eigenvectors are orthogonal in the Hermitian inner product. A degenerate eigenvalue occurs more than once in the spectrum, counted with multiplicity. For a Hermitian matrix, its algebraic multiplicity equals its geometric multiplicity, so degeneracy gives an eigenspace of dimension greater than one; an orthonormal basis can be chosen within it.

With the Einstein summation convention, the magnetic force has components Fi​=qϵijk​vj​Bk​. Thus its matrix acting on velocity is
Mij​=qϵijk​Bk​,M=q​0−Bz​By​​Bz​0−Bx​​−By​Bx​0​​.​
(15)
The Levi-Civita symbol changes sign on exchanging i,j, so MT=−M; the diagonal vanishes and the matrix has zero trace. For nonzero B, MB=qB×B=0, exhibiting a nontrivial kernel and proving noninvertibility. If B=0 or q=0, M is the zero matrix and is again noninvertible.
Equivalently, an odd-dimensional real antisymmetric matrix has detM=detMT=det(−M)=−detM, hence zero determinant. The same antisymmetry gives vTMv=0, expressing that the magnetic force does no work.

A map between vector spaces over the same field is linear when L(av+bw)=aL(v)+bL(w) for every v,w and scalars a,b. Its kernel is kerL={v∈V:L(v)=0}. It contains zero because L(0)=0. If v,w∈kerL, then
L(av+bw)=aL(v)+bL(w)=0.
(27)
Thus it is closed under linear combinations and is a vector subspace of V. No injectivity or equality of the dimensions of V,W is needed.

For two nonparallel vectors v1​,v2​∈R2, a nontrivial relation av1​+bv2​=0 would make one a scalar multiple of the other. They are therefore linearly independent. Two independent vectors in a two-dimensional vector space form a basis.
Likewise, three vectors in R3 that are not contained in one plane through the origin cannot obey a nontrivial linear dependence: such a relation would express one as a combination of the other two, or already make that pair dependent. Thus they are independent and form a basis of R3. The plane condition here is linear coplanarity with the origin, as required for vectors, rather than affine coplanarity of three arbitrary points.
