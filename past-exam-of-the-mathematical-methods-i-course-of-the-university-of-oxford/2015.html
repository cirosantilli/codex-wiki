Use polar coordinates z=reiθ, where r=∣z∣≥0. The equation sets r=θ, giving an Archimedean spiral. The origin is excluded because the complex argument of zero is undefined. With the principal complex argument in (−π,π], the locus is
z(θ)=θcosθ+iθsinθ,0<θ≤π.​
(1)
It approaches the excluded origin along the positive real axis, passes through iπ/2, and ends at −π. The choice of argument matters: the convention [0,2π) instead gives 0<θ<2π, while allowing all values of the complex argument gives every turn with θ>0.

Taking the conjugate transpose reverses the product order: (AB)†=B†A†=BA. Thus
AB is Hermitian if and only if AB=BA.​
(7)

The diagonal entries of a Hermitian matrix satisfy Ajj​=Ajj​​, so each is real. Consequently their sum, the trace, is real. No additional condition is required. The same conclusion follows by summing the real eigenvalues.

iv OurBigBook.com
If Au=λu and Av=μv, the Hermitian matrix identity gives
λ⟨u,v⟩=⟨Au,v⟩=⟨u,Av⟩=μ⟨u,v⟩,
(9)
because both eigenvalues are real. Thus eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal. A simple spectrum guarantees this for an arbitrary choice of one eigenvector per eigenvalue.
Within a degenerate eigenspace, arbitrary independent eigenvectors need not be orthogonal. The Gram-Schmidt process always supplies an orthonormal basis of that eigenspace. Accordingly the existence of an orthonormal eigenbasis requires no additional condition, whereas automatic orthogonality of arbitrary independent eigenvectors is ensured by distinct eigenvalues. Scalar multiples of the same nonzero eigenvector are of course not mutually orthogonal.

iv OurBigBook.com
For an integer c, repeated multiplicativity of the determinant, with the inverse identity for negative powers, gives
det(Ac)=(detA)c(c∈Z).​
(17)
For an arbitrary real c, an invertible real matrix does not by itself define a unique real matrix power. For example, the scalar matrix (−1) has no real square root. If a matrix logarithm L has been chosen with eL=A and Ac is defined as ecL, the matrix exponential determinant identity gives the precise formula
det(Ac)=ectrL.​
(18)
This cannot always be replaced by an independently chosen scalar branch of (detA)c. For instance, A=−I2​ has the complex square root iI2​, whose determinant is −1, while the principal scalar square root of detA=1 is +1. For a positive-definite symmetric matrix with its usual positive spectral power, all eigenvalues are positive and the familiar formula (detA)c holds for every real c.

A negative value of either radius makes the intersection empty. Assume henceforth α,β≥0 and put D=∣d∣. If D=0, the two spheres are concentric: their intersection is the entire sphere when α=β>0, the origin when α=β=0, and empty otherwise.
For D>0, subtract their squared equations to obtain the plane
x⋅d=2D2+α2−β2​.
(24)
The centre of the circle of intersection of two spheres is x0​=sd, where
d=Dd​,s=2DD2+α2−β2​.
(25)
Every point of that plane can be written x=x0​+u with u⋅d=0. Its remaining equation is
∣u∣2=ρ2=α2−s2=4D2[(α+β)2−D2][D2−(α−β)2]​.
(26)
Thus the intersection is a circle of centre x0​ and radius ρ if ∣α−β∣<D<α+β; a single point x0​ at either equality; and empty otherwise. These include tangency to a zero-radius sphere. A negative s places the circle centre beyond the first sphere's centre on the side opposite d.
In Rn for n≥2, the same calculation gives an (n−2)-dimensional sphere in the perpendicular affine hyperplane when ρ2>0, a point when ρ2=0, and the empty set otherwise. In two dimensions the positive-radius case consists of two points. In one dimension the perpendicular hyperplane has only one point, so a nonconcentric intersection exists only when ρ2=0. Concentric equal-radius sets coincide in every dimension.

Solution

11 OurBigBook.com
For a sinusoidal drive of variable positive angular frequency, the average absorbed power is proportional to
(Ω2−ω2)2+4γ2ω2ω2​.
(31)
It peaks at ω=Ω. The absorbed-power bandwidth of a viscously damped oscillator is the separation of its half-power frequencies: solving ∣Ω2−ω2∣=2γω gives
ω±​=Ω2+γ2​±γ,Δω=2γ.​
(32)
The usual quality factor is Q=Ω/(2γ)=Ω/Δω​. Large Q means a narrow response band relative to the resonant frequency. The post-drive motion has amplitude envelope e−γt, connecting a narrow bandwidth to a long ringdown. For weak damping, the cycle-averaged energy decays as e−2γt and Q is also 2π times stored energy divided by the energy lost per cycle. The exact instantaneous energy has an oscillatory factor, so this cycle interpretation uses the weak-damping approximation.

A fixed point obeys (I−M)X=c. Thus the necessary and sufficient existence condition is
c∈range(I−M).​
(41)
If 1 is not an eigenvalue of M, then I−M is an invertible matrix and the unique constant solution is X=(I−M)−1c​. If I−M is singular but the equation is consistent, all constant solutions are X0​+ker(I−M) for any particular solution X0​. For an existing fixed point, xk+1​−X=M(xk​−X); the successive difference in the question likewise obeys δxk+1​=Mδxk​.

For a diagonalizable matrix, write M=Pdiag(λj​)P−1. Relative to an existing fixed point,
xk​−X=Pdiag(λjk−1​)P−1(x1​−X).
(44)
Convergence to the same fixed point for every initial vector therefore holds exactly when
∣λj​∣<1 for all j,equivalently ρ(M)<1.​
(45)
Here ρ(M) is the spectral radius. Necessity follows by choosing an initial displacement in each eigenspace; sufficiency follows because every displayed scalar power tends to zero. The condition also makes I−M an invertible matrix, so that fixed point exists uniquely for every c.
If instead different initial vectors may converge to different constant solutions, an eigenvalue 1 is allowed: all other eigenvalues must have modulus less than one, and the component of c in the eigenspace for 1 must vanish. That component of the initial vector then remains unchanged. No other unit-modulus eigenvalue is compatible with convergence for arbitrary initial vectors.

Because A is a real symmetric matrix, differentiating the quadratic form gives
∇v​a=(A−αI)v.
