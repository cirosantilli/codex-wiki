Let w satisfy sinw=z and put t=eiw=0. The exponential definition of the complex sine gives
2it−t−1​=z,t2−2izt−1=0.
(4)
The quadratic formula therefore gives t=iz±1−z2​. Taking the complex logarithm yields the multivalued complex inverse sine formula
w=−ilog(iz±1−z2​).​
(5)
The two quadratic roots have product −1, so neither is zero and their logarithms exist as multivalued quantities. If w0​ is one local inverse value, the complete set of values is w0​+2πk and π−w0​+2πk, with k∈Z.
Branch qualification: the source's ± formula describes the branches of the complex inverse sine, not two interchangeable expressions for a single chosen inverse branch. At z=0, the plus sign with the usual local choices gives 0, whereas the minus sign with log(−1)=iπ gives π. Thus both signs cannot simultaneously equal the principal inverse at zero. A principal complex inverse sine requires compatible square-root and logarithm branches, normalized to w0​=0 at zero and continued on the chosen cut domain.

Write all vectors in Cartesian components. The first component of the nested cross product is
[a×(b×c)]x​​=ay​(bx​cy​−by​cx​)−az​(bz​cx​−bx​cz​)=bx​(ay​cy​+az​cz​)−cx​(ay​by​+az​bz​)=bx​(a⋅c)−cx​(a⋅b).​

a×(b×c)=(a⋅c)b−(a⋅b)c.​

(8)
Each coefficient cancels because the dot product is symmetric. This is the Jacobi identity for the cross product.

Let Av=λv with v=0 and A†=−A. Taking the conjugate of the scalar v†Av gives
λ∗v†v=v†A†v=−λv†v.
(9)
Since v†v>0, it follows that
λ∗=−λ,Reλ=0.​
(10)

For Au=λu and Av=μv, compute the same mixed scalar in two ways:
λ∗u†v=(Au)†v=u†A†v=−μu†v.

(12)
The relevant orthogonality uses the complex inner product, not the unconjugated bilinear dot product. Repeated eigenvalues do not force every possible pair of eigenvectors within that one eigenspace to be orthogonal.

De Moivre's theorem states that, for real θ and integer n,
(cosθ+isinθ)n=cos(nθ)+isin(nθ).​

For z=x+iy=0, z−1=(x−iy)/(x2+y2). The first constraint is x=x2+y2 and the second is −y=x2+y2. Completing the squares gives
(x−21​)2+y2=41​,x2+(y+21​)2=41​,z=0.​
(39)
These circles have centres (1/2,0) and (0,−1/2) and radius 1/2. Their algebraic intersections satisfy x=−y and 2x2=x, giving zero and (1−i)/2. Zero is excluded from both original loci, so their only actual intersection is
z=21−i​.​
(40)
At (x,y)=(1/2,−1/2) the two circle normals are (0,−1) and (1,0), so the tangent directions are perpendicular. The intersection angle is π/2​. This is also an instance of the orthogonality of reciprocal-coordinate circles under complex inversion.
The first small circle touches the unit circle at z=1, and the second at z=−i. On the first, r2=x≤r; on the second, r2=−y≤r. Thus each lies entirely within r≤1, attaining equality only at its stated point. They are internally tangent to the unit circle and do not cross it. The original sketch marks the excluded common origin with an open symbol and the genuine intersection with a filled symbol.

The cyclic products obtained by direct matrix multiplication are σx​σy​=iσz​, σy​σz​=iσx​, and σz​σx​=iσy​. Reversing any two distinct indices changes the sign; equal-index products are I. Therefore every pair is covered by
{σi​,σj​}=2δij​I,[σi​,σj​]=2ik∑​ϵijk​σk​.​
(42)
Here {A,B} is the anticommutator and [A,B] the commutator. Explicitly the three diagonal anticommutators are 2I, all six off-diagonal ones are zero, and all three diagonal commutators vanish. The cyclic commutators are 2iσz​,2iσx​,2iσy​, respectively, with their reversed partners negative. The convention is ϵxyz​=1.
