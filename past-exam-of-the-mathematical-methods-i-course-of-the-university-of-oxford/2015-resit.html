Let A1​,A2​,A3​ denote the three matrices in their printed order. A Hermitian matrix satisfies A†=A, a unitary matrix satisfies A†A=I, and an orthogonal matrix is real with ATA=I.
For the first matrix, A1†​=A1​ and A12​=I. It is therefore Hermitian and unitary. It is not real, and even allowing complex entries in the transpose test gives A1T​A1​=−I, so it is not orthogonal.
The second matrix is A2​=iI. Thus A2†​A2​=(−iI)(iI)=I, whereas A2†​=−A2​ and A2T​A2​=−I. It is unitary only among the listed classes.
The third matrix is real, but direct multiplication gives
A3T​A3​=diag(1,41​,1).
(6)
It is neither orthogonal nor unitary. Its unequal off-diagonal transpose entries also show A3T​=A3​, so it is none of the three. The common prefactor 1/2 applies to the central diagonal entry as well; replacing that entry by one would describe a different matrix.

If Mv=λv for a nonzero eigenvector, then M2=M implies (λ2−λ)v=0, hence λ∈{0,1}. Conversely, the real-symmetric spectral theorem gives M=QΛQT with orthogonal Q. If every diagonal entry of Λ is zero or one, then Λ2=Λ and therefore M2=M. Thus the necessary and sufficient condition is
Every eigenvalue of M is either 0 or 1.​
(15)
Such a symmetric idempotent matrix is the orthogonal projection onto its eigenvalue-one eigenspace, with its eigenvalue-zero eigenspace as the kernel. Diagonalizability is essential to the sufficiency argument from eigenvalues alone; symmetry supplies it here.

After discarding f0​=0, the preceding inner products show that distinct sine functions are orthogonal; dividing each by π​ makes them orthonormal. More precisely, their infinite expansions form an orthogonal basis of the odd part of L2(−π,π) in the Hilbert-space sense. This is not a claim that every arbitrary odd function is a finite linear combination of sines.
Take the inner product of x=∑n≥1​an​sin(nx) with sin(mx). Orthogonality gives
am​=π1​∫−ππ​xsin(mx)dx=π2​[−mxcos(mx)​+m2sin(mx)​]0π​=m2(−1)m+1​.
(42)
Therefore the Fourier series of a periodic sawtooth, scaled to this interval, is
x=2n=1∑∞​n(−1)n+1​sin(nx),−π<x<π.​
(43)
Its periodic extension is piecewise smooth, so the Dirichlet convergence theorem for Fourier series gives pointwise convergence to x inside the interval. At the jump endpoints of that periodic extension, the series instead converges to zero, the midpoint of the two one-sided limits.

The function x2−1 is an even function, while every sine basis function is an odd function. Their product is odd, so every sine coefficient is zero:
⟨x2−1,sin(nx)⟩=0.
(44)
The function itself is not zero, and therefore it cannot be represented by this sine basis. The basis spans the odd subspace, whereas the requested nonzero even function lies in its orthogonal complement. Equivalently, any pointwise limit of odd partial sums remains odd wherever both reflected points have limits.
