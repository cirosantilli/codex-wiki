Spatial and chemical multisensory processing share problems of binding, uncertainty, learning and common-cause attribution. They often differ in what their inputs represent: spatial cues can estimate the same location, whereas taste and odor frequently supply complementary attributes of a food. The comparison concerns particular computations and tasks, not a rigid separation between two kinds of brain.
In spatial localization, vision, audition and touch can each provide information about position, although they begin in different coordinates. Visual information is initially related to the retina, auditory location depends on binaural and spectral cues, and touch is related to a body surface. Combining them requires accounting for eye, head and body position. Spatial coincidence supports a common event, but is not sufficient: unrelated events can occur nearby, and one moving object can produce cues at different times.

Correlated errors, bias, a nonflat prior or separate causes change this benchmark.
Marc O. Ernst and Martin S. Banks measured visual and haptic estimates of object height and compared combined judgments with predictions based on unisensory variability. Manipulating visual uncertainty and cue conflict supported reliability-dependent weighting and improved combined precision in that task.[ref] The conclusion is conditional optimality for measured inputs and task, not that every multisensory judgment or neuron always computes an optimum.
Development supplies a useful qualification. Monica Gori and colleagues measured visual, haptic and combined judgments of block size and orientation in children. Younger children did not always obtain the adult-like precision gain; the dominant modality differed between size and orientation, and adult-like combination emerged around eight to ten years in those tests.[ref] The authors proposed a role for cross-sensory calibration. The result does not establish one universal maturation age for all multisensory tasks. It shows why adult cue-combination rules should not simply be assigned to every developing observer.
The chemical senses pose a different representational problem. Gustation responds to dissolved chemicals at taste receptors, while olfaction responds to volatile molecules; oral touch, temperature and irritation also contribute to a food percept. Retronasal odor can be attributed to the mouth through olfactory referral, even though its receptors remain in the nose. A sweet taste and a fruit odor are not necessarily two noisy measurements of one physical sweetness coordinate. They can jointly identify a food or predict nutritional and hedonic properties. Adding them with inverse-variance weights is therefore not automatically meaningful.
Nevertheless, the same inferential questions arise: do signals belong to the same food, how should prior experience affect interpretation, and which component is responsible for a judged quality? Richard J. Stevenson and colleagues' odor–taste conditioning results show that pairing an odor with sweet or sour tastes can alter later odor judgments.[ref] Learned co-occurrence can thus influence flavor qualities. Verbal confusion or a rating change alone does not establish full sensory fusion; separate taste, odor and pleasantness scales help test response dumping.
Neural convergence is also shared without implying identical architectures. Ivan E. T. de Araujo and colleagues delivered tastes and retronasal odors separately and together during human functional magnetic resonance imaging. They identified overlapping responses in several regions and interactions in orbitofrontal cortex; some responses also covaried with consonance or pleasantness ratings.[ref] A mixture interaction is stronger evidence than overlap alone, but an imaging voxel does not prove that every responding neuron receives both inputs. A neural correlation with pleasantness also differs from a causal explanation of food preference.
Temporal and spatial structure further distinguish typical tasks. Spatial audiovisual cues can be brief, externally localized events; food perception unfolds with sniffing, chewing, dissolution and swallowing. Taste and retronasal odor may be bound despite unlike onset profiles because their sequence is expected within eating. Chemical signals can adapt and interact at peripheral as well as central stages, while vision and audition face their own receptor and pathway nonlinearities. Neither class is exclusively early or late, automatic or attention-dependent. Attention, task instructions, hunger and learned expectations can change what is selected or reported; these influences must be measured rather than inferred from a unitary percept alone.
A decisive comparison therefore uses matched inferential questions: estimate a common property, identify a common object, or judge whether two signals share a cause. Measure unisensory performance, test cue conflict and temporal or semantic congruence, and distinguish changes in quality, uncertainty and response. Both domains integrate evidence about events; spatial averaging is a useful special case, while complementary chemical information often requires object, quality and value representations beyond a shared coordinate.
