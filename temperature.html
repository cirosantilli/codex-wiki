Temperature

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 9
Temperature labels thermal equilibrium: two internally equilibrated bodies have the same temperature when contact permitting only heat exchange produces no net heat transfer. The zeroth law of thermodynamics makes the comparison transitive. If bodies A and B separately reach thermal equilibrium with the same thermometer without appreciably disturbing either body, they agree with each other. This is what lets a gas thermometer compare a metal block with water, despite their different internal energies and heat capacities. Insulation alone makes no such comparison: it can prevent heat transfer between bodies at different temperatures. [ref]
The zeroth law of thermodynamics establishes equality comparisons, but does not choose a numerical scale. Thermodynamic temperature supplies an absolute scale consistent with Carnot's theorem and entropy. For a simple compressible system at thermodynamic equilibrium with fixed particle number N and composition,
T=(∂S∂U​)V,N​,T1​=(∂U∂S​)V,N​.
(75)
Here U is internal energy, S is entropy, and V is volume; the second equality assumes the derivative can be inverted. Temperature is a thermodynamic conjugate variable to entropy and belongs to the intensive and extensive thermodynamic quantities as an intensive quantity. Its partial derivative tells us how much internal energy must change per unit change of entropy under the stated constraints. More general systems require their other mechanical and material variables to be held fixed too. [ref]
For an infinitesimal quantity δQ>0 of heat transferred from A to B, with no work or matter exchange, the equilibrium entropy changes give
dStotal​=δQ(TB​1​−TA​1​).
(76)
For ordinary positive temperatures, spontaneous heat transfer from TA​>TB​ increases total entropy; thermal equilibrium has TA​=TB​. This comparison uses changes in entropy, rather than the total internal energy contained in either body. A small hot object can have less internal energy than a large cool one. See entropy maximization under thermal contact for the equilibrium criterion and negative temperature for the special case of bounded energy spectra.
Express absolute thermodynamic temperature T in kelvin, and a Celsius reading tC​ in degree Celsius:
∘CtC​​=KT​−273.15,∘CΔtC​​=KΔT​.
(77)
The offset is exact: 300K corresponds to 26.85∘C, and 400K to 126.85∘C. Their absolute temperature ratio is 4/3; dividing their Celsius readings gives a different number. Thus gas thermometer pressure ratios and Carnot's theorem require thermodynamic temperature. The modern SI definition of the kelvin fixes the Boltzmann constant kB​=1.380649×10−23JK−1; a reference bath realizes or transfers the scale rather than defining that constant.[ref]
Temperature is not universally the mean kinetic energy of a particle. In a classical, nonrelativistic ideal gas at thermal equilibrium, the equipartition theorem gives the mean translational kinetic energy relative to the gas's bulk motion:
⟨21​m∣v−u∣2⟩=23​kB​T.
(78)
Here m is particle mass, v its velocity, and u the mean flow velocity. In this regime, different particle masses at the same temperature have equal mean translational kinetic energies but different characteristic speeds. A moving gas can also carry bulk kinetic energy without being hotter in its rest frame. Internal molecular modes and quantum statistics require further care; the equipartition theorem has classical assumptions, whereas the equilibrium entropy definition applies more widely. [ref]
