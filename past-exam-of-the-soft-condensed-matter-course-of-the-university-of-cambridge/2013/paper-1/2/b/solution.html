DLVO theory adds attractive Van der Waals forces and electric double layer repulsion to obtain an effective colloid pair potential. For two identical spheres of radius b at a surface gap h≪b, a useful schematic form is
U(h)≃−12hAH​b​+Be−κD​h,B>0.
(11)
Here AH​ is the Hamaker constant. For a symmetric monovalent electrolyte with number density n of each ionic species, κD2​=2ne2/(ϵkB​T). The exponential is a simplified screened repulsion; its amplitude depends on electric potential at the surface, charge regulation and geometry. Constant-charge and constant-potential boundary conditions need not produce the same close-approach law.
A primary attractive minimum near contact may be separated from dispersed configurations by an energy barrier; a shallower secondary minimum can produce loose reversible aggregation. A barrier much larger than kB​T can give long-lived kinetic stabilization without making the dispersed state the global free-energy minimum. Adding salt shortens the Debye–Hückel screening length and can lower the barrier, allowing colloidal coagulation. Changing pH can alter surface ionization, so screening alone does not determine stability.
One can vary salt concentration while holding particle concentration, temperature and pH fixed, then follow particle aggregation using light scattering or microscopy. A solvent-only control and an independently measured surface-charge indicator help distinguish a change of screening from adsorption or charge reversal. Classical DLVO theory omits steric stabilization, hydration forces and strong ionic correlations; the formal attraction diverging at contact is cut off by molecular-scale physics.
