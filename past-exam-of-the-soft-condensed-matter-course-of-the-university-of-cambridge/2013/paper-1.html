The tension is supplied by Stokes drag on the free bead. With bead radius b=0.5μm, the stipulated isolated-sphere approximation gives
f=6πηbU=2.83pN.​
(1)
The trap also balances drag on the trapped bead, but that bead's drag is not an additional force transmitted through the chain. The polymer contour length is L=Na=6μm. Use a room-temperature estimate T≃300K, consistent with the water-viscosity approximation imported from part (a); the expressions below retain the dependence on T.
For a theta solvent, Gaussian chain elasticity gives Fel​=3kB​TR2/(2Na2) and hence
Rθ​=3kB​TfNa2​=0.819(T300K​)μm.​
(2)
This is the mean extension along the force, rather than the unforced end-to-end distance of a polymer chain aN​=60nm. At 300K, fa/(kB​T)=0.410; the finite-link freely jointed chain result R=L[coth(fa/kB​T)−kB​T/(fa)] is 0.810μm, close to the Gaussian estimate.
The good-solvent force-extension scaling changes the elasticity. In the tensile blob regime, a blob has size ξt​=kB​T/f, contains g∼(ξt​/a)5/3 segments, and the aligned string of N/g blobs has length
Rgood​∼gN​ξt​=Na(kB​Tfa​)2/3≃3.31(T300K​)2/3μm.​
(3)
Only the scaling and order of magnitude of the good-solvent value are determined: solvent-dependent swelling amplitudes are not supplied. The nonlinear force law is the essential correction to extrapolating a weak-force coil spring.[ref]
To expose the unrealistic extrapolation, the unforced good-solvent size estimate is R0​∼aN3/5=151nm. Treating this coil as a linear spring with spring constant 3kB​T/R02​ predicts R≃5.17μm at 300K, although its required condition fR0​≪kB​T fails by a factor of about 103. Dropping the order-one factor three, as in the rough estimate k∼kB​T/R02​, even gives R∼15.5μm>L. An extension beyond the polymer contour length is an artefact of extending the coil's linear response outside its range. The tensile blob estimate avoids that violation but here ξt​≃1.46nm and g∼4.4, so it is only marginally within a many-segment-blob regime. finite extensibility of a polymer chain, chain drag and flow disturbance by both large beads further limit quantitative realism. The specified neglect of chain drag is a model assumption, not evidence that it is physically negligible.

For a symmetric Flory-Huggins polymer blend, the mixing free energy per lattice site is
kB​Tf​=Nϕlnϕ+(1−ϕ)ln(1−ϕ)​+χϕ(1−ϕ).
(4)
The critical conditions f′′=0 and d3f/dϕ3=0 give ϕc​=1/2 and χc​=2/N=0.002. Therefore
Tc​=0.032+0.00248.2​=1.418×103K.​
(5)
Since dχ/dT<0, heating improves miscibility: this is an upper critical solution temperature in the stipulated model. The very high value is an extrapolation of the supplied interaction-parameter fit, not evidence that an intact experimental blend can be brought reversibly to this temperature; chemical stability and the fitted temperature range must be checked before interpreting it as an accessible transition.

A viscoelastic material stores part of the deformation energy and dissipates part of it. In linear viscoelasticity, a small imposed shear strain produces a stress determined by its history, τ(t)=∫−∞t​G(t−t′)γ˙​(t′)dt′. A shear step probes stress relaxation; a stress step probes creep compliance. A purely elastic solid has persistent stress after a held strain, while a Newtonian fluid relaxes that stress and creeps continuously under constant stress.
For a Maxwell model, a spring of shear modulus G0​ and a dashpot of viscosity η are in series:
γ˙​=G0​τ˙​+ητ​,G(t)=G0​e−t/τM​,τM​=η/G0​.
(9)
For the convention γ∝eiωt,
G′=G0​1+(ωτM​)2(ωτM​)2​,G′′=G0​1+(ωτM​)2ωτM​​.​
(10)
The storage modulus dominates at short times or high frequencies; the loss modulus dominates at long times or low frequencies. The energy dissipated per cycle per volume is πG′′γ02​. A Kelvin-Voigt model places spring and dashpot in parallel and instead gives bounded creep J(t)=(1−e−G0​t/η)/G0​: a simple illustration of a viscoelastic solid. Real materials can require a spectrum of relaxation times rather than one element.
A small-amplitude oscillatory rheometer measures stress amplitude and phase relative to imposed strain, giving G′=∣τ0​/γ0​∣cosδ and G′′=∣τ0​/γ0​∣sinδ. First sweep strain amplitude to identify the linear range; control temperature, sample ageing, wall slip and instrument inertia. Frequency-dependent phase lag alone is insufficient if these apparatus effects are unresolved.

DLVO theory adds attractive Van der Waals forces and electric double layer repulsion to obtain an effective colloid pair potential. For two identical spheres of radius b at a surface gap h≪b, a useful schematic form is
U(h)≃−12hAH​b​+Be−κD​h,B>0.
(11)
Here AH​ is the Hamaker constant. For a symmetric monovalent electrolyte with number density n of each ionic species, κD2​=2ne2/(ϵkB​T). The exponential is a simplified screened repulsion; its amplitude depends on electric potential at the surface, charge regulation and geometry. Constant-charge and constant-potential boundary conditions need not produce the same close-approach law.
A primary attractive minimum near contact may be separated from dispersed configurations by an energy barrier; a shallower secondary minimum can produce loose reversible aggregation. A barrier much larger than kB​T can give long-lived kinetic stabilization without making the dispersed state the global free-energy minimum. Adding salt shortens the Debye–Hückel screening length and can lower the barrier, allowing colloidal coagulation. Changing pH can alter surface ionization, so screening alone does not determine stability.
One can vary salt concentration while holding particle concentration, temperature and pH fixed, then follow particle aggregation using light scattering or microscopy. A solvent-only control and an independently measured surface-charge indicator help distinguish a change of screening from adsorption or charge reversal. Classical DLVO theory omits steric stabilization, hydration forces and strong ionic correlations; the formal attraction diverging at contact is cut off by molecular-scale physics.

The coil-globule transition is the contraction of an isolated flexible polymer as solvent quality worsens. A swollen chain in a good solvent has size R∼aN3/5 in the Flory approximation. At the theta temperature the effective two-segment excluded volume vanishes and R∼aN1/2. A poor solvent favors a dense polymer globule, with R∝N1/3 at fixed internal density.
The competition is visible in the scaling free energy
kB​TF​∼Na2R2​+R3vN2​+R6uN3​,u>0.
(12)
For v>0, elastic cost balances two-segment repulsion to give the swollen-coil law. At v=0, three-segment repulsion stabilizes ideal-chain scaling; for v<0, attraction balanced by the positive three-segment term gives R3∼uN/∣v∣. The dense polymer globule also has an interfacial free energy. The scaling expression explains regimes, not all fluctuation corrections or a detailed finite-chain transition curve. A finite chain crosses over smoothly; the sharp theta-point limit refers to indefinitely long chains.
A dilute-solution light-scattering experiment can follow radius of gyration while changing temperature or solvent composition. Measure at several concentrations and extrapolate toward isolated chains; check molecular-weight distribution, reversibility and the absence of growing multi-chain aggregates. dynamic light scattering measures hydrodynamic size, which is related to but distinct from radius of gyration. A falling scattering-derived size accompanied by a rising aggregate population cannot by itself establish single-chain collapse. solvent quality controls conformation through the balance of segment interactions and conformational entropy.

Write s for the number of grafted chains per unit area and Σ=sa2 for its dimensionless form; this separates the physical grafting density from a dimensionless coverage. A free dilute chain has size R0​∼aNν, with ν=1/2 in a theta solvent and approximately 3/5 in a good solvent. An isolated end-grafted chain loses translations and configurations that cross the substrate, but retains the same leading size exponent if the surface is nonadsorbing. When the lateral graft spacing D∼s−1/2 exceeds R0​, the layer consists of polymer mushrooms. When coils overlap, crowding stretches the chains away from the surface into a polymer brush.
The polymer mushroom-to-brush crossover is
s∗​∼R0−2​,Σ∗​∼N−2ν.​
(13)
For the stipulated statistical-segment interpretation of N and a, the scaling estimates are
θgood​R0​14nm25.5nm​D∗​14nm25.5nm​s∗​(nm−2)5.10×10−31.54×10−3​​
(14)
Thus Σ∗​≃2.50×10−3 and 7.54×10−4 respectively. The numerical constants are scaling conventions: using a radius of gyration rather than an end-to-end size changes order-one amplitudes, not the powers of N.
In the Alexander-de Gennes brush model, take blobs of lateral size D. Within a blob, D∼agν; beyond it, the N/g blobs are aligned away from the wall. Thus
g∼(D/a)1/ν,H∼ND/g=NaΣ(1−ν)/(2ν).​
(15)
With coarse-grained segment volume a3, conservation gives the polymer-brush volume fraction through ϕH∼Na3s, hence
ϕ∼Σ(3ν−1)/(2ν),θgood​HNaΣ1/2NaΣ1/3​ϕΣ1/2Σ2/3​​​
(16)
The theta-solvent brush height includes three-segment repulsion even though the effective two-segment interaction vanishes. These are scaling estimates for a roughly uniform brush, not an assertion that every chain end lies at the same height in an actual brush.
The many-segment-blob requirement g≫1 gives D≫a, or Σ≪1. Together with overlap, the regime is N−2ν≪Σ≪1. At the dense-brush packing limit, ϕ∼1, g∼1 and H∼Na. Here s∼a−2=2.04nm−2 and H∼280nm. If one formally continues a dense incompressible layer above this boundary, conservation would instead give
ϕ≃1,H≃Na3s=NaΣ.​
(17)
This is the requested formal dense-layer expression, but for Σ>1 it gives H>Na, which violates finite extensibility of a polymer chain. There is no physically admissible continuation above this limit in the stated segment-volume model. A Kuhn length segment is not necessarily a cube of material: with an independently specified material volume v0​ per counted segment, the dense-layer law is H=Nv0​s, and the packing bound is s≤a/v0​. The data do not determine v0​ separately. The simple model therefore establishes its own breakdown, rather than a universal microscopic maximum grafting density.
grafting-to uses preformed chains whose lengths and dispersities can be characterized before attachment. As coverage rises, each incoming coil must penetrate a crowded brush and lose conformational entropy to bring its end to the surface; the growing insertion barrier limits achievable coverage. grafting-from supplies small monomers to surface-bound growing chains and avoids insertion of a whole coil. Choose grafting-from for high grafting density. Initiator coverage and reaction control still matter, and inaccessible active sites, termination and unequal growth can broaden the resulting chain-length distribution. Neither route removes the elastic and packing costs of the final brush.
A generic application is steric stabilization of synthetic colloids in a good solvent. Bringing coated particles together compresses and interpenetrates their brushes, increasing osmotic pressure and conformational free-energy cost. This repulsion can oppose aggregation. The chains must remain attached and sufficiently cover the surface; poor-solvent collapse, sparse coverage or interparticle bridging can defeat stabilization.

Use the Monge representation r(x,y)=(x,y,h(x,y)) for a single-valued surface without overhangs. Its area element is dA=1+∣∇h∣2​dxdy; at small slopes, dA≃[1+∣∇h∣2/2]dxdy and twice the mean curvature is 2H≃∇2h, up to orientation sign. For zero spontaneous curvature, the quadratic excess Helfrich energy is
F=21​∫dxdy[κ(∇2h)2+σ∣∇h∣2].​
(18)
The fluid membrane shear response has no equilibrium static in-plane shear modulus: constituents can rearrange to relax shear. Bending and isotropic tension still cost energy; viscous shear stresses can matter during motion. A Gaussian curvature term is omitted under the stated approximation; for fixed closed-surface topology its integral is constant, but boundaries and topology changes require care. The small-slope Monge expression describes weak deformations, not an entire fully wrapped sphere.[ref]
For spherical-particle wrapping by a fluid membrane, integrate the spherical area element from polar angle 0 to α:
A=∫02π​dφ∫0α​r02​sinθdθ=2πr02​(1−cosα)=2πr02​ξ.
(19)
Its footprint radius is r∥​=r0​sinα, so
A∥​=πr02​(2ξ−ξ2),ΔA=πr02​ξ2.​
(20)
For a spherical surface 2H=2/r0​, making the bending cost κ(2/r0​)2A/2=4πκξ. adhesion contributes −2πwr02​ξ, and tension contributes σΔA. Therefore
πκE​=(4−2wˉ)ξ+σˉξ2,wˉ=κwr02​​,σˉ=κσr02​​.​
(21)
All three contributions are energies; wˉ and σˉ are dimensionless. This is the specified bound-patch model: deformation of the free membrane and the neck is omitted.
The allowed interval 0≤ξ≤2 corresponds to no adhesion, partial wrapping and full wrapping. At ξ=2, the cap covers a whole sphere and its footprint shrinks to zero. This is a closed neck in the geometric limit. It does not by itself prove membrane scission: creating a detached enclosure changes topology and involves neck physics absent from this energy.
For positive membrane tension, E′′/(πκ)=2σˉ>0. Projecting the unconstrained minimum (wˉ−2)/σˉ onto the allowed interval gives
ξmin​=⎩⎨⎧​0,(wˉ−2)/σˉ,2,​wˉ≤2,2<wˉ<2+2σˉ,wˉ≥2+2σˉ.​​
(22)
The tension-dependent wrapping phase diagram boundaries are wˉ=2 and wˉ=2+2σˉ. At zero membrane tension, the energy is linear: the minimum jumps from 0 to 2 as wˉ crosses 2, while all indentation values are degenerate at wˉ=2. Negative membrane tension is outside the stable-flat-membrane interpretation used here. Increasing membrane tension makes the excess area required for wrapping more expensive, broadening the partially wrapped region.
