The relevant comparison is between gravity and surface tension. With the mass density difference Δρ≃103kgm−3, the Bond number and capillary length are
Bo=γΔρgr2​=0.072103(9.81)(5×10−4)2​≃0.034,ℓc​=Δρgγ​​≃2.71mm.
(1)
Thus the drop is small compared with the capillary length; hydrostatic pressure variations only weakly perturb its shape. The Young–Laplace equation then gives approximately constant interfacial mean curvature, and an axisymmetric drop on a uniform flat substrate is a spherical cap. The substrate selects the contact angle, measured through the water.
Write R for the radius of the generating sphere, s for the contact-circle radius, and h for the apex height. Elementary trigonometric identities give
s=Rsinθ,h=R(1−cosθ)=stan(θ/2).
(2)
Taking the stated droplet radius to mean its radius of curvature, R=r, gives
h=0.5(1−cos55∘)mm=0.213mm.​
(3)
There is no diagram or explicit radius convention in the original PDF. If r instead denotes the contact-circle radius, the answer is h=rtan27.5∘=0.260mm. If it denotes the radius of an equal-volume free sphere, use the volume of a spherical cap, πh2(3R−h)/3=4πr3/3, to obtain h=r[4(1−cosθ)/(2+cosθ)]1/3=0.436mm. The spherical cap approximation is justified in all three conventions; a unique numerical height requires specifying which radius is meant.
