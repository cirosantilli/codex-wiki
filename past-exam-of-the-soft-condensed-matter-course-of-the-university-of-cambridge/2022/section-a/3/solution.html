Treat the liposome using the usual incompressible-tail packing model of a lipid bilayer: neglect the head-group thickness and let v be the volume filling the hydrophobic part of the lipid vesicle. Both membrane leaflets have area a per head group. Put d=v/a, and let R be the unknown outer radius. Counting molecules from the inner and outer surface areas gives
Mi​=a4πri2​​,Mo​=a4πR2​.
(7)
Equating the total molecular volume to the volume of the spherical shell therefore gives
34π​(R3−ri3​)=v(Mi​+Mo​),R3−ri3​=3d(R2+ri2​).​
(8)
To prove uniqueness in the physical domain R>ri​>0, define
q(R)=R2+ri2​R3−ri3​​.
(9)
Its derivative is
q′(R)=(R2+ri2​)2R4+3R2ri2​+2Rri3​​>0.
(10)
Also q(ri​)=0 and q(R)→∞ as R→∞. By continuity and strict monotonicity, q(R)=3d has exactly one solution with R>ri​. This proves the required uniqueness without choosing among ambiguous branches of a cubic formula.
The maximum tail length is a physical admissibility bound, not automatically the actual leaflet thickness. If rm​ is the radius where the two tail regions meet, separate membrane leaflet volume balances give
rm3​=ri3​+3dri2​,R3−rm3​=3dR2.
(11)
The unique candidate must satisfy
rm​−ri​≤l,R−rm​≤l.​
(12)
In particular R−ri​≤2l is necessary. The premise that a lipid vesicle has assembled supplies this compatibility: arbitrary positive choices of ri​,v,a,l need not allow any such lipid bilayer. For a very large vesicle the two thicknesses tend to d, so R−ri​≃2v/a. Setting the thickness equal to 2l for every molecule would confuse maximum extension with actual packing. The volume-balance argument and its domain are an example of uniqueness of the packed spherical bilayer radius.
