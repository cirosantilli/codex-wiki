First hold the surface electric charge fixed and increase the bulk salt concentration. Since κ2∝c, the Debye–Hückel screening length decreases as c−1/2. For surface gap h=R−2a≥0,
Uel​(R)∝(1+κa)2e−κh​,∂κ∂logUel​​=−h−1+κa2a​<0.
(51)
Thus both the range and the strength of repulsion decrease. In the repulsive phase diagram, the sample moves downwards at fixed volume fraction and can melt from a crystal into a fluid. Because screening length is already a diagram axis, changing salt moves the state through that diagram rather than independently changing its fixed-charge coexistence curves. If Van der Waals forces become dominant, however, adding salt may instead destabilize the dispersion and cause aggregation. These are distinct physical regimes.
Second increase σ at fixed salt concentration and particle size. The electric charge is Q=4πa2σ, so the pair repulsion increases approximately as σ2 within the Debye–Hückel approximation, while the reservoir screening length remains unchanged. Freezing then becomes possible at lower volume fraction or shorter screening length. At fixed λD​, the coexistence curves shift towards lower η; a previously fluid sample may crystallize.
These statements assume fixed, uniform charge and a salt reservoir dominating the screening. Strong charging requires nonlinear screening and possibly charge regulation. At very low added salt or appreciable particle concentration, particle-released counterions also contribute to ionic strength, so the screening length is no longer independently fixed by the added salt alone.
