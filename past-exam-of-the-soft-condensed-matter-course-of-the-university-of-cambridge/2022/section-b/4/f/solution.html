For the usual interpretation of a real chain in a good solvent, the detachment force is smaller in the weak-adsorption regime. Excluded volume swells the chain, increasing the cost of confinement near the substrate. The adsorbed layer becomes thicker and its binding free energy per monomer decreases. The same contact energy therefore does not mean the same total binding strength.
The scaling argument can include both adsorption and stretching. Let ξ∼bgν, where ν=1/2 for an ideal chain and approximately 3/5 for a self-avoiding walk in three dimensions. With the same contact-fraction approximation,
NkB​TFads​​∼(ξb​)1/ν−ϵξb​.
(24)
Minimization gives ξ/b∼ϵ−ν/(1−ν) and μ/(kB​T)∼ϵ1/(1−ν). At detachment, a tensile blob of size ξt​∼kB​T/f costs approximately kB​T; its monomer count is gt​∼(ξt​/b)1/ν. Equating the stretching gain per monomer kB​T/gt​ to μ gives
f∼bkB​T​(kB​Tμ​)ν∼bkB​T​ϵν/(1−ν).
(25)
Thus freal​∼(kB​T/b)ϵ3/2, compared with fideal​∼(kB​T/b)ϵ, so their ratio scales as ϵ1/2<1 for weak attraction. These are scaling relations, not exact prefactors. The real-chain adsorption exponents here use the same simple uniform-contact model as the earlier parts; a more detailed surface theory of critical phenomena can refine them. The thicker, more weakly bound layer reflects the higher cost of confining a good solvent chain.[ref]
“Real” alone does not specify solvent quality: a collapsed chain in a poor solvent, strong adsorption, or substantial bending stiffness requires a different comparison. The smaller-force conclusion concerns the flexible good solvent chain implicit in the excluded-volume correction.
