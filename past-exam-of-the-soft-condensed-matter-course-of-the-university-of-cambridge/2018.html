Assume steady fully developed laminar flow of a Newtonian fluid in a long circular tube, with no slip at a rigid wall. The Hagen-Poiseuille equation gives the volume flow rate
Q=8ηℓπR4Δp​,
(1)
where R is the lumen radius, ℓ the tube length, η the dynamic viscosity and Δp the pressure drop. If Δp, ℓ and η remain fixed, then
Qold​Qnew​​=(Rold​Rnew​​)4.
(2)
Thus doubling the flow requires
Rnew​=21/4Rold​≃1.189Rold​,Rold​ΔR​≃18.9%​.
(3)
This estimate neglects pulsation, arterial compliance, entrance losses, the detailed shape of a stenosis and changes in the rest of the circulatory resistance. It treats blood as a homogeneous Newtonian fluid and assumes that enlarging the artery does not change its applied pressure drop. These assumptions supply the intended fourth-power scaling; an actual circulation need not keep that pressure drop fixed.

34πa3​(ρp​−ρw​)g=6πηavt​.
(4)

vt​=9η2(ρp​−ρw​)ga2​​.

vt​≃9(10−3)2(4.2×103)(9.81)(10−6)2​=9.2×10−6ms−1≃9μms−1downwards​.

The polymer overlap concentration is reached when there is roughly one chain per coil volume. If M is the chain molar mass, choose the explicit geometric convention Vcoil​=4πRg3​/3, where Rg​ is the radius of gyration. Then
c∗≃NA​(4π/3)Rg3​M​​.
(7)
Here c∗ is mass per solution volume and NA​ is the Avogadro constant. A good solvent gives Rg​∝Nν with ν≃0.588, approximated by 3/5 in the Flory approximation. With a polystyrene repeat-unit molar mass of about 104.15gmol−1, the chain has approximately N=960 repeat units. This fixes the chain length but not the size amplitude: the problem supplies no statistical segment length or measured coil size.
For a numerical estimate, use the good-solvent size calibration for polystyrene,
nmRg​​≃0.0123[gmol−1M​]0.593.
(8)
This empirical calibration is quoted for benzene or toluene near room temperature at molar masses above 2×105gmol−1; using it here is a modest extrapolation to M=105gmol−1. It gives Rg​≃11.3nm. Thus
c∗≃(6.022×1023mol−1)(4π/3)(11.3×10−9m)3100kgmol−1​≃3×101kgm−3≃3×101gL−1​.
(9)
The unrounded calibration gives about 27gL−1. This is an estimate under a stated extra material input, not a unique number determined solely by molecular weight and the phrase good solvent. Choosing a different coil-volume convention changes its prefactor. The robust chain-length dependence is c∗∝M1−3ν≃M−4/5 for a flexible good-solvent coil.

Two stabilization mechanisms. Steric stabilization coats each colloid with adsorbed or grafted polymer layers. Bringing two particles close overlaps or compresses those layers, increasing their free energy through loss of chain conformations and, in a suitable solvent, increased local osmotic pressure. The resulting repulsion can keep the cores apart. A well-solvated nonionic coating can remain effective when added salt would screen electrostatic forces. Coatings can fail through desorption, poor solvent conditions or bridging, so simply adding arbitrary free polymer does not guarantee stabilization.
Charge stabilization of colloids instead uses similarly charged surfaces and their diffuse electric double layers. Their approach costs electrostatic and ionic free energy. In DLVO theory, that repulsion competes with attractive Van der Waals forces. It is particularly sensitive to salt concentration, counterion valence and surface ionization.
Bjerrum length. The standard Bjerrum length is the separation at which the Coulomb interaction energy of two elementary charges equals thermal energy:
4πε0​εr​ℓB​e2​=kB​T,ℓB​=4πε0​εr​kB​Te2​​.
(10)
At T≃298K and εr​=78, this is
ℓB​≃7.2×10−10m=0.72nm​.
(11)
It compares ionic electrostatic coupling with thermal agitation. For ions of signed valences z1​,z2​, U/(kB​T)=z1​z2​ℓB​/r. The standard reference length uses e, not the whole colloid charge: assigning a colloid charge Ze does not change the solvent's tabulated ℓB​.
Screening and the printed normalization. In a symmetric z:z electrolyte, z is the ionic valence magnitude, e the elementary electric charge, n∞​ the bulk number density of each ionic species, ε0​ the vacuum permittivity, εR​ the solvent's relative permittivity, kB​ the Boltzmann constant and T absolute temperature. Mobile ions rearrange around a charged surface; the Debye–Hückel screening length κ−1 sets the range of the resulting weak potential.
There is an SI normalization error in the original PDF. Combining the Boltzmann distribution n±​=n∞​e∓zeψ/(kB​T) with the SI Poisson equation and linearizing at ∣zeψ∣≪kB​T gives
∇2ψ=κSI2​ψ,κSI2​=ε0​εR​kB​T2z2e2n∞​​=8πℓB​z2n∞​​.
(12)
The extra 4π in the PDF denominator is inconsistent with those SI charge, permittivity and density conventions. Keeping the printed algebra would make its screening length 4π​ times the SI value at the same ion density. No numerical screening length is required here, and the qualitative dependence κ−1∝n∞−1/2​ is the same in either normalization. Added salt shortens the range of repulsion, allowing the attractive potential to capture particles more readily.
Interaction-potential sketches. Let h be the surface separation between two spheres of radius a. A useful schematic DLVO theory potential has the form
U(h)≃Be−κh−12hAH​a​,
(13)
away from the microscopic contact region. Here AH​>0 is a Hamaker constant; excluded-core repulsion and molecular details regularize contact. The exponential is the double-layer repulsion and the inverse-gap term the short-gap Van der Waals force contribution. A fixed B illustrates the changing screening range; actual charge regulation can change the repulsive amplitude as well.
At low salt there is a high positive barrier between a deep primary minimum near contact and the dispersed state at U(∞)=0. A shallow secondary minimum can occur outside the barrier and support weak reversible association. Increasing salt narrows the repulsive range and lowers the barrier. Thermally activated primary aggregation becomes appreciable when the barrier is only a few kB​T; the idealized barrier-free condition defines the fast-aggregation threshold associated with the critical coagulation concentration. Above that threshold, collisions can result in rapid capture in the primary minimum.
Aggregate structures. Well above the critical coagulation concentration, the sticking probability is close to one. In diffusion-limited cluster aggregation, the first encounter attaches particles or clusters at accessible outer branches before they can explore many alternative configurations. The result is an open, tenuous structure. Typical ideal three-dimensional cluster models have mass fractal dimension df​≃1.8.
Near the threshold on its lower-salt, slow-aggregation side, a remaining barrier makes most encounters unsuccessful. Reaction-limited cluster aggregation then permits repeated approaches and orientations before attachment, commonly giving denser clusters with df​≃2.1. Since particle count scales as N∝(Rg​/a)df​, the larger exponent means more particles at a given large cluster size. This explanation does not require already formed bonds to rearrange: unsuccessful encounters themselves alter which configurations eventually bind.
The side of the threshold matters. At the ideal barrier-free threshold itself, or just above it, aggregation can already be diffusion limited; proximity to the critical coagulation concentration alone does not universally imply a denser reaction-limited structure. The quoted dimensions are typical of irreversible approximately isotropic cluster models, not exact values for every colloid. Strong bond rearrangement or anisotropic attractions can change this comparison.

Oscillatory response. A material with viscoelasticity stores some deformation energy and dissipates some over a loading cycle. In small-amplitude oscillatory shear, represent the strain by γ(t)=Re(γ0​eiωt). The shear-stress amplitude is related to the strain amplitude by the complex viscoelastic modulus
G∗(ω)=G1​(ω)+iG2​(ω)​,τ(t)=γ0​[G1​cosωt−G2​sinωt].
(14)
Here G1​=G′ is the storage modulus, the component in phase with strain, and G2​=G′′ is the loss modulus, the dissipative quadrature component. Both have units of pressure. The stress leads the strain by an angle δ, with
tanδ=G1​G2​​​.
(15)
For this eiωt convention, a passive material has G2​≥0 at positive frequency. Integrating τγ˙​ over one period gives dissipated energy per unit volume πγ02​G2​, and the average input power per unit volume is ωγ02​G2​/2. Thus the loss tangent compares the dissipative and elastic parts of the response. The frequency dependence reflects which internal relaxation processes can follow the imposed deformation.
Torsional measurement. Use ρ for radial position inside the rod, reserving r for its outer radius. A small angular displacement θ of one end relative to the fixed other end gives shear strain
γ(ρ)=ℓρθ​.
(16)
In harmonic amplitude notation, the shear stress is G∗ρΘ/ℓ. Its restoring torque is obtained by multiplying by the lever arm and integrating over the circular section:
T=−∫0r​ρℓG∗ρΘ​2πρdρ=−2ℓπr4​G∗Θ.
(17)
The factor J=∫A​ρ2dA=πr4/2 is the polar second moment of area; it is not the disc's mass moment of inertia I. The torsional spring constant becomes the complex stiffness K∗=JG∗/ℓ. Angular Newton's second law therefore gives the paper's compact representation
Iθ¨+2ℓπr4​(G1​+iG2​)θ=0​.
(18)
Its precise interpretation is a torsional measurement of a complex shear modulus. At a specified frequency, with complex angular amplitude Θ, the driven response obeys
[−Iω2+2ℓπr4​G∗(ω)]Θ=Texternal​.
(19)
Setting the external torque to zero supplies the free-mode condition, whose damped frequencies are generally complex. A dissipative free oscillator does not execute a sustained nonzero oscillation at a real frequency without driving. In real time, a general frequency-dependent complex viscoelastic modulus represents a causal memory response, rather than a complex instantaneous force on a real displacement. The derivation assumes a uniform slender circular rod, small twist, negligible rod inertia relative to the disc, and no additional bending, slip or external damping.
Frequency regimes and sketches. A permanently crosslinked elastomer has a low-frequency rubbery plateau GR​>0. Slow chain rearrangements can follow the strain, so the network is relatively compliant but still elastic. At high frequency the relevant segmental motions are frozen, giving a much larger unrelaxed or glassy stiffness GU​. Between these regimes the motions lag behind the loading and dissipate strongly.
A standard linear solid model supplies a consistent illustrative sketch of this process. Put x=ωτr​, where τr​ is the stress viscoelastic relaxation time, and ΔG=GU​−GR​>0. Then
G∗=GR​+ΔG1+ixix​,G1​=GR​+ΔG1+x2x2​,G2​=ΔG1+x2x​​,
(20)
so
tanδ=GR​+GU​x2ΔGx​​.
(21)
At very low frequency, G1​→GR​, G2​∼ΔGx and tanδ→0. Through the relaxation region G1​ rises, G2​ has a maximum and tanδ has a loss peak. At high frequency, G1​→GU​, G2​∼ΔG/x and tanδ→0 again. Real polymers usually have a spectrum of relaxation times, so these features are broader and can have further structure.
The two kinds of loss peak need not coincide. The peak frequencies in a standard linear solid are
ωG2​​τr​=1,ωtanδ​τr​=GR​/GU​​​.
(22)
The first follows by differentiating x/(1+x2); the second by differentiating x/(GR​+GU​x2). This is why the sketch marks the peaks separately. If the polymer is an uncrosslinked melt rather than a permanent network, there is an additional ultimate low-frequency terminal-flow regime, with G1​∝ω2 and G2​∝ω; a permanent elastomer does not have that zero-frequency fluid limit.
Interstitial atoms in a metal. A suitable mechanism is the Snoek effect, a form of anelasticity. An interstitial defect distorts its local crystal structure anisotropically. Different site orientations become unequally favorable under an applied stress, so hopping atoms redistribute towards the preferred orientations and supply additional strain. The hopping time is thermally activated, approximately τa​=τ0​eEa​/(kB​T), with activation energy Ea​.
At low frequency, ωτa​≪1, the populations nearly equilibrate during each cycle. The defect contribution to strain is nearly in phase with stress, giving a relaxed compliance and little dissipation. At intermediate frequency, ωτa​∼1, redistribution lags behind the stress and energy dissipation is largest. At high frequency, ωτa​≫1, the populations are effectively frozen; the extra relaxation strain cannot develop, and the response approaches the stiffer unrelaxed elastic limit. For weak single-mode relaxation, the loss tangent is approximately proportional to ωτa​/[1+(ωτa​)2]. The metal remains a solid in the low-frequency regime: the extra delayed strain is recoverable, not unrestricted viscous flow.

Ideal and self-avoiding walks. An ideal chain has independent isotropic coarse-grained steps and permits self-intersections in its mathematical model. For N steps of length b, the cross terms average to zero, so its root-mean-square end-to-end distance of a polymer chain obeys
⟨R2⟩=Nb2,Rrms​∝N1/2​.
(23)
A self-avoiding walk excludes repeated occupancy or intersection at the segment scale. This represents positive excluded volume in a dilute good solvent. The nonlocal constraint correlates otherwise distant portions of the chain and swells it:
Rrms​∝bNν,ν≃0.588in three dimensions​.
(24)
The Flory approximation replaces this exponent by 3/5, by balancing stretching free energy R2/(Nb2) with an interaction contribution vN2/R3. The result is a more extended and less internally dense coil than the ideal walk; self-avoidance changes the statistics, not merely the local step length.
Two ideal-chain regimes. At the theta temperature for a dilute polymer–solvent pair, leading effective segment attractions and repulsions cancel. Large-scale chain statistics are approximately ideal, even though microscopic atoms still have finite size. In a homogeneous polymer melt, surrounding chains screen long-range excluded volume interactions of a chosen chain, again making its equilibrium large-scale size approximately ideal. Local bond-angle correlations must be included in an effective statistical length such as the Kuhn length. Polymer entanglements can slow the dynamics without invalidating this leading equilibrium size law.
Semiflexible DNA. The nucleic acid double helix gives double-stranded DNA a bending stiffness and substantial local orientational persistence. Independent step orientations are therefore a poor description on short scales, while self-avoidance alone does not specify the energy cost of bending. The worm-like chain describes the molecule as a continuously bendable, locally inextensible curve r(s) parameterized by arc length s, with unit tangent t(s)=dr/ds. Its bending energy is
Eb​=2A​∫0L​​dsdt​​2ds,∣t∣=1,
(25)
where A is bending stiffness. In the free three-dimensional model, the persistence length is ℓp​=A/(kB​T) and
⟨t(s)⋅t(s+u)⟩=e−u/ℓp​​.
(26)
Thus ℓp​ is the contour separation over which tangent alignment falls by the factor e−1; it quantifies stiffness relative to thermal bending. The model bridges locally rod-like and globally coil-like conformations. It omits long-range self-avoidance in its simplest form, so sufficiently long dilute DNA can require excluded-volume corrections.
Flexible and rigid limits. Integrating the tangent correlation over the two contour positions gives the end-to-end distance of a worm-like chain:
⟨R2⟩=2∫0L​(L−u)e−u/ℓp​du=2ℓp​L−2ℓp2​(1−e−L/ℓp​).
(27)
For a very flexible chain on the scale of its total length, L/ℓp​≫1, the exponential is negligible and
⟨R2⟩≃2ℓp​L,Rrms​≃2ℓp​L​≪L​.
(28)
This is the ideal chain result with Kuhn length bK​=2ℓp​ and effective step count NK​=L/(2ℓp​). Many persistent sections have decorrelated orientations. The flexible limit means a large ratio L/ℓp​ within the continuum model's range, not an extrapolation of the persistence length below all molecular length scales.
For a nearly rigid chain, L/ℓp​≪1, the constant and linear terms in the exponential cancel those outside it. The Taylor series gives
⟨R2⟩=L2−3ℓp​L3​+12ℓp2​L4​+⋯,
(29)
so
Rrms​≃L(1−6ℓp​L​+⋯)⟶L​.
(30)
This is the expected rod limit: all tangents are nearly aligned, and the end-to-end distance approaches the arc length.
Helix–coil curves. A helix-coil transition model allows ordered helical runs and disordered coil regions. Use s and σ with exactly the free-energy conventions defined in the paper. A negative ΔFhc​ gives s>1 and favors the helical state; a positive junction cost gives 0<σ<1 and penalizes starting new domains. The supplied helical fraction is the long-chain form associated with the Zimm-Bragg model.
Its main features follow directly without assuming a sharp transition. Put Q=(s−1)2+4sσ. For every σ>0,
s↓0lim​fh​(s)=0,fh​(1)=21​,s→∞lim​fh​(s)=1​,
(31)
and differentiating gives
dsdfh​​=Q3/2σ(s+1)​>0,dsdfh​​​s=1​=4σ​1​​.
(32)
Thus all curves rise monotonically and cross at (1,1/2). Increasing the boundary free-energy cost reduces σ, suppresses frequent coil–helix boundaries and makes longer runs favorable. The transition region becomes narrower and steeper: balancing (s−1)2 against 4sσ gives a width of order σ​ near s=1.
For σ=1, the fraction simplifies to fh​=s/(1+s)​, the noncooperative two-state result. For 0<σ≪1, the steep curve describes cooperative helix formation. In the singular limit σ↓0, it approaches zero for s<1 and one for s>1; its midpoint remains 1/2 when approached through positive σ. Setting σ=0 directly makes the formula indeterminate at s=1. For any fixed positive σ, the given long-chain curve is smooth: it is a cooperative crossover, not a true discontinuity. The plot varies s at fixed σ; in an actual temperature scan both parameters may change, and a finite chain can further round the crossover.
