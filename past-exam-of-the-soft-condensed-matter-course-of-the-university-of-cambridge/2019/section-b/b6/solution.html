Stabilization and screening. Charge stabilization of colloids means suppressing aggregation through repulsion between similarly charged particles and their electric double layers. In DLVO theory, this repulsion competes with attractive Van der Waals forces. A substantial repulsive barrier compared with thermal energy kB​T can make aggregation slow, even when the dispersed state is only metastable.
The Debye–Hückel screening length λD​=κ−1 is the characteristic range over which a weak electric potential is attenuated by rearrangement of mobile ions. It measures the thickness of a diffuse screening cloud, not a molecular diameter. For a plane, a weak potential decays as e−κx into the bulk.
Derivation and the source convention. Put a planar interface at x=0, with electrolyte in x>0, bulk potential zero, and absolute permittivity ε=ε0​εr​. For a symmetric z:z electrolyte, let n∞​ be the bulk number density of each ionic species. The Boltzmann distribution gives
n±​(x)=n∞​exp(∓kB​Tzeψ(x)​),
(39)

ρe​=ze(n+​−n−​)=−2zen∞​sinh(kB​Tzeψ​).
(40)
Substitution into the SI Poisson equation, ∇2ψ=−ρe​/ε, gives the Poisson-Boltzmann equation
∇2ψ=ε2zen∞​​sinh(kB​Tzeψ​).
(41)
For ∣zeψ∣≪kB​T, linearize the hyperbolic sine. The Debye–Hückel approximation is then
∇2ψ=κ2ψ,κ2=ε0​εr​kB​T2z2e2n∞​​(SI)​.
(42)
Here z is the ionic valence magnitude, e the elementary electric charge, kB​ the Boltzmann constant, T absolute temperature, ε0​ the vacuum permittivity, and εr​ the solvent's relative permittivity. For several ionic species, the numerator 2z2e2n∞​ is replaced by e2∑i​zi2​ni,∞​.
The original PDF includes an additional 4π in the denominator. That factor does not follow from SI Poisson equation with number densities and the stated permittivities. The Bjerrum length provides a useful check: ℓB​=e2/(4πεkB​T) and the consistent result is κ2=8πℓB​z2n∞​. A Gaussian-unit convention moves factors of 4π together with the charge and permittivity conventions; it does not justify inserting this extra factor while retaining all the given SI quantities. Both numerical interpretations are reported below rather than silently changing the source.
Conditions favoring stability. Increasing the surface-potential or surface-charge magnitude usually strengthens the electrostatic barrier. Lower ionic strength increases λD​ and generally makes repulsion longer ranged; added salt and especially multivalent counterions shorten it and can promote aggregation. Suitable pH can keep an ionizable surface charged, while conditions near an isoelectric state weaken charge repulsion. The solvent-dependent Van der Waals forces, charge regulation and particle geometry also affect the barrier, so a long screening length is not by itself sufficient. At fixed density and valence, the linear formula gives λD​∝εT​, but actual temperature changes can also alter the permittivity and surface chemistry.
Surface charge at small potential. Take the usual one-sided planar-interface model, with negligible electric displacement on the nonaqueous side. The solution decaying into the solution is
ψ(x)=ψ0​e−κx​.
(43)
The electric field is Ex​=−ψ′=κψ0​e−κx. The electrostatic interface boundary conditions then give the surface charge density
σ=εEx​(0+)=εκψ0​​.
(44)

∫0∞​ρe​(x)dx=−εκ2ψ0​∫0∞​e−κxdx=−εκψ0​=−σ.

If instead the object is an isolated sheet wetted by identical electrolyte on both sides, each side screens half its total charge and σsheet​=2εκψ0​. The one-sided assumption is the conventional local model for the exterior of a large charged colloid; it must be stated because the question does not specify the material on the other side.
