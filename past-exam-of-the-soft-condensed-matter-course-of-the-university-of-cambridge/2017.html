Entropic elasticity. A rubber contains flexible polymer strands tied into a network by cross-links. Stretching biases the strands toward relatively extended configurations, reducing their configurational entropy. The restoring entropic force follows from the increase in Helmholtz free energy F=U−TS. For a Gaussian strand with Ns​ statistical steps of length b, its deformation-dependent free energy is approximately 3kB​TR2/(2Ns​b2). Its stiffness therefore increases with absolute temperature, even if the underlying bond stiffness changes little.
In the ideal affine-network model of rubber elasticity, the shear modulus is
G=νs​kB​T,​
(1)
where νs​ is the number density of elastically active network strands. Thus heating increases the modulus approximately in proportion to T, provided the network density and rubbery state remain approximately unchanged. Thermal expansion, changing relaxation times or damage can modify that simple trend.
Examples are lightly crosslinked natural rubber, based on cis-rich polyisoprene, and crosslinked polydimethylsiloxane. The crosslinks prevent the long-time macroscopic flow of an unconnected chain solution.
Density estimate and the unspecified modulus convention. With room temperature T≃298K, the Boltzmann constant gives kB​T≃4.11×10−21J. If the stated modulus is G=2×105Pa, then
νs​=kB​TG​≃4.9×1025m−3≃81molm−3.​
(2)
The molar value counts moles of active strands. If “modulus” instead means Young's modulus E, a nearly incompressible isotropic rubber has E≃3G, and the inferred value is
νs​≃3kB​TE​=1.6×1025m−3≃27molm−3.​
(3)
Thus the robust order-of-magnitude estimate is 1025m−3. The cross-link density inferred from rubber elasticity is usually an effective strand density; the actual junction density is νj​=2νs​/f for junction functionality f in an ideal network without inactive loops or dangling ends. For four-functional junctions this would be half the strand density. The paper supplies neither modulus type nor junction functionality, so a unique chemical junction count cannot be extracted.

For an ideally dissociated electrolyte, the Debye–Hückel screening length is
λD​=(e2∑i​ni​zi2​ε0​εr​kB​T​)1/2,I=21​∑i​ci​zi2​.
(4)
Here ni​ is ionic number density, zi​ the charge in units of the elementary charge, and ci​ is molar concentration. Conversion between I and number density cancels in a ratio. At fixed relative permittivity, λD​∝T/I​.
In 0.01M sodium chloride, cNa+​=cCl−​=0.01M, giving I1​=0.01M. Complete dissociation of 0.1M aluminium chloride gives cAl3+​=0.1M and cCl−​=0.3M, so
I2​=21​[0.1(3)2+0.3(1)2]=0.60M=60I1​.
(5)
The factor is sixty, not ten: the squared charge and the three counterions per formula unit both matter. Using the given 5nm reference length directly, and T1​≃298K, T2​=323K,
λD,2​=5nm298323​0.600.01​​≃0.67nm.​
(6)
The modest temperature increase raises the screening length by only about 4% at fixed concentration; the large increase in ionic strength shortens it by almost an order of magnitude overall.
Under charge stabilization of colloids, the electrostatic double-layer repulsion now has a much shorter range. Attractive Van der Waals forces can then dominate at closer separations, reducing the barrier to particle aggregation in DLVO theory. Thus an electrostatically stabilized colloid is likely to be destabilized, with aggregation more likely than in the original dispersion.
This is a conditional physical prediction, not a proof of instability from a length alone. No particle radius, surface potential, attractive interaction strength or steric coating is specified. Steric stabilization could preserve stability, and trivalent-ion adsorption can alter the surface charge. Moreover, ideal screening theory is only an estimate at I≃0.6M, where ion association, hydrolysis and activities can matter. The numerical result uses the supplied reference length and the stipulated unchanged permittivity, rather than replacing either with an unprovided calibration.

Write the membrane height above its mean plane as a sum of Fourier modes, h(r)=∑q​hq​eiq⋅r. The supplied average is a thermal mean-squared modal height; for complex Fourier coefficients it is understood as ⟨∣hq​∣2⟩, with h−q​=hq∗​ for a real height field. The terms are:
  • kB​T is the thermal energy, with Boltzmann constant kB​ and absolute temperature T.
  • A is the projected membrane area used in the Fourier normalization.
  • q=∣q∣ is the in-plane wavenumber; the corresponding wavelength is 2π/q.
  • σ is the membrane tension, in Nm−1=Jm−2.
  • κ is the membrane bending modulus, in joules.
In the small-slope approximation, Helfrich membrane energy contains
F=21​∫A​[σ∣∇h∣2+κ(∇2h)2],d2r=2A​∑q​(σq2+κq4)∣hq​∣2.
(7)
The equipartition theorem, with the independent real modes counted consistently, gives the stated membrane fluctuation spectrum under tension. Its units are length squared: σq2 and κq4 both have units Jm−4, so kB​T/[A(σq2+κq4)] has units m2.
At small length scales, q is large and the bending term dominates:
⟨∣hq​∣2⟩≃Aκq4kB​T​for q≫σ/κ​.​
(8)
The fluctuation amplitude is controlled mainly by the membrane bending modulus and thermal energy, rather than tension. The crossover occurs when σqc2​=κqc4​, hence
qc​=κσ​​,ℓc​≡qc−1​=σκ​​,λc​=2πσκ​​.​
(9)
The bending-dominated regime extends up to the membrane elastic length when scale means 1/q, or up to λc​ when it means wavelength. At still larger scales tension gives a q−2 spectrum. At the opposite end, the continuum formula requires wavelengths larger than the microscopic membrane thickness or molecular cutoff. Without numerical κ and σ, no numerical crossover scale is determined.

The Reynolds number compares inertial and viscous terms in the momentum equation:
Re=ηρUL​.​
(10)
Here ρ is fluid mass density, U a characteristic speed, L a swimmer or flow length, and η the dynamic viscosity. The advective inertia scale is ρU2/L and the viscous force-density scale is ηU/L2, whose ratio is Re. For an illustrative micron-scale swimmer with L=2μm and U=20μms−1 in water, taking ρ=103kgm−3 and η=10−3Pas gives Re∼4×10−5.
At such low Re, Stokes flow is the useful approximation. For an incompressible Newtonian fluid it obeys −∇p+η∇2u=0 and ∇⋅u=0, with negligible inertia. Swimming is not a sequence of propulsive impulses followed by coasting: drag balances propulsive forces almost instantaneously. For example, the drag on a slowly translating sphere is 6πηaU. A freely swimming organism has approximately zero total external force and torque; its body motion balances the forces and torques generated by its appendages.
The linear equations have kinematic reversibility of Stokes flow: reversing the boundary velocities reverses the fluid motion. A reciprocal stroke that retraces exactly the same sequence of shapes therefore retraces its displacement. This is the scallop theorem. A single hinge opening and closing along the same shape path cannot propel an ideal swimmer through an otherwise quiescent Newtonian Stokes fluid, regardless of merely changing how quickly it opens relative to closing.
A useful design has at least two shape coordinates driven out of phase, so the stroke follows a loop rather than a retraced line in shape space. In the three-bead example the two link lengths change in a cycle; hydrodynamic interactions differ at different points of the cycle, allowing net translation. The drawing illustrates this mechanism without assigning a particular swimming speed.
Biological routes include continuous rotation of a chiral helical flagellum, nonreciprocal ciliary strokes, and traveling bending waves along a flagellum. An elongated filament generally has greater drag for motion normal to itself than along itself; this drag anisotropy helps convert those motions into propulsion. The body may counterrotate to balance appendage torque. Micro-organisms must exploit nonreciprocal shape changes, rotation or another mechanism consistent with viscous force balance; inertia-based swimming intuition fails. Viscoelastic surroundings or appreciable inertia can invalidate the particular Newtonian assumptions behind the simple scallop result.

A grafted polymer chain is tethered to a surface at one end. Let its isolated coil size be R∼bNν, where N is the number of statistical units and b their statistical segment length. For a good solvent, the Flory approximation gives ν≃3/5; an ideal chain has ν=1/2. Let σg​ be the number of tethers per unit area, distinct from the membrane tension in Question 1. Their typical lateral separation is D∼σg−1/2​.
At low coverage, D≫R, the chains hardly overlap. Each forms a polymer mushroom, retaining roughly the isolated-coil conformation apart from the constraint of the solid surface. Raising coverage until D∼R makes neighboring coils overlap. The crossover criterion is
σg​R2∼1,σg∗​∼R−2∼b−2N−2ν.​
(11)
For D<R in a good solvent, maintaining isolated coil shapes would crowd segments into the same lateral regions, increasing excluded-volume and osmotic free energy. Chains stretch predominantly away from the surface to relieve that crowding, at the cost of their conformational entropy. The resulting dense tethered layer is a polymer brush.
A simple scaling estimate makes the balance explicit. For brush height H, the monomer concentration is of order Nσg​/H. The free energy per chain in units of kB​T is
kB​TFchain​​∼Nb2H2​+HvN2σg​​,
(12)
where v>0 is the effective segment excluded volume. The first term is Gaussian stretching; the second is the cost of segment crowding in a good solvent. Minimizing with respect to height gives
H∼Nb(bvσg​​)1/3.​
(13)
When v∼b3, this is H∼Nb(σg​b2)1/3. Height increases with chain length and grafting density, while the lateral spacing is fixed by the attachment points. The estimate assumes stretching strong compared with an isolated coil but still much smaller than contour length Nb; it does not describe fully extended chains. Poor-solvent attraction can instead collapse the layer, so solvent quality must be specified before using the good-solvent formula.
The mushroom-to-brush change is a crowding crossover for finite chains, rather than automatically a sharp thermodynamic phase transition. A swollen brush can provide steric stabilization: bringing two coated surfaces together further compresses and crowds their chains, producing a repulsive free-energy cost. The controlling comparison is tether separation versus isolated coil size, and the brush height follows from competition between chain stretching and segment interactions.

The requested model is the standard linear solid model: a spring of modulus E0​>0 in parallel with a Maxwell branch consisting of a spring E1​>0 in series with a dashpot of viscosity η>0. Both parallel branches have the same strain; their stresses add. In the Maxwell branch the spring and dashpot have the same stress and their strains add. The equilibrium spring prevents indefinite flow at long times.
For harmonic strain ϵ(t)=Re[ϵ0​eiωt], define the dynamic modulus by σ=E∗(ω)ϵ. The Maxwell branch has compliance 1/E1​+1/(iωη). Defining τ=η/E1​ therefore gives
E∗(ω)=E0​+E1​+iωηiωηE1​​=E0​+E1​1+iωτiωτ​.​
(14)
The same mechanical model can represent a shear modulus if the appropriate shear stresses and strains are used consistently.
Writing E∗=E′+iE′′, its storage modulus and loss modulus are
E′=E0​+E1​1+(ωτ)2(ωτ)2​,E′′=E1​1+(ωτ)2ωτ​.​
(15)
With this time convention the physical stress is ϵ0​[E′cosωt−E′′sinωt] and leads the strain by the loss angle. The dissipated energy per unit volume per cycle is πE′′ϵ02​≥0. Thus E′ describes elastic storage and E′′ describes dissipation.
At low frequency, ωτ≪1, the dashpot flows during a cycle and relaxes the Maxwell spring, leaving E′→E0​, E′′∼E1​ωτ. At high frequency, ωτ≫1, the dashpot hardly deforms within a cycle, so both springs carry elastic load: E′→E0​+E1​, while E′′∼E1​/(ωτ). The loss modulus is maximal at ωτ=1, with peak value E1​/2.
The equivalent constitutive equation is
σ+τσ˙=E0​ϵ+τ(E0​+E1​)ϵ˙.
(16)
After a sudden strain ϵs​ is imposed and held, the stress relaxes as σ(t)=ϵs​[E0​+E1​e−t/τ]. It falls from the instantaneous modulus E0​+E1​ to the nonzero relaxed modulus E0​. This single-time relaxation is the simplest improvement over a purely elastic solid or a Maxwell fluid with zero long-time modulus.
The loss tangent is tanδ=E′′/E′. Its maximum need not coincide with the loss-modulus maximum: the peak frequencies in a standard linear solid give ωτ=E0​/(E0​+E1​)​ for the loss tangent. The model captures one viscoelastic crossover between two finite elastic plateaux, with dissipation concentrated near the relaxation frequency.

Molecular area from the adsorption slope. The supplied Gibbs adsorption equation identifies the surface excess number density Γ=1/Σ through
Γ=−kB​T1​dlncdγ​,Σ=−dγ/dlnckB​T​.
(17)
The logarithm is understood relative to a fixed concentration unit or reference concentration; only concentration ratios enter its differences. The pure-water point c=0 supplies a baseline surface tension, but cannot be included in lnc.
An estimate near the later working concentration 0.055moldm−3 uses its two bracketing points:
dlncdγ​≃ln(0.06/0.05)(45.2−48.5)×10−3​=−1.810×10−2Nm−1.
(18)
Taking room temperature as 298K,
Σ≃1.810×10−2Jm−24.11×10−21J​=2.27×10−19m2≃0.23nm2.​
(19)
This corresponds to about 4.4 adsorbed molecules per nm2. The data are not perfectly linear in lnc, so the inferred molecular area depends somewhat on the concentration range: a fit to all five positive-concentration points gives about 0.26nm2, while the higher-concentration region gives roughly 0.22nm2. An effective area of order 0.2–0.3nm2 is justified; a concentration-independent exact value is not.
Contact angle. Linear interpolation between 0.05 and 0.06moldm−3 gives γLV​(0.055)=(48.5+45.2)/2=46.85mNm−1. For an ideal equilibrium contact angle measured through the liquid, Young's equation is
γSV​−γSL​=γLV​cosθ.
(20)
The supplied solid-vapour and solid-liquid values have difference 40mNm−1. Hence
θ=cos−1(46.85300−260​)=31.4∘.​
(21)
This assumes clean homogeneous equilibrium interfaces, negligible contact-angle hysteresis, and the stated solid interfacial energies applicable to the solution.
Complete wetting. The spreading coefficient is S=γSV​−γSL​−γLV​=40mNm−1−γLV​. The onset of complete wetting occurs at S=0, or γLV​=40mNm−1, if the solid interfacial energies remain unchanged. The last measured value is still 42.0mNm−1, so this requires extrapolation beyond the table.
Continuing the last local slope in concentration, (42.0−45.2)/(0.07−0.06)=−320mNm−1 per moldm−3, gives

A surface-tension plateau caused by micellization, or a change in γSL​, could prevent or shift this threshold; no such extra data are given.

This is the height above the reservoir level in the ideal static capillary approximation; meniscus-volume corrections and contact-angle hysteresis are neglected. The positive cosθ gives a rise, rather than a depression.

Diffuse-interface free energy. A sharp boundary assigns one mathematical surface to two uniform fluids. Molecular mixing instead changes composition over a finite distance. In square-gradient theory of a liquid interface, let ϕ(r) be a dimensionless local composition and write
F[ϕ]=∫[fb​(ϕ,T)+2K​∣∇ϕ∣2]dV,K>0.
(25)
The bulk free-energy density fb​ favors the equilibrium phase compositions, while the gradient term penalizes changing composition too abruptly. Since fb​ has units Jm−3 and ϕ is dimensionless, K has units Jm−1. The theory balances bulk mixing cost against this smoothness cost, rather than imposing a zero-width surface from the start.
At coexistence the two compositions ϕ−​,ϕ+​ have a common-tangent construction for phase coexistence. Subtract this tangent from fb​ to define an excess grand-potential density Δω(ϕ), zero at both bulk compositions. For a planar profile varying along z, minimizing the excess free energy gives
Kϕ′′=dϕdΔω​.
(26)
Multiply by ϕ′ and integrate using ϕ′→0 in the bulk:
2K​(ϕ′)2=Δω(ϕ).​
(27)
It follows that the interfacial surface tension is
γ=∫−∞∞​[Δω+2K​(ϕ′)2]dz=∫−∞∞​K(ϕ′)2dz=∫ϕ−​ϕ+​​2KΔω(ϕ)​dϕ.
(28)
This connects a continuous composition profile to a positive interfacial energy per area.
Broadening near criticality. Close to a symmetric mean-field thermodynamic critical point, an order parameter m measuring departure from the critical composition has bulk density
fb​(m)=2r​m2+4u​m4,r∝T−Tc​<0,u>0.
(29)
The coexistence values are m=±m0​, m0​=∣r∣/u​. The scalar quartic domain wall profile is
m(z)=m0​tanh(z/w),w=∣r∣2K​​.​
(30)
The curvature at either bulk minimum is fb′′​(m0​)=2∣r∣, so the bulk correlation length is ξ=K/(2∣r∣)​ and this convention gives w=2ξ. As T→Tc−​, the bulk distinction between phases weakens, its free-energy curvature and barrier decrease, and composition can vary gradually over an increasing distance at little bulk cost. The gradient penalty then favors a wider profile:
w∝(Tc​−T)−1/2in mean-field theory.​
(31)
The same model gives γ=22K​∣r∣3/2/(3u), which vanishes at criticality. The exponents here are mean-field results, not universal exact values for every real mixture.
The figure uses two illustrative parameter values in this quartic model, not experimental parameters supplied by the paper. In addition to the intrinsic gradient-theory width, thermal capillary waves move and roughen an interface. Their increasing amplitude as surface tension decreases can broaden the spatially averaged measured profile further. Thus a measured broad interface can include both intrinsic composition smoothing and long-wavelength interface-position fluctuations.

Meaning of the mixing free energy. The supplied expression is the Flory–Huggins solution theory mixing free energy per lattice site, or per solvent-sized reference cell. Although the question calls it “per particle,” it cannot mean per polymer molecule: one chain occupies N sites. Let the site volume be v0​, the total number of sites be M=V/v0​, and the chain and solvent counts be np​,ns​, so
M=Nnp​+ns​,ϕ=MNnp​​,Fmix​=MkB​Tg(ϕ),
(32)
where
g(ϕ)=Nϕ​lnϕ+(1−ϕ)ln(1−ϕ)+χϕ(1−ϕ).
(33)
The first term is the polymer's translational entropy of mixing. Its factor 1/N expresses that N connected segments translate as one molecule. The second is the solvent's translational mixing entropy. Both are negative in the mixing free energy for 0<ϕ<1, favoring mixing.
The final term is a mean-field excess contact contribution. The Flory interaction parameter χ measures the relative preference for unlike polymer-solvent contacts compared with like contacts. In the simplest lattice model with coordination number z and pair energies ϵps​,ϵpp​,ϵss​,
χ=kB​Tz​[ϵps​−2ϵpp​+ϵss​​].
(34)
Positive χ penalizes unlike contacts and promotes separation; negative χ favors them. Effective real-solution values can also include non-enthalpic effects, so their temperature dependence need not be exactly a constant divided by T. The subsequent derivatives assume composition-independent χ as in the supplied model.
Osmotic pressure and the special value one half. At fixed chain count, ∂ϕ/∂V=−ϕ/V. Differentiating Fmix​=(V/v0​)kB​Tg(ϕ) with respect to volume, relative to the pure-solvent reference, gives
π=v0​kB​T​[ϕg′(ϕ)−g(ϕ)].
(35)
The resulting osmotic pressure in Flory-Huggins solution theory is
kB​Tπv0​​=−ln(1−ϕ)−(1−N1​)ϕ−χϕ2=Nϕ​+(21​−χ)ϕ2+3ϕ3​+O(ϕ4).​
(36)
The leading term is the ideal osmotic pressure of chains, since their number density is ϕ/(Nv0​). At χ=1/2 the second-virial term vanishes: effective two-segment attraction and excluded-volume repulsion balance. This is the theta solvent condition for a sufficiently long dilute chain; the positive third-order contribution remains, so a finite-concentration solution is not a completely noninteracting gas. The site-volume factor is necessary to give physical pressure units.
For χ<1/2, the effective pair excluded volume is positive. In a good solvent, the isolated chain expands relative to a Gaussian coil, with R∼bNν, ν≃0.588 or 3/5 in the Flory approximation. For χ>1/2, attraction wins in a poor solvent and a sufficiently long chain collapses toward a dense polymer globule, with asymptotic size R∝N1/3. Close to the theta condition, finite-chain crossover effects matter. At χ=1/2, R∼bN1/2 to leading scaling order. This theta criterion is not the exact finite-N phase-separation critical point; the lattice model has ϕc​=1/(1+N​) and χc​=(1+1/N​)2/2.
Overlap concentration. The polymer overlap concentration is reached when there is roughly one isolated coil in its own pervaded volume. If the coil size is R and a repeat unit occupies volume v0​,
ϕ∗∼R3Nv0​​.
(37)
With R∼bNν, the general scaling law is
ϕ∗∼b3v0​​N1−3ν.​
(38)
The usual single-segment-volume estimate v0​∼b3 gives ϕ∗∼N1−3ν: N−1/2 in a theta solvent, approximately N−4/5 in a good solvent, and order one for compact globules. A coil has no sharp surface, so choosing a radius of gyration or a geometric coil volume changes numerical prefactors.
For the stated polystyrene at N=1500 and χ=1/2, the conventional scaling estimate is
ϕ∗∼N−1/2=1500−1/2=0.0258≃2.6%.​
(39)
This is a volume-fraction estimate, not a molar concentration. It uses the implicit site-volume approximation; the question does not provide an independent repeat-unit volume to determine a more precise prefactor.
Semidilute length scale. Beyond overlap, a semidilute polymer solution is characterized locally by a concentration blob size ξ. Suppose one blob contains g statistical units. At the theta condition the subchain is Gaussian, so ξ∼bg​, while matching its internal concentration to the bulk gives ϕ∼gv0​/ξ3. Eliminating g gives the concentration-blob size in a theta solvent:
ξ∼b2ϕv0​​∼ϕb​when v0​∼b3.​
(40)
Using the supplied statistical step length b=0.67nm and ϕ=0.05,
ξ∼0.050.67​nm=13.4nm≃13nm.​
(41)
The same convention gives g∼ϕ−2=400<N=1500, and the whole ideal chain has R∼bN​≃26nm. Thus the blob is smaller than the chain. Because ϕ/ϕ∗≃1.94 is only modestly above overlap, the value is a scaling estimate in a crossover region, not a precision asymptotic measurement. A statistical step length is not automatically a physical monomer diameter: if v0​ differs from b3, the more general estimates are ϕ∗∼(v0​/b3)N−1/2 and ξ∼v0​/(b2ϕ), with the missing prefactor left explicit.
