An amphiphile has a solvent-affine part and a solvent-averse part within one molecule. In water these are commonly called hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tail. Two nonbiological examples are sodium dodecyl sulfate, with a twelve-carbon tail and an anionic sulfate head, and cetyltrimethylammonium bromide, with a sixteen-carbon tail and a cationic quaternary-ammonium head. Their counterions balance the ionic head-group charge; the head and tail do not have interchangeable interactions with water.
Aggregation reduces unfavorable exposure of hydrocarbon tails to water but loses molecular translational entropy. Interfacial free energy, head-group solvation and electrostatic repulsion, chain packing and conformational entropy determine the preferred aggregate. The hydrophobic effect is a solvent-mediated free-energy balance, rather than a literal attractive bond between tails.
The amphiphile packing parameter
P=a0​ℓc​v​
(13)
compares tail volume v, effective head-group area a0​ and maximum tail length ℓc​. A spherical core of radius R obeys v/a0​=R/3, and a long cylindrical core obeys v/a0​=R/2. The condition R≤ℓc​ gives the conventional spherical window P≲1/3 and cylindrical window 1/3≲P≲1/2. Less strongly curved bilayers become compatible for 1/2≲P≲1, with planar packing near one; values above one can favor inverse curvature in an appropriate nonpolar surrounding medium. These windows are geometric guides rather than exact phase boundaries.
A larger hydrated or electrostatically repelling head increases a0​ and favors positive curvature. Screening of charged heads or a smaller head can lower a0​ and favor rods or sheets. Multiple tails increase the tail volume relative to head area and can favor bilayers. Tail length, branching, temperature and solvent selectivity alter both geometry and interaction free energy, so one cannot predict a unique aggregate from hydrophobicity alone.[ref]
