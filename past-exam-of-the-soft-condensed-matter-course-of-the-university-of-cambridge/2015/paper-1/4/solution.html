In a regular solution, ideal combinatorial entropy competes with a nonzero unlike-contact energy. For equal molecular volumes and mole fraction ϕ of species A, write mixing free energy per site as
kB​Tf(ϕ)​=ϕlnϕ+(1−ϕ)ln(1−ϕ)+χϕ(1−ϕ),
(20)
where
χ=kB​Tz​[ϵAB​−2ϵAA​+ϵBB​​].
(21)
Here z is the coordination number and ϵij​ a pair-contact energy counted consistently in the lattice model. A positive χ penalizes unlike contacts; a negative value favors them. The logarithmic terms lower free energy on mixing. The sign of the enthalpy of mixing alone therefore does not determine miscibility.
At fixed temperature and composition, a homogeneous phase is globally stable only if no division into two compositions lowers its free energy. The common-tangent construction selects coexisting compositions ϕa​,ϕb​, equivalent to equality of both species' chemical potentials. Local stability to infinitesimal fluctuations instead requires
kB​Tf′′​=ϕ1​+1−ϕ1​−2χ>0.
(22)
Zero curvature gives the spinodal, while the coexistence boundary is the binodal. Between them a homogeneous state can be metastable and require nucleation; inside the spinodal its small composition fluctuations grow. The symmetric regular solution has
ϕc​=21​,χc​=2.​
(23)
Below this contact penalty, the free energy is convex at every composition. Above it a miscibility gap is possible around the central composition. If χ=A/T with A>0, heating promotes mixing, but specific interactions or temperature-dependent noncombinatorial terms can produce other trends.
For two polymers, let NA​,NB​ count equal-volume segments per chain, and now let ϕ be the segment volume fraction of A. Connecting segments into chains reduces their independent translational entropy. The binary Flory-Huggins polymer blend has
kB​Tf​=NA​ϕ​lnϕ+NB​1−ϕ​ln(1−ϕ)+χϕ(1−ϕ).
(24)
The energetic contact term remains extensive in the number of segments, while the favorable entropy per segment is reduced by 1/NA​ and 1/NB​. This is the key long-chain limit of polymer-blend miscibility: a small positive incompatibility can overcome the mixing entropy. The lattice treatment is a controlled model for this argument, rather than a claim that every real blend has composition-independent χ.[ref]
The local stability condition and critical interaction parameter become
NA​ϕ1​+NB​(1−ϕ)1​−2χ>0,χc​=21​(NA−1/2​+NB−1/2​)2.​
(25)
For equal chain lengths, χc​=2/N. Similar segment chemistry and matched cohesive interactions can reduce the unlike-contact penalty; favorable specific interactions, such as complementary hydrogen bonding, can make mixing favorable even for long chains. Lower molar mass increases mixing entropy. temperature can help when it lowers the effective χ, but heating is not universally sufficient. A suitable common solvent can facilitate processing or ternary mixing, and a block-copolymer compatibilizer can stabilize fine domains; neither guarantees that the final solvent-free binary material is one equilibrium molecular phase.
An oil–water emulsion illustrates emulsion stability versus molecular miscibility. A surfactant adsorbs with its water-affine head toward water and its oil-affine tail toward oil, lowering interfacial tension and the work γdA needed to make additional interface. Breaking a fixed oil volume Vo​ into spherical droplets of radius R gives total area 3Vo​/R, so creating small droplets still requires substantial area and usually mechanical work.
Adsorbed surfactants can oppose droplet coalescence through charged double-layer repulsion, hydrated-head interactions or steric repulsion from sufficiently extended groups. Adequate coverage, adsorption time, ionic conditions and film drainage affect the lifetime. A lower interfacial tension does not itself remove the bulk miscibility gap, and a protected emulsion is commonly kinetically stabilized. A thermodynamically stable microemulsion is a distinct situation requiring an appropriate composition and free-energy balance. The two liquids must not be called molecularly miscible simply because droplets disperse visibly.
For the capillary, balance upward contact-line force against hydrostatic column weight, neglecting meniscus volume and gas density. For inner radius r,
2πrγcosθ=ρgπr2h,h=ρgr2γcosθ​.
(26)
The contact angle is measured through the liquid. complete wetting means θ2​=0, so the contact-angle change of capillary rise gives
h2​=cos35∘0.050​=0.06104m≃6.10cm.​
(27)
This follows from the stipulated wetting change, holding radius, bulk liquid surface tension and density fixed. One should not replace that premise with a general claim that grease always improves glass wetting; if contamination also changes the liquid surface tension, the original height and new angle alone would not determine the new height.
For two wide vertical plates separated by gap d, use unit width. There are two upward contact-line contributions and liquid cross-sectional area d, giving the capillary rise between parallel plates:
2γcosθ=ρgdhplates​,hplates​=ρgd2γcosθ​.​
(28)
Only one principal meniscus curvature contributes between wide plates, whereas a circular tube has two. For the same fluid and angle,
htube​hplates​​=dr​.​
(29)
If the plate gap equals the tube diameter, d=2r, the rise is half as large: the initial and completely wetting heights would be 2.50 cm and 3.05 cm. If instead d=r, the heights are equal. In either geometry, changing only the angle from 35∘ to zero multiplies the height by 1/cos35∘. Since the question specifies no plate spacing, the general gap-dependent relation is the unambiguous result; the half-height answer requires the equal-gap-to-diameter convention.
