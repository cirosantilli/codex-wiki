The Reynolds number compares inertial and viscous terms in the momentum equation:
Re=ηρUL​.​
(10)
Here ρ is fluid mass density, U a characteristic speed, L a swimmer or flow length, and η the dynamic viscosity. The advective inertia scale is ρU2/L and the viscous force-density scale is ηU/L2, whose ratio is Re. For an illustrative micron-scale swimmer with L=2μm and U=20μms−1 in water, taking ρ=103kgm−3 and η=10−3Pas gives Re∼4×10−5.
At such low Re, Stokes flow is the useful approximation. For an incompressible Newtonian fluid it obeys −∇p+η∇2u=0 and ∇⋅u=0, with negligible inertia. Swimming is not a sequence of propulsive impulses followed by coasting: drag balances propulsive forces almost instantaneously. For example, the drag on a slowly translating sphere is 6πηaU. A freely swimming organism has approximately zero total external force and torque; its body motion balances the forces and torques generated by its appendages.
The linear equations have kinematic reversibility of Stokes flow: reversing the boundary velocities reverses the fluid motion. A reciprocal stroke that retraces exactly the same sequence of shapes therefore retraces its displacement. This is the scallop theorem. A single hinge opening and closing along the same shape path cannot propel an ideal swimmer through an otherwise quiescent Newtonian Stokes fluid, regardless of merely changing how quickly it opens relative to closing.
A useful design has at least two shape coordinates driven out of phase, so the stroke follows a loop rather than a retraced line in shape space. In the three-bead example the two link lengths change in a cycle; hydrodynamic interactions differ at different points of the cycle, allowing net translation. The drawing illustrates this mechanism without assigning a particular swimming speed.
Biological routes include continuous rotation of a chiral helical flagellum, nonreciprocal ciliary strokes, and traveling bending waves along a flagellum. An elongated filament generally has greater drag for motion normal to itself than along itself; this drag anisotropy helps convert those motions into propulsion. The body may counterrotate to balance appendage torque. Micro-organisms must exploit nonreciprocal shape changes, rotation or another mechanism consistent with viscous force balance; inertia-based swimming intuition fails. Viscoelastic surroundings or appreciable inertia can invalidate the particular Newtonian assumptions behind the simple scallop result.
