In the weak-field reptation regime of agarose gel electrophoresis, the electrophoretic mobility of a long linear DNA chain scales as μ∝M−1, where M is its number of base pairs. One way to see the scaling is that the constrained-chain diffusion coefficient scales as D∝M−2, whereas its effective electric charge scales as M; the drift response is proportional to DM. With the same electric field and running time, the migration distance is therefore x=μEt∝M−1. The time cancels from the ratio, giving
M1​M2​​=x2​x1​​,M2​=104.54​kbp≃8.9kbp.​
(1)
Here “kbp” means a thousand base pairs, not a rate. The second gel band has traveled farther and corresponds to the shorter chain under this migration model. The inverse-length law applies only over an appropriate migration regime.[ref].
At very low agarose concentration the mesh becomes too open to sieve these chains effectively. In the free-solution limit the chain's electric charge and friction are both approximately proportional to its length, so both chains have nearly the same electrophoretic mobility and their gel bands cease to separate well. At very high agarose concentration the pores become small and transport is strongly hindered; over a fixed experimental time both chains migrate very little, so their absolute spatial separation again becomes small. Increasing gel concentration can improve resolution over an intermediate range, but not indefinitely.
The numerical result is conditional on the inverse-length reptation law. Actual gel electrophoresis size calibration often uses an empirical approximately linear relation between distance and logM over a specified range. One known band alone cannot fix that relation's slope. The PDF supplies neither gel concentration nor a calibration curve, so 8.9kbp is the standard model estimate rather than a uniquely calibrated experimental size.

Choose a coordinate z measured from the sphere centre towards increasing distance from the block, so an atom in the sphere is at height L+z. Summing the atom–block potential energy with the sphere's number density gives
U(L)=−6Bπn2​∫∣x∣<R​(L+z)3d3x​.
(2)
For R/L≪1, a uniformly valid Taylor series over the sphere is
(L+z)31​=L31​[1−L3z​+L26z2​−L310z3​+O(L4z4​)].
(3)
The odd moments vanish by reflection symmetry. The sphere volume is Vs​=4πR3/3, and its second moment is
∫z2d3x=π∫−RR​z2(R2−z2)dz=154πR5​=5Vs​R2​.
(4)
Consequently
U(L)=−9L32π2Bn2R3​[1+56​L2R2​+O(L4R4​)].​
(5)
The first finite-radius correction is therefore −4π2Bn2R5/(15L5). There is no relative R/L correction because the first moment about the centre is zero. The leading term is just the number of sphere atoms times the potential at its centre; the correction increases the attraction because the nearer atoms contribute more strongly than the farther atoms lose.
This is the far-field sphere-plane dispersion energy, with Hamaker constant AH​=π2Bn2. It must be distinguished from the near-contact sphere-plane dispersion force, whose small parameter is the surface gap divided by the radius. Here L is explicitly the centre-to-plane distance.

a OurBigBook.com
Use a rheometer, for example a cone-and-plate rheometer or a parallel-plate rheometer, to impose a small deformation and measure the required torque, or impose a torque and measure the deformation. The apparatus geometry converts these into shear stress and strain. First check that reducing the imposed amplitude does not change the normalized response; this identifies the linear regime and avoids yielding or structural rearrangement.
Three complementary protocols characterize the material's memory. In a stress relaxation experiment impose a small step ϵ0​H(t) and hold the deformation fixed. The output is the relaxation modulus G(t)=σ(t)/ϵ0​, revealing how stored stress decays and whether an elastic plateau remains. In a creep compliance measurement impose a small stress step σ0​H(t) and record J(t)=ϵ(t)/σ0​. Removing the load also distinguishes recoverable deformation from permanent flow.
In small-amplitude oscillatory shear, impose sinusoidal strain over a range of frequencies and measure both stress amplitude and phase. With ϵ=ϵ0​cosωt, fit
σ(t)=ϵ0​[G′(ω)cosωt−G′′(ω)sinωt].
(11)
The outputs are the storage modulus G′ and loss modulus G′′, or equivalently amplitude ratio and loss tangent tanδ=G′′/G′. These measure elastic storage and dissipative response, respectively. The step experiments give time-domain information and the oscillatory experiment gives the corresponding frequency-domain response. All comparisons must use the same deformation convention and controlled temperature.

The dynamic modulus, or complex viscoelastic modulus, is the ratio of complex stress amplitude to complex strain amplitude in the periodic steady response:
σ(t)=ℜ[σeiωt],ϵ(t)=ℜ[ϵeiωt],G∗(ω)=ϵσ​=G′+iG′′.​
(12)
This defines the sign convention; for passive response the loss modulus is nonnegative at positive frequency. A Newtonian fluid obeys σ=ηϵ˙, while a Hooke's law solid obeys σ=G0​ϵ. Hence
GNewtonian∗​=iωη,GHookean∗​=G0​.​
(13)
The first is purely dissipative and the second purely elastic. Here ϵ denotes the scalar deformation used in the problem, with any shear-versus-extension geometric factors already included in its modulus and viscosity.

Parallel elements have the same strain, while their stresses add. The spring supplies G0​ϵ and the viscous element supplies η0​ϵ˙, so the Kelvin-Voigt model has constitutive equation
σ(t)=G0​ϵ(t)+η0​ϵ˙(t).​
(14)
For the oscillatory deformation this gives
σ(t)=(G0​+iωη0​)ϵ0​eiωt,G∗(ω)=G0​+iωη0​.​
(15)
Physical stress and strain are the real parts of these expressions. Thus the storage modulus is G0​, independent of frequency, and the loss modulus is ωη0​. The equation is the quasistatic material law; an inertial equation for an attached mass would require additional mechanical data.

For elements in series the stress is common and the strains add. If ϵK​ and ϵ1​ are the Kelvin element and added spring amplitudes, then
ϵ=ϵK​+ϵ1​=G0​+iωη0​σ​+G1​σ​.

G∗(ω)=[G0​+iωη0​1​+G1​1​]−1=G0​+G1​+iωη0​G1​(G0​+iωη0​)​.​

GL​=G0​+G1​G0​G1​​,GH​=G1​,ΔG=GH​−GL​=G0​+G1​G12​​,τ=G0​+G1​η0​​.

G′=GL​+ΔG1+x2x2​,G′′=ΔG1+x2x​.​
(19)
Thus G′ increases from the low-frequency plateau GL​ to the high-frequency plateau GH​. The loss modulus has low-frequency slope +1 and high-frequency slope −1 on a log-log plot, with maximum ΔG/2 at ω=1/τ. At that frequency G′=(GL​+GH​)/2. There need not be a crossing of the two curves for arbitrary spring constants.

A Kelvin-Voigt model has no finite instantaneous elastic compliance: the viscous element prevents a sudden deformation under a finite stress step. Its creep compliance is
JK​(t)=G0​1​(1−e−G0​t/η0​),JK​(0+)=0.
(20)
Conversely an ideal strain step requires an impulsive stress,
σ(t)=ϵ0​[G0​H(t)+η0​δ(t)].
(21)
After the impulse the held-strain response is simply constant. There is no finite stress-decay interval, and the loss modulus grows without bound as ω→∞. This fails to represent a material with both a finite instantaneous stiffness and delayed stress relaxation.
The added series spring resolves this particular defect. At the instant of loading the Kelvin element cannot move but the added spring can. The standard linear solid model consequently has
G(t)=GL​+(GH​−GL​)e−t/τ,t≥0,​
(22)
and
J(t)=G1​1​+G0​1​(1−e−G0​t/η0​).​
(23)
Its instantaneous stiffness is GH​=G1​, its long-time stiffness is GL​, and it exhibits genuine stress relaxation as well as delayed creep. The creep retardation time η0​/G0​ differs from the stress-relaxation time τ=η0​/(G0​+G1​). These properties also follow directly from its constitutive equation,
η0​σ˙+(G0​+G1​)σ=G1​η0​ϵ˙+G0​G1​ϵ.
(24)
It remains an idealized solid with one relaxation mode: it cannot describe unrestricted long-time viscous flow, nonlinear response, or a broad spectrum of relaxation times.

a OurBigBook.com
A hydrophobic surface is poorly wetted by water: on a smooth surface its equilibrium contact angle, measured through the liquid, exceeds 90∘. A drop therefore tends to bead up, limiting its contact area. Hydrophobicity does not mean that the surface exerts a mysterious repulsive force on every water molecule.
The waxy coating has a largely nonpolar exterior. Water molecules form favorable hydrogen bonds with one another, whereas a hydrocarbon-rich wax surface offers few suitable hydrogen bond donors or hydrogen bond acceptors. Water–surface adhesion is weak relative to water's cohesion. The drop shape minimizes the combined solid–air, solid–water and water–air interfacial free energies, not merely the liquid's exposed area.
In terms of Young's equation, γSV​−γSL​=γLV​cosθ. A contact angle greater than 90∘ corresponds to γSL​>γSV​: replacing dry surface by wetted surface has an interfacial cost. The balancing liquid interface then favors a small wetted patch. Microscopic leaf texture can amplify the apparent hydrophobicity, especially when it traps air under the drop as a Cassie–Baxter state; the chemistry of a smooth wax layer alone does not guarantee an extremely large apparent angle.

On Teflon, the water drop has an obtuse contact angle and beads up, whereas the oil drop has an acute contact angle and spreads into a shallower patch. For small drops with negligible Bond number, both can be drawn as spherical caps.
To compare shape at equal volume V, let R denote the generating sphere radius. The cap height and footprint radius are
h=R(1−cosθ),s=Rsinθ,V=3π​R3(2−3cosθ+cos3θ).
(25)
For a fixed V, these relations give a taller, narrower drop for the larger contact angle. The angles are measured inside the liquid, including the obtuse angle of the water drop.

γSA​=γSL​+γLA​cosθ,​

Colloidal particles experience attractive Van der Waals forces, arising from correlated molecular charge fluctuations and other dipolar interactions. Integrating many weak molecular attractions over two particles can give a deep near-contact well. For identical spheres of radius a and surface gap h≪a, a positive Hamaker constant gives approximately
UvdW​(h)≃−12hAH​a​.
(29)
Brownian motion brings particles into encounters. Without a repulsive barrier, they can reach the attractive primary minimum; if the binding energy is much larger than kB​T, thermal motion rarely separates them again. Repeated sticking produces aggregates. This is a kinetic stability statement: thermal agitation causes encounters but does not itself supply a stabilizing interparticle repulsion. The molecular cutoff and hard sphere exclusion prevent the unphysical h→0 divergence of the continuum attraction.

One method is to give every particle a sufficiently large surface charge density of the same sign. For example, attach ionizable sulfonate groups covalently to the surface. Dissociation leaves negatively charged surface groups and releases mobile positive counterions into the water. A surfactant with a strongly bound head group can provide another charging route, provided it does not desorb during the experiment.
Each charged particle is surrounded by an electric double layer. As two such particles approach, overlap of these ionic clouds raises the electrostatic and ionic free energy, producing repulsion. In a dilute electrolyte, the range is set by the Debye–Hückel screening length,
λD​=κ−1,κ2=ε0​εr​kB​Te2​∑i​zi2​ni​.
(30)
For identical weakly charged spheres a representative screened Coulomb pair potential is
Uel​(R)≃4πε0​εr​(1+κa)2RQ2​e−κ(R−2a),R≥2a.
(31)
Its sum with the Van der Waals force attraction is the DLVO theory pair potential. Choose the charging level so that the repulsive barrier is many kB​T high; particles then rarely reach the primary attractive well during the observation time. This is kinetic stabilization rather than removal of all attraction.
Deionized water is useful because low added ionic strength gives a long-ranged repulsion. It does not mean that the charged dispersion contains no ions: the surface's own counterions are unavoidable and contribute to screening. Surface dissociation, contamination and particle concentration therefore matter even without added salt. An alternative valid route is a dense, firmly attached layer of water-soluble polyethylene glycol, producing steric stabilization without relying on a large electrostatic barrier.

Without stabilization, the potential energy is predominantly attractive outside the molecular core and drops into a deep primary minimum. With the charging treatment, the DLVO theory sum develops a positive barrier between distant particles and that primary minimum. The barrier must be large compared with kB​T to inhibit aggregation on the experimental timescale. A shallow secondary minimum can remain at larger separation, depending on parameters.
The horizontal axis is the surface gap, so the molecular exclusion boundary is at its origin. Both interactions tend to zero at large separation. Raising salt concentration shortens the Debye–Hückel screening length and can reduce the barrier, allowing particles to stick again.

The Flory–Huggins solution theory treats a polymer solution as an incompressible lattice mixture, with one solvent molecule occupying one site and one chain occupying N connected sites. Thus the supplied f is naturally the mixing free energy per lattice site; if a site has volume v0​, the free-energy density is f/v0​.
The term kB​T(ϕ/N)lnϕ is the polymer translational entropy of mixing contribution. There are only 1/N as many independently translating chains as monomers, which explains the factor 1/N. The term kB​T(1−ϕ)ln(1−ϕ) is the solvent entropy of mixing contribution. Both lower the mixing free energy for 0<ϕ<1.
The term kB​Tχϕ(1−ϕ) is the mean-field excess interaction contribution from replacing like contacts with polymer–solvent contacts. In the simplest energetic lattice model,
χ=kB​Tz​[ϵps​−2ϵpp​+ϵss​​],
(32)
where z counts neighboring contacts and the ϵij​ are pair contact energies. Positive χ penalizes unlike contacts and can favor demixing. Because chain connectivity suppresses the polymer's translational mixing entropy, relatively modest unfavorable interactions can cause separation of long chains. More general effective χ can also include entropic effects.

Local compositional stability requires positive curvature of the mixing free energy. Differentiating twice at fixed temperature, with χ independent of composition as in the given model, gives
kB​T1​∂ϕ2∂2f​=Nϕ1​+1−ϕ1​−2χ.
(33)
The spinodal occurs where this vanishes:
χs​(ϕ)=21​[Nϕ1​+1−ϕ1​].​
(34)
Between the two spinodal compositions at sufficiently large χ, f′′<0 and a homogeneous mixture is unstable to infinitesimal composition fluctuations. The binodal is instead fixed by the common-tangent construction, and encloses a larger coexistence region. Between binodal and spinodal the mixture is locally stable but metastable.
A phase diagram with a conventional upper critical temperature additionally assumes that χ decreases with T, for example χ=C/T with C>0. Then high temperature gives a uniform mixture; cooling produces an asymmetric coexistence dome whose critical composition is on the dilute-polymer side. The spinodal lies inside the binodal dome. An arbitrary χ(T) need not give this orientation, so the free-energy formula alone does not specify a unique temperature axis.

At the thermodynamic critical point, the two spinodal branches merge: f′′=0 and f(3)=0. The third derivative is
kB​Tf(3)​=−Nϕ21​+(1−ϕ)21​.
(35)
For 0<ϕ<1, its zero gives 1−ϕc​=N​ϕc​. Substitution in the spinodal relation yields
ϕc​=1+N​1​∼N−1/2,χc​=21​(1+N​1​)2=21​+N−1/2+2N1​.​
(36)
An absolute critical temperature requires an interaction–temperature relation. Under the usual energetic approximation χ=C/T, define the theta temperature Θ=2C, at which an infinite chain has χ=1/2. Then
Tc​=(1+N−1/2)2Θ​=Θ[1−2N−1/2+3N−1+O(N−3/2)].​
(37)
Thus Tc​ tends to a nonzero constant, Tc​∼N0, while Θ−Tc​∼N−1/2. It is the temperature deficit, not Tc​ itself, that scales as N−1/2.
More generally, if a smooth χ(T) obeys χ(Θ)=1/2 and χ′(Θ)=0, then
Tc​−Θ=χ′(Θ)N−1/2​+O(N−1).
(38)
This is the critical point of a Flory–Huggins polymer solution. The same leading chain-length exponent follows for a smooth temperature law, while its coefficient depends on the omitted solvent thermodynamics. The lattice interpretation connects the chain-length dependence of mixing entropy to the theta condition χ=1/2.[ref].

Interpret the stated transition as an upper critical miscibility temperature: cooling increases the effective χ through χc​. Above Tc​, the homogeneous mixing free energy is convex throughout 0<ϕ<1, with no two-phase common tangent. Below Tc​, it acquires a region with f′′<0, bordered by the spinodal. A lower straight line touches the curve at two binodal compositions ϕ−​ and ϕ+​; this common-tangent construction gives the equilibrium polymer-poor and polymer-rich phases.
The precise raw curve need not have two equal minima: subtracting an affine function of ϕ changes reference chemical potentials without changing curvature or coexistence compositions. The free-energy panels above subtract the tangent at ϕc​ from the upper-temperature curve and the common tangent from the lower-temperature curve. This makes the common tangent and the convex-versus-nonconvex distinction visible.
If the mean initial composition ϕ0​ lies inside the low-temperature spinodal region, an arbitrarily small separation into equal-volume regions with compositions ϕ0​±δϕ changes the mean free energy by
2f(ϕ0​+δϕ)+f(ϕ0​−δϕ)​−f(ϕ0​)=21​f′′(ϕ0​)(δϕ)2+O((δϕ)4)<0.
(43)
Small fluctuations therefore grow spontaneously: this is spinodal decomposition. Interfacial costs limit very short-wavelength fluctuations, and the polymer-poor and polymer-rich domains subsequently coarsen towards their binodal compositions.
If ϕ0​ lies just outside a spinodal branch but still inside the binodal, then f′′(ϕ0​)>0. Small fluctuations increase free energy, so the uniform mixture is metastable. Separation requires a sufficiently large nucleus to overcome the interfacial barrier, followed by nucleation and growth. There is no guarantee that this happens rapidly. If the initial composition is outside the binodal as well, it remains a stable uniform phase after cooling.
For a mean composition in the coexistence interval, conservation of polymer fixes the volume fraction α of the rich phase by the lever rule:
α=ϕ+​−ϕ−​ϕ0​−ϕ−​​,1−α=ϕ+​−ϕ−​ϕ+​−ϕ0​​.​
(44)
The temperatures alone do not determine which mechanism occurs without the initial composition. Numerical free-energy curves additionally require χ(T); the plotted example uses χ(T)=χc​Tc​/T with absolute temperatures Tc​=298.15K, T1​=303.15K and T2​=293.15K.
