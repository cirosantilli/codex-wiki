Treat the liposome using the usual incompressible-tail packing model of a lipid bilayer: neglect the head-group thickness and let v be the volume filling the hydrophobic part of the lipid vesicle. Both membrane leaflets have area a per head group. Put d=v/a, and let R be the unknown outer radius. Counting molecules from the inner and outer surface areas gives
Mi​=a4πri2​​,Mo​=a4πR2​.
(7)
Equating the total molecular volume to the volume of the spherical shell therefore gives
34π​(R3−ri3​)=v(Mi​+Mo​),R3−ri3​=3d(R2+ri2​).​
(8)
To prove uniqueness in the physical domain R>ri​>0, define
q(R)=R2+ri2​R3−ri3​​.
(9)
Its derivative is
q′(R)=(R2+ri2​)2R4+3R2ri2​+2Rri3​​>0.
(10)
Also q(ri​)=0 and q(R)→∞ as R→∞. By continuity and strict monotonicity, q(R)=3d has exactly one solution with R>ri​. This proves the required uniqueness without choosing among ambiguous branches of a cubic formula.
The maximum tail length is a physical admissibility bound, not automatically the actual leaflet thickness. If rm​ is the radius where the two tail regions meet, separate membrane leaflet volume balances give

(11)

For the usual interpretation of a real chain in a good solvent, the detachment force is smaller in the weak-adsorption regime. Excluded volume swells the chain, increasing the cost of confinement near the substrate. The adsorbed layer becomes thicker and its binding free energy per monomer decreases. The same contact energy therefore does not mean the same total binding strength.
The scaling argument can include both adsorption and stretching. Let ξ∼bgν, where ν=1/2 for an ideal chain and approximately 3/5 for a self-avoiding walk in three dimensions. With the same contact-fraction approximation,
NkB​TFads​​∼(ξb​)1/ν−ϵξb​.
(24)
Minimization gives ξ/b∼ϵ−ν/(1−ν) and μ/(kB​T)∼ϵ1/(1−ν). At detachment, a tensile blob of size ξt​∼kB​T/f costs approximately kB​T; its monomer count is gt​∼(ξt​/b)1/ν. Equating the stretching gain per monomer kB​T/gt​ to μ gives
f∼bkB​T​(kB​Tμ​)ν∼bkB​T​ϵν/(1−ν).
(25)
Thus freal​∼(kB​T/b)ϵ3/2, compared with fideal​∼(kB​T/b)ϵ, so their ratio scales as ϵ1/2<1 for weak attraction. These are scaling relations, not exact prefactors. The real-chain adsorption exponents here use the same simple uniform-contact model as the earlier parts; a more detailed surface theory of critical phenomena can refine them. The thicker, more weakly bound layer reflects the higher cost of confining a good solvent chain.[ref]
“Real” alone does not specify solvent quality: a collapsed chain in a poor solvent, strong adsorption, or substantial bending stiffness requires a different comparison. The smaller-force conclusion concerns the flexible good solvent chain implicit in the excluded-volume correction.

The gap is not a fixed mechanical separation. Thermal fluctuations of the wall compete with drift towards the microtubule under the opposing force. A monomer can be incorporated only after the gap reaches its length. The Smoluchowski equation describes this overdamped drift and diffusion of gap probability density.
For gap potential energy U(x)=Fx, the equation and its probability current are
∂t​p=−∂x​J,J=−D(∂x​p+βU′(x)p)=−D(px​+βFp),β=(kB​T)−1.
(26)
The drift mobility is Dβ, the overdamped consequence of the fluctuation-dissipation relation for a Langevin particle. Equivalently, this coefficient is fixed by requiring the zero-current solution to be the Boltzmann distribution p∝e−βU. Solve this equation with the appropriate exclusion, incorporation and probability-normalization conditions. The steady flux through the incorporation threshold gives the addition rate j0​; the elongation speed is v=δj0​.
There is a regime distinction in the printed problem. The absorbing boundary imposed in part (c) means instantaneous incorporation once a gap opens, and leads to the diffusion-limited Brownian ratchet with constant load. A genuinely reaction-limited polymerization Brownian ratchet instead assumes fast gap equilibration followed by a finite chemical addition rate, giving an exponential gating factor multiplying that chemical rate. The equations requested in the subsequent parts specify the former model, which is the one solved here.

In a magnesium chloride chemical solution, the principal pair interactions are hard sphere exclusion, repulsion of overlapping electric double layers, and attractive Van der Waals forces. The positive surface attracts chloride counterions and repels magnesium coions; mobile ions screen the bare Coulomb's law interaction. The competition between screened electrostatic repulsion and Van der Waals force attraction is described by DLVO theory.
For well-separated identical weakly charged spheres, the approximate repulsive pair potential energy, at centre separation R≥2a, is
Uel​(R)≃4πε0​εr​Q2​(1+κa)2Re−κ(R−2a)​.
(43)
It is a screened Coulomb pair potential. An attractive Hamaker constant adds a negative short-range contribution, while overlap is forbidden. Very near contact, solvation and molecular surface structure can also matter. No polymer-induced interaction should be assumed before polymer is added in part (f).

Write the particle volume fraction as η=(4πa3/3)np​. In a stable, repulsion-dominated colloid, low η gives a disordered fluid. Increasing η makes the interaction between neighboring particles important and can produce an ordered crystal, separated from the fluid by a phase coexistence curve. Increasing the Debye–Hückel screening length at fixed charge strengthens the repulsion at a given separation and allows freezing at smaller geometrical volume fraction.
The geometric intuition is that particles behave as though they occupy more than their bare diameter: roughly, an effective exclusion diameter deff​ extends out to the separation where Uel​ is of order kB​T. A hard sphere comparison then uses ηeff​∼η[deff​/(2a)]3. This explains the trend; it is not an exact freezing criterion.
At short Debye–Hückel screening length and negligible attraction, the diagram approaches hard sphere fluid–crystal coexistence. For longer-ranged soft repulsion, body-centered cubic lattice and face-centered cubic lattice crystals can both be stable, with the former often appearing towards the softer, lower-density freezing boundary. Screened-Coulomb simulations exhibit both these crystal structures.[ref]
No universal quantitative diagram follows from (η,λD​) alone: the charge, Hamaker constant and surface conditions must also be fixed. If salt suppresses the stabilizing barrier enough for Van der Waals force attraction to dominate, a colloid-poor/colloid-rich separation or aggregation can replace part of this repulsive reference diagram. Dense colloidal glasses or colloidal gels may also be observed after kinetic arrest; they should not be mistaken for additional thermodynamic equilibrium thermodynamic phases.

First hold the surface electric charge fixed and increase the bulk salt concentration. Since κ2∝c, the Debye–Hückel screening length decreases as c−1/2. For surface gap h=R−2a≥0,

Because screening length is already a diagram axis, changing salt moves the state through that diagram rather than independently changing its fixed-charge coexistence curves. If Van der Waals forces become dominant, however, adding salt may instead destabilize the dispersion and cause aggregation. These are distinct physical regimes.
Second increase σ at fixed salt concentration and particle size. The electric charge is Q=4πa2σ, so the pair repulsion increases approximately as σ2 within the Debye–Hückel approximation, while the reservoir screening length remains unchanged. Freezing then becomes possible at lower volume fraction or shorter screening length. At fixed λD​, the coexistence curves shift towards lower η; a previously fluid sample may crystallize.
These statements assume fixed, uniform charge and a salt reservoir dominating the screening. Strong charging requires nonlinear screening and possibly charge regulation. At very low added salt or appreciable particle concentration, particle-released counterions also contribute to ionic strength, so the screening length is no longer independently fixed by the added salt alone.

For dissolved, nonadsorbing polyethylene glycol, the usual new interaction is a depletion force. Polymer centres are excluded from a layer of thickness of order their radius of gyration Rg​ around each particle. When the particles approach, their exclusion layers overlap. This increases the volume accessible to polymer and its translational entropy, lowering the colloid pair free energy. Equivalently the surrounding polymer osmotic pressure pushes the particles together.
In the dilute-polymer Asakura-Oosawa model, let h=R−2a be the surface gap and Re​=a+Rg​. Then

for 2a≤R<2Re​, and Udep​=0 for R≥2Re​. Here Πp​≃np(polymer)​kB​T is the reservoir polymer osmotic pressure. The overlap-volume formula follows by adding two equal spherical cap volumes with height Re​−R/2. Thus the added attraction has range about 2Rg​ and grows with polymer concentration. The total interaction is approximately hard exclusion plus screened electrostatic repulsion plus Van der Waals force and depletion attractions.
Increasing polymer concentration therefore creates a family of phase diagrams, rather than a unique shift determined by polymer size alone. Where the attraction overcomes repulsion, colloid-poor fluid can coexist with a colloid-rich phase, and fluid–crystal coexistence generally broadens. For sufficiently long-ranged attraction, stable fluid–fluid separation can occur. For the stipulated small polymer, the attraction is short-ranged: fluid–fluid separation can be metastable to crystallization, and a rapid attraction increase can instead produce an arrested colloidal gel. Residual long-ranged repulsion can oppose bulk separation. Simulations and free-volume calculations confirm that longer-ranged electrostatic repulsion can counteract depletion-driven separation.[ref]
The nonadsorption assumption matters. If the polyethylene glycol adsorbs to the particles, a dense attached layer can give steric stabilization, whereas chains attaching to two particles can produce polymer bridging. The paper does not specify surface affinity or polymer concentration, so depletion is the standard conditional answer, not a prediction of an inevitable particular final phase.
