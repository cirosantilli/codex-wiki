Bit cost of modular exponentiation (O(ℓL2))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Number theory Exponentiation by squaring Modular exponentiation
OurBigBook.com Words: 248
The binary powering multiplication count counts arithmetic operations. The time complexity measured in operations on individual bits also depends on operand lengths. Put L=⌊log2​m⌋+1, the binary length of the modulus, and let ℓ be the positive exponent's binary length. Stored residues use at most L bits, and an unreduced product uses at most 2L bits.
With ordinary long multiplication and division with remainder, one modular product costs O(L2) operations on bits. Combining that cost with O(ℓ) modular products gives
bit cost of the powering loop=O(ℓL2).
(27)
This bound includes reductions in the loop, and assumes the base has already been reduced and the exponent is available as a binary expansion. Reading the exponent takes O(ℓ) additional work; initial reduction of a very long base depends on its input length, and an inverse for a negative exponent adds further work. More generally, if a modular product costs R(L) operations on bits, the powering loop costs O(ℓR(L)). Faster multiplication and reduction methods can improve the quadratic bound.[ref]
For ordinary integer powers with ∣a∣≥2, the output itself has ⌊nlog2​∣a∣⌋+1 binary digits. Even writing it takes work proportional to that length. Consequently O(log(n+1)) multiplications does not imply O(log(n+1)) operations on bits: the operands grow during ordinary exponentiation by squaring, whereas modular exponentiation keeps them bounded by the modulus. A squaring or multiplication by a small fixed base can also cost less than a general product, so equal counts need not mean equal running times.
