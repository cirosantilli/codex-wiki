This excess measures the height of the slope-dependent integrand above its tangent line at the reference slope p, evaluated at a trial slope q. A convex function of slope has nonnegative excess. Weierstrass necessary condition (E(x,u,u′,v)≥0)

Words: 173 Articles: 1
Weierstrass excess function OurBigBook.com
At a strong local minimizer of a variational integral, almost every reference slope u′(x) is a point of convexity of v↦f(x,u(x),v). Equivalently its Weierstrass excess function is nonnegative for every trial slope. Uniformly small variations can have large derivatives, which is why this condition tests all trial slopes rather than only nearby ones. It is a necessary condition along the minimizing curve; the strengthened Weierstrass condition additionally controls a neighborhood of that curve and its slopes.

Bounded slope integrands force strong minima to be global (I(u)=∫g(u′),g∈C1(R) bounded)

Words: 110
Weierstrass necessary condition OurBigBook.com
Suppose the Weierstrass necessary condition applies to a strong local minimum of this fixed-endpoint integral. It requires the tangent to g at u′(x) to lie below the entire graph for almost every x. A line of nonzero slope is unbounded above in one direction, while g is bounded; hence this supporting line has slope zero. Its contact value is then a global minimum of g. The resulting function minimizes the integral globally, since its integrand is almost everywhere equal to the global lower bound. Thus for a bounded differentiable slope-only integrand a strong local minimum can exist only at global energy level. Weak local minima can behave differently because their competitors have nearby derivatives.

Strong local minimizer of a variational integral

Words: 134 Articles: 1
Calculus of variations OurBigBook.com
For a fixed-endpoint variational integral, an admissible function u is a strong local minimizer if I(v)≥I(u) for all admissible v whose uniform norm distance from u is sufficiently small. Derivatives can be far apart. Hence strong local minimality implies weak local minimality, while a global minimizer implies both. Small narrow bumps and rapid low-amplitude oscillations explain why the reverse implication can fail.

Coercivity transfers strong minimality to L1 minimality (f(x,u,v)≥c∣v∣p−C,p>1)

With fixed endpoint values on a finite interval, the displayed derivative coercivity makes a finite-energy strong local minimizer of a variational integral also a local minimizer in L1. Extremal

Words: 44
Calculus of variations OurBigBook.com
A sufficiently regular admissible function at which the first variation of a functional vanishes for every permitted variation. An extremal is a stationary candidate; it need not be a local maximum or minimum. For a first-order integral functional with fixed endpoint values, it satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation under the usual regularity assumptions.

Variational principle

Words: 214 Articles: 1
Calculus of variations OurBigBook.com
A variational principle specifies a function, path or field by requiring a functional to be stationary under an allowed class of variations. The admissible class includes the prescribed boundary conditions and constraints. A stationary value need not be a minimum: the Euler-Lagrange equation expresses the first-order stationarity condition, while a minimum requires additional analysis. Fermat principle applies this idea to optical path length, and the Rayleigh-Ritz method approximates a variational problem in a chosen family of trial functions.

Variational projection onto an involutive symmetry (E±​=(a±c)/(1±b))

Words: 146
Variational principle OurBigBook.com
Let H be a lower-bounded self-adjoint Hamiltonian operator and I a commuting self-adjoint operator with I2=1. For a normalized trial vector ϕ, define a=⟨H⟩ϕ​, b=⟨I⟩ϕ​, c=⟨HI⟩ϕ​. The Rayleigh quotient on ϕ+tIϕ is [(1+t2)a+2tc]/[1+t2+2tb], with derivative 2(c−ab)(1−t2)/(1+t2+2tb)2. Unless c=ab, its extrema are the normalized nonzero vectors ϕ±Iϕ, with energies (a±c)/(1±b). Their difference is 2(c−ab)/(1−b2), identifying the maximum and minimum.
The orthogonal projections (1±I)/2 split any trial energy into a weighted mean of the two sector energies, so one nonzero sector projection never raises the trial energy. If ∣b∣=1, one projected vector is zero and its quotient is undefined; the remaining family has constant energy. Exchange symmetry makes these sectors bosonic and fermionic spatial wavefunctions. Stationarity within this two-dimensional trial space does not generally make a vector an exact eigenvector of H.

Brachistochrone problem

Words: 614 Articles: 4
Calculus of variations OurBigBook.com
The brachistochrone problem asks for the curve along which a particle released from rest reaches a specified target fastest under a uniform gravitational field, without friction. With downward depth u, conservation of energy gives speed 2gu​ and travel time ∫(1+u′2)/(2gu)​dx. The Beltrami identity makes u(1+u′2) constant, leading to a cycloid. If only the horizontal endpoint coordinate x0​>0 is fixed, the natural boundary conditions for a free endpoint select the half arch ending at its deepest point, with depth 2x0​/π and minimum time πx0​/g​.

Fixed-length brachistochrone between equal-height endpoints (L>1,b<1,c>0)

Words: 114
Brachistochrone problem OurBigBook.com
For horizontal separation one, let y>0 be downward depth and constrain the arc length to L. Conservation of energy makes the travel time proportional to ∫1+y′2​/y​dx. An isoperimetric constraint adds a Lagrange multiplier λ, and the Beltrami identity gives (y−1/2+λ)/1+y′2​=H>0. With b=λ/H, c=H2 and s=sin(ϕ/2), the stationary arch is y=s2/[c(1−bs)2], dx/dϕ=s2/[c(1−bs)3], for 0≤ϕ≤2π. The endpoint and length integrals fix b,c uniquely for L>1; symmetry gives x(2π−ϕ)=1−x(ϕ). At L=1 the only curve is horizontal and a particle released from rest never completes the journey. The finite-time stationary arches have vertical endpoint tangents, so the graph is smooth only on the open interval. These first-variation equations alone do not establish global minimality in a larger admissible class.

Measure polar angle θ from the downward vertical on the lower hemisphere of radius R. A particle released from rest at the rim and sliding without energy loss has speed 2gRcosθ​. The spherical arc length along ϕ=ϕ(θ) is R1+sin2θϕ′2​∣dθ∣. Thus its travel time has integrand proportional to (1+sin2θϕ′2)/cosθ​. Since the azimuth is a cyclic coordinate, the Euler-Lagrange equation gives
cosθ​1+sin2θϕ′2​sin2θϕ′​=C.
(3)
Solving for ϕ′2 gives the displayed first integral on a segment where θ is a valid path parameter. For finite C, approaching the rim θ↑π/2 gives ϕ′→0. Thus a regular stationary path begins along the meridian toward the bottom. This is an initial direction condition; a path joining other rim points can turn before reaching the bottom. Monotone segments use positive ∣dθ∣ in the time integral, and a turning point requires another path parameter or a split into segments.

Maximum-depth bound for a brachistochrone (T≥[l/h​+πh​]/2g​≥2πl/g​)

Words: 153
Brachistochrone problem OurBigBook.com
Consider a frictionless track from rest between equal-height endpoints separated horizontally by l>0. Let depth y be positive downward and let the maximum depth be h>0. Conservation of energy gives speed 2gy​. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, applied to the tangent displacement, gives
y​ds​≥h​dx​+hyh−y​​∣dy∣.
(4)
The squared norm of the coefficient vector is 1/h+(h−y)/(hy)=1/y. The track goes from depth zero to h and returns to zero, so the second term integrates to at least 2∫0h​(h−y)/(hy)​dy=πh​. Integrating the first term gives l/h​, even if the track backtracks horizontally. Division by 2g​ proves the first bound. The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality proves the second.
Equality requires h=l/π and tangent slope ∣dy/dx∣=(h−y)/y​, on the descending and ascending halves respectively. Substitution y=hsin2θ integrates to the cycloid x=h(θ−21​sin2θ), 0≤θ≤π. It attains the bound, proving global minimality of the single-arch brachistochrone problem between these endpoints. Endpoint singularities are handled as limits of the integrals; the cycloid has finite travel time.

Brachistochrone on a horizontal half-cylinder (z′(θ)=RKcosθ​/1−K2cosθ​)

Words: 133
Brachistochrone problem OurBigBook.com
Measure θ∈[−π/2,π/2] from the vertically downward direction on the lower half of a cylinder of radius R, and let z be axial distance. From rest at an edge, conservation of energy gives speed 2gRcosθ​. The travel time is T=(2gR)−1/2∫R2+z′2​/cosθ​dθ. Since z is a cyclic coordinate, its conjugate quantity z′/[cosθ​R2+z′2​]=K is constant, giving the displayed formula. For finite positive axial separation, 0<K<1 is fixed uniquely by integrating z′ between the edges: the separation grows strictly from zero to infinity as K increases from zero to one. The time integrand is strictly convex in z′, so its supporting-line inequality proves global minimality for the fixed endpoints. As the starting edge is approached, z′→0: the initial direction is directly down the curved surface, with no axial component. This differs from the constant-pitch shortest geodesic on the unrolled cylinder.

Travel-time minimization with speed proportional to squared radius

For speed v=kr2 in the punctured plane, the travel-time element is ds/(kr2). Under the inversion in a circle with radial coordinate u=1/r, this becomes k−1du2+u2dθ2​, the arc length in Euclidean space divided by k. Thus a shortest travel-time route corresponds to a straight line segment in the inverted plane, whenever that segment avoids the origin and lies in the allowed region. Gamma-convergence (Fn​Γ​F)

Words: 64
Calculus of variations OurBigBook.com
A sequence of functionals Gamma-converges in a specified topology when every convergent un​→u satisfies F(u)≤liminfn​Fn​(un​), and each u has a recovery sequence un​→u with Fn​(un​)→F(u). With appropriate compactness of energy sublevels, limits of approximate global minimizers minimize F, and the minimum values converge. This is suited to variational approximation such as the Ambrosio–Tortorelli approximation; it does not describe arbitrary local minima.

Variational problem

Words: 60
Calculus of variations OurBigBook.com
A variational problem seeks a stationary point or minimizer of a functional on a specified function space. For a symmetric coercive bilinear form a and bounded linear functional ℓ on a real Hilbert space, minimizing J(v)=a(v,v)/2−ℓ(v) is equivalent to the weak formulation a(u,v)=ℓ(v) for every test vector. The Lax-Milgram theorem gives a unique solution, and J(u+w)−J(u)=a(w,w)/2 proves unique minimization. Specifying the admissible space and its boundary constraints is part of the problem.

(2x01/3​​)6≤(∫0x0​​x2∣u′∣6dx)(35​x03/5​)5.

Tonelli existence theorem

Words: 116
Direct method in the calculus of variations OurBigBook.com
For a scalar fixed-endpoint problem on a bounded interval, suppose the continuous integrand f(x,y,p) is convex in p and satisfies f(x,y,p)≥Θ(∣p∣)−C, where Θ is nonnegative convex and Θ(r)/r→∞. If there is one admissible absolutely continuous function of finite energy, a global minimizer exists in W1,1.
The lower bound gives uniform integrability of derivatives in a minimizing sequence. Fixed endpoint values then give boundedness and equicontinuity of the functions. Extract uniform convergence of the functions and weak L1 convergence of their derivatives; the limit retains the endpoint values. Convexity in the derivative gives weak lower semicontinuity of convex gradient energies, so the limit attains the infimum. Strict convexity of the full integrand on a convex admissible class additionally gives uniqueness.
