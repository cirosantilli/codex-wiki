Markov's inequality (P(Y≥a)≤EY/a)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Probability and statistics Probability theory Probability inequality
OurBigBook.com Words: 507 Articles: 2
For a nonnegative random variable Y and threshold a>0, Markov's inequality gives
P(Y≥a)≤aEY​.
(14)
The useful case has finite expected value; an infinite right side gives no finite information. The bound also holds if Y is nonnegative almost surely. Combining it with the elementary bound one gives P(Y≥a)≤min{1,EY/a}. Nonnegativity is essential: negative values could otherwise cancel the contribution of large positive values to the expected value.
The idea is an average budget. Every outcome where Y≥a contributes at least a times its probability to EY, and the other outcomes contribute nonnegative amounts. Formally, the indicator function gives Y≥a1{Y≥a}​; taking the expected value proves the bound. [ref].
For example, let Y take 0, 1, 2, 5 with respective probabilities 0.5, 0.2, 0.2, 0.1. Its expected value is 1.1. At a=2, the tail probability is 0.3, while the bound is 1.1/2=0.55. At a=5, the tail probability is 0.1, while the bound is 0.22. A different probability distribution with the same expected value, putting masses 0.78 and 0.22 at 0 and 5, attains the bound 0.22 exactly at a=5.
Markov's inequality is useful when a moment is easy to compute but a full probability distribution is difficult to determine. A nonnegative count with expected value less than one must have some positive probability of being zero, giving the first moment method. Applying the same bound to a squared centered random variable gives Chebyshev's inequality. Applying it to an exponential function of a random variable gives an exponential Markov bound, which can exploit more than a single moment.
