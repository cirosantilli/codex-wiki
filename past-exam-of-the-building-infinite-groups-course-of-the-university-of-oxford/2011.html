Use the commutator convention [x,y]=x−1y−1xy, and let [A,B] denote the subgroup generated by all such commutators with x∈A,y∈B. The lower central series is
γ1​(G)=G,γi+1​(G)=[γi​(G),G].
(1)
A central series has [Gj​,G]≤Gj−1​ for each j≥1. We prove the requested inclusion by induction. At i=0, γ1​(G)=Gn​. If i<n and γi+1​(G)≤Gn−i​, then
γi+2​(G)=[γi+1​(G),G]≤[Gn−i​,G]≤Gn−i−1​.
(2)
Therefore
γi+1​(G)≤Gn−i​(0≤i≤n).​
(3)
In particular γn+1​(G)=1. The proof uses the central-series condition directly; no equality of the intermediate terms is claimed.

Suppose inductively that G=Hγi​(G). In the quotient G/γi+1​(G), the image of γi​(G) is central. Hence this quotient is generated by the image of H together with a central subgroup, so its commutator subgroup lies in the image of H. Pulling this back gives
γ2​(G)≤Hγi+1​(G).
(4)
The original assumption now gives G=Hγ2​(G)≤Hγi+1​(G)≤G. Starting with i=2, induction proves G=Hγi​(G) for every i≥2. These products are subgroups because each γi​(G) is normal; no normality of H is needed.
If G is finitely generated, every quotient, including its abelianization G/γ2​(G), is finitely generated. Conversely, lift a finite generating set of that quotient to g1​,…,gt​∈G, and set H=⟨g1​,…,gt​⟩. Then G=Hγ2​(G). For a nilpotent group, γc+1​(G)=1 for some c, and the established identity gives G=H. Thus
G finitely generated⟺G/γ2​(G) finitely generated.​
(5)

All commutators of H with K lie in the central subgroup Y of HK. In the standard commutator identities, conjugation by elements of H or K therefore acts trivially on these commutators. Since Y is abelian, their order can also be reversed. Consequently
[h1​h2​,k]=[h1​,k][h2​,k],[h,k1​k2​]=[h,k1​][h,k2​].
(6)
Thus the map is a homomorphism in each variable separately; this does not assert that it is a homomorphism from the direct product jointly.
It factors through H/CH​(K) in the first variable and K/CK​(H) in the second. If these finite quotient groups have representatives h1​,…,ha​ and k1​,…,kb​, then the finite collection [hi​,kj​] generates Y. Let e be an exponent of H/CH​(K). For every h,k,
[h,k]e=[he,k]=1.
(7)
Hence Y is a finitely generated abelian group of bounded exponent, and Y is finite. For instance its order is at most eab.
Now suppose G is nilpotent and G/γ2​(G) is finite. Induct on the nilpotency class c. For c=1, G is its own abelianization and is finite. If c≥2, the group Gˉ=G/γc​(G) has smaller class and
Gˉ/γ2​(Gˉ)≅G/γ2​(G),
(8)
since γc​(G)≤γ2​(G). By induction Gˉ is finite. Apply the preceding result with H=G=γ1​(G) and K=γc−1​(G). Their commutator subgroup is Y=γc​(G)≤Z(G). Both relevant centralizer quotients are finite, because their centralizers contain γc​(G) and G/γc​(G) is finite. Thus γc​(G) is finite. The extension of this finite normal subgroup by the finite quotient Gˉ is finite. The converse follows because a quotient of a finite group is finite. Therefore
G finite⟺G/γ2​(G) finite(G nilpotent).​
(9)

Let ∣g∣S​ be the least length of a word in S∪S−1 representing g, and write BS​(r)={g:∣g∣S​≤r}. Polynomial growth of a group of degree at most d means that, for some constant C,
∣BS​(r)∣≤C(1+r)d(r≥0).
(10)
In particular these balls are finite. For the usual convention S is finite; finiteness of BS​(1) would force that in any case.
For a finite subset T, set L=max(1,maxt∈T​∣t∣S​). Replacing each letter of a T-word by an S-word gives
BT​(r)⊆BS​(Lr).
(11)
Thus ∣BT​(r)∣≤C(1+Lr)d≤CLd(1+r)d. This proves the same degree bound for ⟨T⟩. If T is empty the subgroup is trivial and the assertion is immediate.

Choose finite symmetric generating sets Si​ for all Gi​, and let L≥1 bound the S-length of their elements. If [Gi−1​:Gi​]=∞, the connected locally finite Schreier graph of left cosets of Gi​ in Gi−1​ is infinite. Its radius-r ball has at least r+1 vertices: otherwise a finite-diameter locally finite connected graph would be finite. Equivalently, we can choose r+1 distinct left-coset representatives ti,0​,…,ti,r​ of Gi​, each of Si−1​-length at most r, and hence S-length at most Lr.
The (r+1)n products
t1,j1​​t2,j2​​⋯tn,jn​​(0≤ji​≤r)
(12)
are distinct. Indeed, equality first forces equality of the G1​-cosets of the first factors, and hence their indices; cancellation then forces equality of the G2​-cosets of the second factors, and so on. Each product has S-length at most nLr. Therefore
(r+1)n≤∣BS​(nLr)∣≤C(1+nLr)d.
(13)
Letting r→∞ yields
n≤d.​
(14)
For n=0 the assertion is automatic.

First establish a stronger form of the coset-counting argument. If K is a finitely generated infinite-index subgroup of a finitely generated group L of growth at most q, choose a finite generating set T for K, and let M bound its lengths in a generating set S of L. The Schreier graph supplies r+1 distinct left-coset representatives of K of S-length at most r. The corresponding translates of BT​(r) are disjoint and lie in BS​((M+1)r). Hence
(r+1)∣BT​(r)∣≤C(1+(M+1)r)q.
(15)
Thus an infinite-index finitely generated subgroup has growth at most q−1 whenever the ambient upper bound is q.
Every finitely generated K≤H has infinite index in G. Applying this result with L=G gives growth at most n−1. Minimality of n among the supposed counterexamples therefore makes each such K virtually nilpotent. Here n≥1, since a group with a uniform bound on its finite word balls is finite, and finite groups are virtually nilpotent.
For each finitely generated K≤H, let d(K) be the least nonnegative integer giving a polynomial growth upper bound. It exists and belongs to {0,…,n−1}. Choose K for which this integer is maximal. If K≤L≤H and L is finitely generated, then d(L)≤d(K). Were [L:K] infinite, the strengthened bound would make K have growth at most d(L)−1≤d(K)−1, contradicting the definition of d(K). If d(K)=0, the same inequality would force the nonempty balls of K to have cardinality tending to zero, also impossible. Hence every such overgroup has [L:K]<∞.
Since K is virtually nilpotent, choose a nilpotent finite-index subgroup N≤K. It is finitely generated by Schreier's lemma. For any h∈H, the subgroup L=⟨K,h⟩ is finitely generated and [L:K]<∞. Pigeonholing the cosets hjK gives a positive a with ha∈K. Pigeonholing the cosets of N in K then gives a positive b with (ha)b∈N. Consequently
For every h∈H there is r=ab>0 with hr∈N.​
(16)
No normality of K in L or of N in K is required. Using least integer upper bounds also avoids needing any further structural theorem about the exact growth degree of a virtually nilpotent group.

Let B=H1​×⋯×Hm​, with each Hi​ a copy of H. The permutation group F≤Sm​ acts on B by
ασ​(h1​,…,hm​)j​=hσ−1(j)​.
(17)
Define the permutation wreath product to be the semidirect product
HwrF=Hm⋊α​F.​
(18)
Explicitly,
(b,σ)(c,τ)=(bασ​(c),στ).
(19)
Writing hi​ for the element with entry h in coordinate i and identity elsewhere, and writing σ for (1,σ), conjugation satisfies
σhi​σ−1=hσ(i)​.
(20)
Thus the top permutation group permutes the coordinate subgroups in exactly its given action. Distinct coordinate subgroups commute, including when H itself is nonabelian.

Put A=Am​ and let T be the rooted tree of finite words over {1,…,m}. View a∈A as a rooted automorphism: it permutes the first letter by a and fixes the suffix. Denote this rooted copy by A again.
For a∈A, define a directed tree automorphism b(a) by putting local permutation a at every vertex
2,12,112,1112,…
(21)
and identity local permutation at every other vertex. These marked vertices are pairwise incomparable, so the prescribed actions are on disjoint rooted subtrees and define a unique automorphism. Equivalently its root permutation is trivial and its sections are
b(a)=(b(a),a,1,…,1).
(22)
The map a↦b(a) is an injective homomorphism: it is the same copy of a acting on each of those disjoint subtrees. Let B=b(A) and define
H=⟨A,B⟩≤Aut(T).
(23)
Both A and B are finite, so H is finitely generated. For m≥5, Am​ is a nonabelian simple group and hence perfect. Thus A,B≤H′, and because they generate H, H is perfect.
Every section of either defining generator belongs to H, so sections of every element of H belong to H. Its root permutation belongs to A. For a tree automorphism g, write g(iw)=σ(i)gi​(w), where gi​ is its section at input letter i. In the semidirect-product convention of part (a), define
θ(g)=(gσ−1(1)​,…,gσ−1(m)​;σ).
(24)
Composition of tree automorphisms gives exactly the wreath-product multiplication: this is why θ is a homomorphism. It is injective because an automorphism is determined by its first-level permutation and all its first-level sections. On the generators,
θ(a)=(1,…,1;a),θ(b(a))=(b(a),a,1,…,1;1).
(25)
It remains to prove surjectivity; merely writing these recursions would not establish it. Choose σ=(234)∈A, which fixes 1 and sends 2 to 3. For a,c∈A, the two base tuples θ(b(a)) and θ(σb(c)σ−1) overlap only in their first coordinate. Their commutator is therefore
([b(a),b(c)],1,…,1;1)=(b([a,c]),1,…,1;1).
(26)
Since A is perfect, products of these commutators give every element of the copy of B localized in coordinate 1. Canceling this first coordinate from θ(b(a)) then gives (1,a,1,…,1;1), the rooted copy of A localized in coordinate 2. Conjugating by the transitive rooted group Am​ supplies both localized A and localized B in every coordinate. Since H=⟨A,B⟩, the image contains the full base Hm. It also contains the top Am​. Therefore it is the entire wreath product and
θ:H⟶∼​HwrAm​.​
(27)
Finally H is infinite: it contains the nontrivial rooted Am​, and a finite order h=∣H∣ would satisfy h=hm∣Am​∣, which is impossible for m≥5.

Let L be the subgroup of HwrAm​ generated by all its elements of order 3. The top group Am​ is generated by 3-cycles, also when m=3, so Am​≤L.
Take σ=(123). For any h∈H, the conjugate h1​σh1−1​ has order 3, as does σ−1. Thus their product belongs to L and equals
d12​(h)=h1​σh1−1​σ−1=h1​h2−1​.
(28)
Using σ−1 instead supplies d13​(h)=h1​h3−1​. Since distinct coordinate groups commute,
[d12​(h),d13​(k)]=[h,k]1​∈L.
(29)
The hypothesis that H is perfect says that products of its commutators generate H, so H1​≤L. Transitivity of Am​ now gives Hi​≤L for every coordinate. Hence Hm≤L and Am​≤L, proving
HwrAm​ is generated by elements of order 3.​
(30)
The proof does not require H itself to be finitely generated, nor does it require Am​ to be perfect for m=3,4.
