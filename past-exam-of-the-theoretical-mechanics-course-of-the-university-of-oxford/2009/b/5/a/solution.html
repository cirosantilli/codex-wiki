Split I into the two tails and the interval ∣k−k∗​∣<δ. On each tail ψ′ has a fixed nonzero sign, so changing variable to s=ψ(k) produces a nonstationary Fourier integral. Under the usual decay and regularity needed for integration by parts, these pieces are O(t−1).
Near the nondegenerate stationary point, make the signed local change
z=sgn(k−k∗​)2[ψ(k)−ψ(k∗​)]/ψ′′(k∗​)​.

It has dk/dz=1 at zero and makes the phase exactly ψ(k∗​)+ψ′′(k∗​)z2/2. The transformed amplitude tends to f(k∗​). Scale z by t−1/2 and use the Fresnel integral to obtain
I(t)=f(k∗​)eitψ(k∗​)+iπ/4tψ′′(k∗​)2π​​+o(t−1/2).​
(23)
The O(t−1) tails are smaller than this leading stationary contribution. If ψ′′(k∗​)<0, complex conjugation of the Gaussian phase changes the phase correction to −π/4 and replaces the second derivative by its absolute value:
I(t)=f(k∗​)eitψ(k∗​)−iπ/4t∣ψ′′(k∗​)∣2π​​+o(t−1/2).​
(24)
When the leading coefficient is nonzero this may be written as an asymptotic equivalence. For full rigor, continuity of an arbitrary amplitude alone is not sufficient for the stated nonstationary O(t−1) rate, nor does it ensure convergence of an infinite integral. Piecewise differentiability with integrable derivative, bounded variation, or an equivalent localization/decay hypothesis supplies the usual missing assumption.
