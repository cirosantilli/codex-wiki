For a material volume V(t) moving with velocity u, the Reynolds transport theorem is
dtd​∫V(t)​fdV=∫V(t)​[∂t​f+∇⋅(fu)]dV.
(1)
It applies componentwise to vector quantities. Cauchy's stress theorem represents traction on a surface with outward unit normal n as ti​=σij​nj​. Conservation of mass gives d∫V(t)​ρdV/dt=0 and hence ∂t​ρ+∇⋅(ρu)=0. Constant density reduces this to ∇⋅u=0​.
With no body force, conservation of momentum and the divergence theorem give
dtd​∫V(t)​ρui​dV=∫∂V​σij​nj​dS=∫V​∂j​σij​dV.
(2)
Applying transport and then mass conservation cancels the terms proportional to ui​, leaving ρ(∂t​ui​+uj​∂j​ui​)=∂j​σij​. For the stated Newtonian stress, constant viscosity gives ∂j​σij​=−∂i​p+μΔui​+μ∂i​(∇⋅u). The last term vanishes. Since the material volume was arbitrary,
ρ[ut​+(u⋅∇)u]=−∇p+μΔu.​
(3)
A body force, if present, would have to be added to the momentum balance; it is absent from the specified model.

For u=u(y,t)ex​, incompressibility is automatic and the nonlinear advection term is zero. With no pressure gradient the streamwise momentum equation is ρut​=μuyy​​. Seek its periodic response as u=Re[Uf(y)eiΩt]. The amplitude satisfies f′′=iρΩf/μ with f(±d)=1. Since [k(1+i)]2=iρΩ/μ for k=ρΩ/(2μ)​, even symmetry gives
f(y)=cosh[k(1+i)d]cosh[k(1+i)y]​.​
(4)
Odd components vanish because the two boundary amplitudes are equal. This is the unique periodic solution. Without a specified initial profile, decaying zero-wall diffusion transients may be added, so the formula should not be read as a claim that every transient is absent.
As kd→∞, the wall motions penetrate only a distance k−1=2μ/(ρΩ)​. Near either wall, with inward distance s=d−∣y∣, the leading velocity is
u∼Ue−kscos(Ωt−ks).​
(5)
The two oscillatory viscous layers are separated by an almost motionless core; the phase lag increases inward. This is in-phase oscillating parallel-plate flow.

Let δ=Re−1/2 and use the inner variables Y=y/δ=Re1/2y,V=v/δ=Re1/2v​. Continuity balances ux​ with VY​. In the tangential momentum balance, advection is order one while the normal viscous term is Re−1δ−2uYY​=uYY​. Streamwise viscosity is smaller by Re−1. The normal momentum balance forces the leading pressure to be independent of Y. Thus
ux​+VY​=0,uux​+VuY​=−px​+uYY​,pY​=0.​
(6)
On the stationary impermeable plate, no-slip boundary condition and no-penetration boundary condition give u=V=0 at Y=0, x>0. Matching to the uniform outer stream gives u→1 as Y→∞, and the leading-edge/upstream data are u→1 as x↓0 at fixed Y>0. There is no requirement that the scaled transverse velocity V vanish at infinity; its physical value v=δV matches a smaller outer correction. The outer streamwise Euler equation with constant speed one gives px​=0, and pY​=0 carries this pressure gradient throughout the layer.

The Von Mises boundary-layer transformation uses Ψ as transverse coordinate. With u=U(x,Ψ) and Ψx​=−V, ΨY​=u=U, the chain rule gives
ux​=Ux​−VUΨ​,uY​=UUΨ​,uYY​=U∂Ψ​(UUΨ​).​
(7)
The streamwise advection terms reduce to uux​+VuY​=UUx​, so, for Y>0 where U>0, division by U gives
Ux​=∂Ψ​(UUΨ​).​
(8)
Continuity was already enforced by the stream function. Substitute U=g(η) with η=Ψ/x​. Then Ux​=−ηg′/(2x) and ∂Ψ​(UUΨ​)=(gg′)′/x, yielding
(gg′)′+21​ηg′=0,g(0)=0,g(∞)=1.​
(9)
The wall coordinate is zero because Ψ was set to zero there, and the far condition matches unit external speed. This is the transformed Blasius similarity problem. One must not additionally prescribe a finite g′(0): the coordinate change degenerates at the no-slip wall, and the regular physical shear corresponds to g∝η​ near zero. This similarity reduction is obtained for the attached monotone boundary layer assumed in the question.
Existence of a positive normalized profile can also be seen directly. Put q=gg′ and choose q(0)=a>0. In g coordinates the regular initial-value system is dq/dg=−η/2, dη/dg=g/q, with η(0)=0. It starts as g∼2aη​. For finite positive η, q′/q=−η/(2g) keeps q positive and q≤a, while (g2)′=2q gives g≤2aη​. Consequently q′/q≤−η​/(22a​), so q is integrable at infinity. It follows that g increases to a finite positive limit M. The rescaled profile g​(η)=M−1g(M​η) satisfies the same equation and has limit one. This supplies the requested similarity solution with both boundary conditions, rather than only its formal differential equation.

Scale x,y by L, z by δL, tangential velocities by U, normal velocity by δU, and time by L/U. Continuity balances all three derivatives. Tangential viscous forces scale as μU/(δ2L2), hence their pressure balance requires
p∼δ2LμU​,t∼L/U.​
(10)
The inertial/normal-viscous ratio is δ2ρLU/μ, so it is negligible under the given smallness condition. Tangential viscous derivatives are smaller by δ2; the normal pressure balance gives pz​=0 at leading order. In dimensional variables the remaining equations are continuity, μuzz​=px​, μvzz​=py​, pz​=0.
No-slip boundary condition at each plate imposes u=v=0, while their normal velocities are w(0)=0 and w(h)=h˙. Integrating the two tangential equations twice gives
(u,v)=2μz(z−h)​∇p,u=h1​∫0h​(u,v)dz=−12μh2​∇p.​
(11)
Integrate continuity through the gap. Since h is independent of in-plane position and the tangential velocity vanishes at the upper wall, the flux divergence is ∇⋅(hu) and the normal contribution is h˙. Therefore
h˙+∇⋅(hu)=0.​
(12)

b OurBigBook.com
For spatially uniform h(t), the gap equation gives Δp=12μh˙/h3. Axisymmetry makes this r−1(rpr​)r​=12μh˙/h3. Its general integral contains a logarithmic term; bounded pressure at zero excludes that term. Enforcing zero gauge pressure at the outer edge gives the axisymmetric squeeze-film pressure
p(r,t)=h33μh˙​(r2−R2).​
(13)
The mean radial velocity is ur​=−h2pr​/(12μ)=−rh˙/(2h). At the material edge this must equal R˙, so 2hR˙+Rh˙=0. Hence
πR(t)2h(t)=πR(0)2h(0).​
(14)
It is conservation of the incompressible liquid volume, with no liquid entering or leaving through the plates or material rim.
When the plates approach, h˙<0 and p>0 inside: the liquid is squeezed outward. Separation requires p<0 relative to the surrounding air and inward motion of the edge. Negative gauge pressure is not intrinsically impossible, but substantial suction can cause cavitation or air invasion, and an advancing air/liquid interface can develop fingering. Thus the same mathematical lubrication solution exists for either sign, whereas its opening-plate physical validity needs these additional failure mechanisms to be absent; the closing case is the robust regime of the stated idealized model.

The inviscid equations are ρt​+∇⋅(ρu)=0 and ρ[ut​+(u⋅∇)u]=−∇p. Write ρ=ρ0​+ρ′, p=P(ρ0​)+p′ and retain only linear terms. With c02​=P′(ρ0​)>0, barotropy gives p′=c02​ρ′. Irrotational perturbations allow u′=∇ϕ. The momentum equation integrates to p′=−ρ0​ϕt​, after using the time-dependent gauge of ϕ to remove the spatially constant integration term. Thus
c02​=P′(ρ0​),p′=−ρ0​ϕt​,ρ′=−c02​ρ0​​ϕt​.​
(15)
Substitution into ρt′​+ρ0​Δϕ=0 gives the acoustic wave equation ϕtt​=c02​Δϕ​.

Retain the rigid-wall cosine in x and the lower-wall cosine in y:
ϕ=Acos(nπx/a)cos(βy/b)e−iωt.
(18)
The wave equation gives ω2=c02​[(nπ/a)2+(β/b)2]. The upper-wall condition gives −kβsinβ/b−ω2cosβ=0, hence
βtanβ=−ω2b/k.​
(19)
For nonzero real β, eliminating ω gives −tanβ=M(β+κn2​/β) with M=c02​/(kb)>0, κn​=nπb/a.
On every interval ((j+1/2)π,(j+1)π), the left side decreases from positive infinity to zero, while the right side is finite and positive. Continuity gives at least one intersection per interval for each n. The functions are analytic between their poles, so roots are isolated there; their union over countably many intervals is a countably infinite set. Reflection gives the redundant negative-β solutions. For n=0, the limiting formal root β=0 is the zero-frequency gauge mode, not one of these positive-frequency branches.
The graph uses representative dimensionless values M=1 and b/a=1; the interval argument applies for all positive values. For fixed n and fixed branch j, take M→∞. The positive right side becomes large, forcing the intersection towards the tangent pole from the right:
βj​=(j+21​)π+O(M−1).
(20)
More precisely, if βj0​=(j+1/2)π, then βj​−βj0​∼βj0​/[M((βj0​)2+κn2​)]. Therefore the requested real-branch frequencies satisfy
ωn,j​∼πc0​a2n2​+b2(j+1/2)2​​,n,j=0,1,2,….​
(21)
The limiting upper condition is effectively pressure release for these acoustic branches. No claim is made here about imaginary or other complex β branches, which were excluded from the requested analysis.

Split I into the two tails and the interval ∣k−k∗​∣<δ. On each tail ψ′ has a fixed nonzero sign, so changing variable to s=ψ(k) produces a nonstationary Fourier integral. Under the usual decay and regularity needed for integration by parts, these pieces are O(t−1).
Near the nondegenerate stationary point, make the signed local change
z=sgn(k−k∗​)2[ψ(k)−ψ(k∗​)]/ψ′′(k∗​)​.
(22)
It has dk/dz=1 at zero and makes the phase exactly ψ(k∗​)+ψ′′(k∗​)z2/2. The transformed amplitude tends to f(k∗​). Scale z by t−1/2 and use the Fresnel integral to obtain
I(t)=f(k∗​)eitψ(k∗​)+iπ/4tψ′′(k∗​)2π​​+o(t−1/2).​
(23)
The O(t−1) tails are smaller than this leading stationary contribution. If ψ′′(k∗​)<0, complex conjugation of the Gaussian phase changes the phase correction to −π/4 and replaces the second derivative by its absolute value:
I(t)=f(k∗​)eitψ(k∗​)−iπ/4t∣ψ′′(k∗​)∣2π​​+o(t−1/2).​
(24)
When the leading coefficient is nonzero this may be written as an asymptotic equivalence. For full rigor, continuity of an arbitrary amplitude alone is not sufficient for the stated nonstationary O(t−1) rate, nor does it ensure convergence of an infinite integral. Piecewise differentiability with integrable derivative, bounded variation, or an equivalent localization/decay hypothesis supplies the usual missing assumption. The exponentially decaying beam amplitude below has the required piecewise regularity.

Use η​(k,t)=∫η(x,t)e−ikxdx and the inverse factor 1/(2π). Fourier transformation gives η​tt​+λ2k4η​=0. The supplied Lorentzian transform and zero initial velocity therefore imply
η​=ae−ϵ∣k∣cos(λk2t),η(x,t)=2π1​∫η​(k,t)eikxdk.​

Integrate each steady one-dimensional conservation law across a thin region enclosing the shock. Mass conservation gives constant mass flux j=ρu. Momentum conservation includes both advected momentum and pressure force, so p+ρu2 is constant. The total energy flux is u(ρe+p)=j(e+p/ρ), where e=cv​T+u2/2. For a genuine shock j=0, division by the mass flux and the equation of state cv​T=p/[(γ−1)ρ] give the conserved stagnation enthalpy
e+p/ρ=2u2​+(γ−1)ργp​.
(28)
Thus the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas are
[ρu]=0,[p+ρu2]=0,[2u2​+(γ−1)ργp​]=0.​
(29)
The velocities here are measured in the stationary shock frame; a moving-shock problem must first be transformed to that frame.

Let state zero be the undisturbed gas and state one the uniform gas between piston and shock. Their laboratory velocities are zero and U. In the shock frame their speed magnitudes are V and V−U. Mass and momentum jumps give
ρ1​=ρ0​V−UV​,p1​−p0​=ρ0​VU.
(30)
Energy conservation gives γ[p1​/ρ1​−p0​/ρ0​]/(γ−1)=VU−U2/2. Substitute the preceding relations and γp0​/ρ0​=c02​, then divide by the nonzero U. The result is
V2−2γ+1​UV−c02​=0.​
(31)
The right-moving root is
V=4γ+1​U+c02​+(4γ+1​U)2​.​
(32)
The other root is negative and does not describe the outgoing shock. For U>0, the quadratic is negative at V=U, hence its positive root obeys V>U: the shock stays ahead of the piston and the density ratio is positive.

The second law requires the specific entropy to increase along the fluid's passage through a nontrivial physical shock: s1​>s0​. For a perfect gas this selects a compressive shock, whose upstream shock-frame Mach number is greater than one and whose downstream shock-frame flow is subsonic.
Here the upstream Mach number is M0​=V/c0​. The quadratic gives V2−c02​=(γ+1)UV/2>0, so M0​>1​. Density and pressure both rise. With c12​=γp1​/ρ1​, one also has V−U<c1​. For example, put M=V/c0​ and use the normal-shock identity
c12​(V−U)2​=2γM2−(γ−1)(γ−1)M2+2​<1(M>1).
(33)
The characteristic geometry is therefore c0​<V<U+c1​. The plus-family characteristics from the gas ahead are overtaken by the shock, while those behind catch up with it; they converge into the discontinuity. The minus-family speeds are −c0​ ahead and U−c1​ behind. The piston is the line x=Ut and the shock is the steeper-in-x/t line x=Vt.

d OurBigBook.com
For U<0 the same positive algebraic root remains real and satisfies 0<V<c0​, because V2−c02​=(γ+1)UV/2<0. The jump relations then give a density decrease, ρ1​/ρ0​=V/(V−U)<1, and a pressure decrease. Where both states remain thermodynamically positive, this is an expansion shock: its upstream shock-frame flow is subsonic and its entropy change has the forbidden sign. At sufficiently large negative U the formula even predicts nonpositive pressure, an additional reason it cannot represent a physical gas state.
The observed outgoing wave is instead a continuous isentropic rarefaction wave. Its plus-family rays spread from the initial piston corner rather than coalescing. The fan head enters the undisturbed gas at x=c0​t; for moderate recession the tail is x=(U+c1​)t, with the uniform gas next to the piston moving at U and c1​=c0​+(γ−1)U/2>0. The appropriate Riemann invariant is u−2c/(γ−1)=−2c0​/(γ−1), so inside the fan, with ξ=x/t,
u=γ+12(ξ−c0​)​,c=γ+12c0​+(γ−1)ξ​.
(34)
These expressions show the spreading characteristics in the right panel of the preceding figure. If U≤−2c0​/(γ−1), the gas cannot follow the piston with positive sound speed; a vacuum gap forms, bounded by the limiting rarefaction wave edge. Thus the fan description also identifies the strong-recession limit.
