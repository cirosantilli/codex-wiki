Label fluid particles by a, with flow map x=χ(a,t) satisfying χt​=u(χ,t). If J=detDa​χ, differentiation of the determinant gives Jt​=J∇⋅u. Pull the material-volume integral back to a fixed initial volume:
dtd​∫V(t)​FdV=dtd​∫V(0)​F(χ(a,t),t)Jda.
(1)
The chain rule and the Jacobian identity give the integrand J(Ft​+u⋅∇F+F∇⋅u). Transforming back yields the Reynolds transport theorem
dtd​∫V(t)​FdV=∫V(t)​[Ft​+∇⋅(Fu)]dV.​
(2)
Equivalently the additional surface flux is ∫∂V(t)​Fu⋅ndS. The material boundary moves with the same normal velocity as the fluid, which is the key distinction from a fixed control volume.

Use conservation of mass and Newton's momentum balance, in addition to the supplied constitutive law. For a material volume,
dtd​∫V(t)​ρudV=∫∂V(t)​σndS+∫V(t)​ρgdV.
(3)
The Reynolds transport theorem and ρt​+∇⋅(ρu)=0 reduce the left side to ∫ρDu/Dt. The divergence theorem then gives ρDui​/Dt=∂j​σij​+ρgi​. For constant μ,
∂j​σij​=−∂i​p+μ∂j​∂j​ui​+μ∂i​(∂j​uj​).
(4)

ρDtDu​=−∇p+μ∇2u+ρg.​
(5)
The stress formula and zero divergence alone are not a dynamical law: the force balance supplies the required physical input.

Take u=(u(y),0) and no imposed streamwise pressure gradient. The normal momentum balance gives p=p∗​−ρgycosα. The streamwise equation is μu′′+ρgsinα=0, since the convective acceleration vanishes. With no slip at the lower wall and signed upper-wall traction τ in the positive x direction, the conditions are
u(0)=0,μu′(h)=τ.
(6)
Integration gives
u(y)=2μρgsinα​y(2h−y)+μτ​y,β=2μρ​,γ=2h+ρgsinα2τ​.​
(7)
The required upper-wall velocity is its endpoint value u(h)=ρgh2sinα/(2μ)+τh/μ. The unfactored formula remains meaningful when sinα=0.
The fluid exerts shear μu′(0)=ρghsinα+τ​ per unit length on the lower plane in the positive x direction. The force of that plane on the fluid has the opposite sign. The total wall force on the fluid balances its downslope weight, providing an independent sign check.

Let δ=Re−1/2, stretch Y=y/δ and write the original transverse velocity as v=δV. Continuity then gives ux​+VY​=0. In the streamwise momentum equation uyy​/Re=uYY​ is order one, while uxx​/Re is negligible. The transverse equation gives pY​=0 to leading order, so pressure is supplied by the outer Euler flow. Its streamwise balance is px​=−UU′. Consequently
uux​+VuY​=UU′+uYY​,ux​+VY​=0.​
(8)
For a stationary impermeable plate, u(x,0)=V(x,0)=0​, with matching u(x,Y)→U(x)​ as Y→∞. The transverse velocity must match the outer displacement flow; it is generally wrong to impose V→0 independently at infinity.
At the leading edge the wall no-slip data meet the external stream abruptly, and the boundary-layer approximation is nonuniform. For a constant external speed, the thickness is O(x/Re​), whose ratio to x ceases to be small when the local Reynolds number is order one. A leading-edge region retaining neglected derivatives is needed there. An adverse pressure gradient can also cause separation farther downstream; a large global Reynolds number alone does not prevent it.

Use u=ψY​, V=−ψx​, and put η=Y/xγ, ψ=xβf(η). Then
u=xβ−γf′,V=−xβ−1(βf−γηf′).
(9)
Matching u∼xm requires β−γ=m. Equating the powers in streamwise inertia x2m−1 and transverse diffusion xm−2γ gives
γ=21−m​,β=21+m​.​
(10)
The two convective terms combine to x2m−1[m(f′)2−βff′′], while UU′=mx2m−1. Hence the Falkner-Skan equation in this scaling is
f(3)+βff′′+m[1−(f′)2]=0,f(0)=f′(0)=0,f′(∞)=1.​
(11)
The additive stream-function constant is fixed by f(0)=0; when β=0 this also encodes no penetration. For β=0 it is a harmless choice of additive constant choice. Existence of an attached physical branch for a given m is a separate issue from deriving the similarity reduction.
On an attached branch with w=f′>0 away from the wall, use f itself as the independent variable. The chain rule gives f′′=wwf​ and f(3)=w(wf2​+wwff​). Therefore
w2wff​+w(wf​)2+βfwwf​+m(1−w2)=0,w(0)=0,w(f)→1 as f→∞.​
(12)
If the wall shear s=f′′(0)>0, then f∼sη2/2, so w(f)∼2sf​. Thus wf​ is singular at zero while wwf​→s is finite. Imposing a finite arbitrary wf​(0) would remove the usual attached branch. This is the velocity as dependent variable in the Falkner-Skan equation reduction.

No slip on the two fixed radial walls gives g(±α)=0. The outward flux through a circular arc is ∫−αα​ur​rdθ=∫−αα​gdθ, so source strength Q requires that integral to equal Q. Set g=QG and ϵ=Q/ν. Then

G0​=C(cos2θ−cos2α),C=sin2α−2αcos2α1​.​
(17)

The normalization denominator vanishes when sin2α−2αcos2α=0, namely tan2αc​=2αc​​. The relevant first root above π/2 is αc​≃2.24670 radians. The zero root is not the critical angle of interest.
For π/2<α<αc​ the denominator is positive. The profile is even, with outward flow near the bisector and reverse flow adjacent to each wall:
G0​>0(∣θ∣<π−α),G0​<0(π−α<∣θ∣<α).
(18)
It vanishes at the walls and at θ=±(π−α); its minima are at θ=±π/2. The following sketch shows the leading approximation to G for small ϵ.
As α approaches αc​ from below, the large positive central and negative wall contributions nearly cancel while the prescribed net flux remains one. The leading Stokes solution diverges, so the supposedly small nonlinear inertial term can no longer be treated as a perturbation. This is the critical angle of a flux-normalized Stokes wedge flow. It demonstrates breakdown of this regular expansion; it does not establish that the exact viscous flow must have infinite speed or that a global steady branch continues through the critical angle.

u=1−hY​+2PX​​Y(Y−h),q=∫0h​udY=2h​−12h3PX​​.​

The first energy term is the rate of change of internal plus kinetic energy per unit volume; the second transports that energy with the moving gas; the pressure term is the mechanical work flux across the boundary. There is no heat flux or viscous work in the stated inviscid adiabatic model.
Use mass conservation to replace a conservative derivative of ρe by ρDe/Dt. Dotting the momentum equation with u gives ρD(∣u∣2/2)/Dt=−u⋅∇p. Subtraction from total energy leaves
ρcv​DtDT​+p∇⋅u=0.
(24)
Now p=ρRT and Dρ/Dt=−ρ∇⋅u imply
DtDS​=cv​(p1​DtDp​−ργ​DtDρ​)=Tcv​​DtDT​+R∇⋅u=0.
(25)
Thus DS/Dt=0​: smooth adiabatic inviscid trajectories are isentropic. Shocks need an entropy inequality rather than this smooth equality.
Adding ∇⋅(k∇T) models Fourier heat conduction, with heat flux −k∇T. The same subtraction gives ρcv​DT/Dt+p∇⋅u=∇⋅(k∇T), hence
TDtDS​=ρ1​∇⋅(k∇T).​
(26)
For a fixed container use impermeability u⋅n=0, insulation k∇T⋅n=0, and mass conservation. Then
dtd​∫D​ρSdV=∫D​T1​∇⋅(k∇T)dV=∫D​T2k∣∇T∣2​dV.
(27)
The boundary term vanishes and ∇(1/T)=−∇T/T2 supplies the positive sign. For T>0, k≥0, total entropy cannot decrease: this is entropy production by Fourier heat conduction.

Rigid walls require zero normal velocity, so ϕx​=0 at x=0,L and ϕy​=0 at y=0,b. Separation of variables gives spatial modes cos(mπx/L)cos(nπy/b), for nonnegative integers m,n. The wave equation therefore gives
ωm,n2​=π2c02​(m2/L2+n2/b2).​
(28)
The (0,0) spatially constant mode has zero frequency and is not an ordinary positive-frequency acoustic oscillation.
A spatially uniform boundary forcing excites the n=0 transverse mode. Put ϕ=X(x)cosωt. Then X′′+(ω/c0​)2X=0 and X′(L)=0, hence X=Ccos[ω(L−x)/c0​]. The condition X′(0)=a fixes C and gives
ϕ=ωsin(ωL/c0​)ac0​cos[ω(L−x)/c0​]​cosωt.​
(29)
Arbitrary free oscillations can be superposed. The denominator vanishes at longitudinal resonances. Near them the lossless steady harmonic response ceases to be a uniformly small-amplitude approximation; damping from viscosity, heat transfer or wall losses regularizes the response, and large amplitudes can require nonlinear equations of motion or a more accurate moving-boundary model. At exact resonance a switched-on ideal forcing produces secular growth rather than a bounded harmonic particular solution.
For the semi-infinite duct expand F(y)=∑n≥0​Fn​cos(nπy/b). Each axial mode has
kn2​=ω2/c02​−(nπ/b)2.​
(30)
For a propagating mode a particular solution is ϕn​=(Fn​/kn​)cos(nπy/b)cos(kn​x−ωt); differentiating at zero gives the prescribed Fn​cos(nπy/b)sinωt. A nonzero higher transverse coefficient propagates when ∣ω∣>nπc0​/b.
The outgoing radiation condition selects c02​kn​/ω>0, its positive axial group velocity. Thus ωkn​>0​. This sign is selected by the absence of waves incident from infinity, not by the differential equation alone. If F has a nonzero mean, its n=0 component can propagate even below every higher-mode cutoff.
For F=cos(πy/b) and 0<∣ω∣<πc0​/b, put κ=(π/b)2−ω2/c02​​. The decaying solution is
ϕ=−κ1​e−κxcos(πy/b)sinωt.​
(31)
It is evanescent and carries no mean energy to infinity. These mode and sign conditions are summarized by the radiation condition for a waveguide mode.

Along x=Vt+x0​, the phase is tΦ(k)+kx0​, with Φ(k)=kV−ω(k). Graphically, a line of slope V intersects the dispersion curve transversely except where it is tangent. On a transverse region, neighboring wavenumbers acquire order-one phase differences over a width Δk∼t−1, so cancellation leaves an O(t−1) contribution. More formally, integration by parts uses
eitΦ=itΦ′1​dkd​eitΦ
(32)
and gives that order when the amplitude and its derivative are integrable and Φ′ is bounded away from zero on the region.
At a nondegenerate tangent point k∗​, Φ′(k∗​)=0, equivalently V=ω′(k∗​)​, and Φ(k)=Φ(k∗​)−ω′′(k∗​)(k−k∗​)2/2+⋯. Coherent wavenumbers now occupy width O(t−1/2), yielding the stationary-phase contribution
A(k∗​)eik∗​x0​+itΦ(k∗​)−isgn(ω′′(k∗​))π/4t∣ω′′(k∗​)∣2π​​.
(33)
These are the packets whose group velocity follows the observer. The displayed decay rates require the usual regularity and nondegeneracy hypotheses; A→0 alone is not a complete analytic hypothesis, and a vanishing amplitude or degenerate stationary point changes the leading order.
For deep water take a velocity potential satisfying Laplace's equation below the undisturbed surface and decaying with depth. A Fourier mode has vertical factor e∣k∣z. The linearized kinematic and dynamic surface conditions are ηt​=ϕz​ and ϕt​+gη=0 at z=0. Thus
∂t2​η​(k,t)+g∣k∣η​(k,t)=0.
(34)
Release from rest gives η​t​(k,0)=0, so inversion of the Fourier transform gives
η(x,t)=2π1​∫−∞∞​η​0​(k)cos(tg∣k∣​)e−ikxdk.​
(35)
Evenness of the initial profile makes its transform even and the reconstructed surface even; the same formula also works without that symmetry when the usual reality condition holds.
Split the cosine into its two time phases. Along the moving observer, their stationary conditions are ±ω′(k)=V, where ω(k)=g∣k∣​. Since ∣ω′(k)∣=g​/(2∣k∣​), for V=0,
∣k∗​∣=4V2g​,k∗​=±4V2g​.​
(36)
These are the possible dominant stationary-phase packets, provided the initial Fourier amplitude at the selected wavenumber is nonzero. For V=0 there is no finite nonzero stationary wavenumber; the formula must not be applied by division by zero.

Let the bed be z=0 and the free surface z=h(x,t). In a long, shallow layer, vertical acceleration is smaller than gravity, so the vertical Euler equation gives hydrostatic pressure p=patm​+ρg(h−z). Incompressibility makes the vertical speed smaller than the horizontal speed by the aspect ratio; irrotationality then makes the leading horizontal velocity depth-independent. The horizontal Euler equation becomes
ut​+uux​+ghx​=0.​
(37)
Integrating ux​+wz​=0 across the depth, using bed impermeability w(0)=0 and the surface condition w(h)=ht​+uhx​, yields
ht​+(hu)x​=0.​
(38)
Combining these gives the conservative momentum equation (hu)t​+(hu2+gh2/2)x​=0. Integral mass and momentum balance across a stationary thin jump therefore require
[hu]−+​=0,[hu2+gh2/2]−+​=0.​
(39)
These conservation laws still apply across a jump where the smooth hydrostatic/irrotational derivation does not resolve the internal turbulent structure.
Put A=h−​, B=h+​ and q=Au−​=Bu+​>0, orienting the flow from minus to plus. For a nontrivial jump the momentum condition gives q2=gAB(A+B)/2. Its change in mechanical-energy flux is
[ρhu(u2/2+gh)]−+​​=ρq[2q2​(B21​−A21​)+g(B−A)]=−4ABρgq(B−A)3​=4h+​ρgu−​(h−​−h+​)3​​.​
(40)
A passive jump cannot create mechanical energy, so the change is nonpositive. Since q>0, h+​>h−​​ for a genuine jump; equality describes no jump. The upstream flow is supercritical and the downstream flow subcritical, since Fr−2​=B(A+B)/(2A2)>1 and Fr+2​=A(A+B)/(2B2)<1.
The gas-dynamic analogue is a compressive normal shock: density and pressure increase and flow changes from supersonic to subsonic. Entropy increases; directed mechanical energy is irreversibly converted into internal energy. Total gas energy, including internal energy, is conserved across an adiabatic shock. This distinguishes that total-energy balance from the reduced shallow-water mechanical-energy loss.
