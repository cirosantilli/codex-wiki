Let t(n) denote traction: the force per unit area exerted across an oriented internal surface with unit normal n. Define σij​ as component i of the traction on a coordinate plane whose normal is ej​. Balance of linear momentum on a shrinking tetrahedron makes the surface-force terms, of order area, dominate its inertia and body forces, of order volume. Resolving its oblique face into the three coordinate projected areas gives Cauchy's traction formula
t(n)=σn,ti​(n)=σij​nj​.​
(1)
Opposite faces have opposite tractions, as follows independently from a shrinking pillbox. This fixes both the index and normal conventions.
For a material volume V(t), the Reynolds transport theorem says d∫V(t)​fdV/dt=∫V(t)​[ft​+∇⋅(fu)]dV. Apply it componentwise to f=ρui​ and use mass conservation. With no body force in this calculation, balance of linear momentum and the divergence theorem give
∫V(t)​ρDtDui​​dV=∫∂V(t)​σij​nj​dS=∫V(t)​∂j​σij​dV.
(2)
Arbitrariness of the material volume yields ρDui​/Dt=∂j​σij​​, with D/Dt=∂t​+uj​∂j​.
For constant dynamic viscosity and incompressible velocity, ∂j​uj​=0, so
∂j​σij​=−∂i​p+μ∂j​∂j​ui​+μ∂i​(∂j​uj​)=−∂i​p+μ∇2ui​.
(3)
Thus ρ(ut​+u⋅∇u)=−∇p+μ∇2u​.
In fully developed steady pipe flow u=w(x,y)ez​, the convective acceleration vanishes. The transverse equations give px​=py​=0 and the axial equation gives pz​=μ(wxx​+wyy​). One side depends only on z and the other only on (x,y), so both equal a constant. Hence
wxx​+wyy​=pz​/μ,pz​=constant.​
(4)
If n is outward from the fluid cross-section, the wall-on-fluid axial shear per length is μ∮∂n​wds=μ∫A​ΔwdA=Apz​. The fluid-on-pipe force is its negative:
drag per unit length=−Apz​.​
(5)
For forward pressure-driven flow pz​<0, this force points along the flow. Its value depends on cross-sectional area and pressure drop, even though the detailed velocity profile depends on the shape.

Let the thin transverse length be Re−1/2 times the longitudinal scale and write Y=Re1/2y, V=Re1/2v. Continuity then remains ux​+VY​=0. Transverse momentum gives pY​=0 to leading order; the quiescent exterior has constant pressure, hence px​=0. Axial viscous diffusion in x is smaller by Re−1, leaving
uux​+VuY​=uYY​,ux​+VY​=0.​
(6)
Symmetry about the jet centreline requires V=0 and uY​=0 at Y=0. At large positive Y, require u→0 and uY​→0. The transverse velocity can approach a finite nonzero scaled entrainment velocity; imposing V→0 as well would rule out the jet. The physical transverse velocity remains smaller by Re−1/2 and matches an entraining outer flow.
Using continuity, the momentum equation is ∂x​(u2)+∂Y​(uV−uY​)=0. Integrating across the jet, the boundary terms vanish because u,uY​→0 while V is finite. Thus
M=∫−∞∞​u2dY=constant.​
(7)
Apart from the dimensional density and velocity/length factors, this is the conserved axial momentum flux supplied by the nozzle. Entrainment increases volume flux without adding axial momentum from the resting exterior.
For u=xαf′(η) with η=Y/xβ, use the stream function ψ=xα+βf(η) so that u=ψY​ and V=−ψx​. Momentum conservation requires 2α+β=0. Inertia and diffusion scale as x2α−1 and xα−2β, so α+2β=1. Therefore α=−1/3,β=2/3​. In particular
V=x−2/3(2ηf′−f)/3.
(8)
Substitution reduces momentum to 3f(3)+ff′′+(f′)2=0, with f(0)=f′′(0)=0 and f′(∞)=0. Integrating first gives 3f′′+ff′=0, the integration constant vanishing at the centreline. A second integration gives 3f′+f2/2=C. Parameterize its positive constant as C=18A2 and solve the resulting Riccati equation with f(0)=0:
f=6Atanh(Aη),u=6A2x−1/3sech2(AYx−2/3).​
(9)
The momentum integral fixes its scale, since ∫−∞∞​sech4zdz=4/3:
M=48A3,A=(M/48)1/3.​
(10)
The upper-half entrainment is V→−2Ax−2/3; the negative side has the opposite sign, drawing ambient fluid inward. This is the Bickley jet, valid in the slender far-downstream laminar regime, rather than in the immediate nozzle region.

Scale lateral distance with L, transverse distance with h, lateral velocity with U, and the normal velocity with Uh/L. Balancing lateral pressure force with transverse viscous stress gives pressure scale μUL/h2. The ratio of inertia to that stress is Uh2/(νL), while lateral viscous derivatives are smaller by (h/L)2. The two stated smallness conditions therefore reduce the lateral equations to μuzz​=px​, μvzz​=py​. The normal component of balance of linear momentum gives pz​=0 at leading order; continuity remains ux​+vy​+wz​=0. The no-slip, impermeable rigid plates require u=v=w=0 at both plate surfaces.
Integrating with those boundary values gives
(u,v)=−2μz(h−z)​∇p,uˉ=−K∇p,K=12μh2​.​
(11)
Depth-integrated continuity has no normal boundary flux, so ∇⋅uˉ=0 and ∇2p=0​ away from the injection point.
Neglecting surface tension, the fluid free boundary has p=pext​, and its normal velocity is the fluid velocity: R˙=uˉr​(R). Any constant capillary pressure for a circular interface can instead be included in that spatially uniform boundary pressure. Conservation of injected volume gives 2πrhuˉr​=Q. Hence
uˉr​=2πhrQ​,R2=R02​+πhQt​,p(r,t)=pext​+πh36μQ​logrR(t)​.​
(12)
The logarithmic pressure singularity represents the ideal point source, which in a physical cell is replaced by a finite inlet. The formula is an exact radial solution of the depth-averaged model for either sign of Q, while R2>0.
Its robustness differs sharply between the signs. With viscous fluid inside a much less viscous exterior, injection displaces the less viscous phase and is stabilizing; suction draws the exterior into the viscous region and is prone to fingering. One can see this directly without relying on a slogan. Write the boundary as R+an​cosnθ and its regular pressure perturbation as bn​rncosnθ. Linearizing the constant-pressure condition gives δp(R)=−an​p0′​(R)=an​Q/(2πhKR). The perturbation radial velocity is then −nan​Q/(2πhR2), and evaluation of the base velocity at the displaced boundary adds −an​Q/(2πhR2). Thus
a˙n​=−2πhR2(n+1)Q​an​.​
(13)
Modes decay for injection and grow for suction. Accordingly the suction circle is a mathematically possible symmetric solution but generally an unstable prediction, failing before its formal collapse in a perturbed experiment. This is Stability of an interior Hele-Shaw blob under injection and suction.

Two-dimensional incompressibility permits u=ψy​, v=−ψx​. Taking the curl of the Stokes momentum equation eliminates pressure and gives ∇2(vx​−uy​)=0. Since vx​−uy​=−∇2ψ, the stream function obeys ∇4ψ=0​.
Scale radius by a, velocity by U and stream function by Ua. In polar coordinates ur​=r−1ψθ​, uθ​=−ψr​. No penetration makes ψ constant on the cylinder; choose that constant zero. No slip also requires ψr​=0 at r=1. The uniform stream has ψ=rsinθ, specifying the far-field condition.
The boundary forcing has angular mode sinθ, so its corresponding mode must have ψ=f(r)sinθ. Define L1​=d2/dr2+r−1d/dr−r−2. Then ∇4ψ=(L12​f)sinθ, while L1​rs=(s2−1)rs−2. Thus the indicial equation of L12​ is (s2−1)[(s−2)2−1]=(s−3)(s−1)2(s+1)=0, giving
f=Ar3+Brlogr+Cr+Dr−1.​
(14)
The repeated exponent one supplies the logarithmic term. The condition f′→1 first forces A=B=0 and C=1. Then f(1)=0 forces D=−1, but f′(1)=C−D=2=0. No additional Fourier mode can repair the missing boundary condition in this angular mode. Therefore the unbounded-domain Stokes problem has no solution with both no slip and a nonzero uniform far stream.
This is Stokes' paradox. It identifies a nonuniform approximation, rather than the absence of physical flow. For any small but nonzero Reynolds number, convective inertia becomes important sufficiently far away. An inner Stokes region must be matched to an outer Oseen or Navier-Stokes region that retains that inertia. Alternatively, finite external boundaries introduce a different well-posed physical problem; they cannot be sent to infinity while keeping the naive Stokes solution unchanged.

For a material volume, mass conservation is d∫V(t)​ρdV/dt=0. The transport theorem and arbitrariness of that volume give ρt​+∇⋅(ρu)=0​. Balance of linear momentum includes the pressure traction and body force:
dtd​∫V(t)​ρudV=−∫∂V(t)​pndS+∫V(t)​ρgdV.
(15)
Apply transport, the divergence theorem and the mass equation to obtain
ut​+(u⋅∇)u=−ρ1​∇p+g.​
(16)
For small perturbations of the uniform resting barotropic state, write ρ=ρ0​+ρ′, p=p0​+c02​ρ′+⋯. Linearized mass and momentum are
ρt′​+ρ0​∇⋅u=0,ut​=−ρ0​c02​​∇ρ′,c02​=P′(ρ0​)>0.​
(17)
Taking the curl shows that initially vanishing vorticity remains zero, so the acoustic velocity has a potential u=∇ϕ. Integrating momentum, using the additive freedom of a velocity potential to remove the spatially constant integration term, gives ρ′=−(ρ0​/c02​)ϕt​. Continuity now yields ϕtt​=c02​∇2ϕ​.
For spherical symmetry, set ϕ=e−iωtF(r) and k=ω/c0​. The radial equation is (r2F′)′+k2r2F=0, or (rF)′′+k2(rF)=0. Hence
ϕ=re−iωt​[Acoskr+Bsinkr].​
(18)
Rigid boundaries require radial velocity F′=0 at both radii. A form automatically satisfying the condition at a is
F(r)=rC​[cosk(r−a)+ka1​sink(r−a)].
(19)
At b, the condition b(rF)′−(rF)=0 becomes
k(b−a)cosk(b−a)−(1+k2ab)sink(b−a)=0.
(20)
Therefore the nonzero oscillatory eigenfrequencies obey
(1+k2ab)tank(b−a)=k(b−a),ω=kc0​.​
(21)
The undivided sine-cosine form avoids artificial issues from dividing by a cosine. The formal k=0 root is the spatially constant zero-frequency potential additive freedom of a velocity potential, not a propagating acoustic oscillation. This is the radial acoustic spectrum between rigid concentric spheres.

For a vector a described in axes rotating at constant angular velocity Ω, the rotating-axes theorem is (da/dt)inertial​=(da/dt)rotating​+Ω×a. Inertial particle velocity is u+Ω×x. Applying the theorem once more gives inertial acceleration Du/Dt+2Ω×u+Ω×(Ω×x). Thus the rotating momentum equation is
DtDu​+2Ω×u+Ω×(Ω×x)=−ρ1​∇p+g.​
(22)
If g=−∇Φ and density is constant, the centrifugal term can be absorbed into the reduced pressure in a rotating fluid:
P=p+ρΦ−2ρ​∣Ω×x∣2,ut​+(u⋅∇)u+2Ω×u=−∇P/ρ.​
(23)
For small disturbances with Ω=Ωez​, linearize to ut​−2Ωv=−Px​/ρ, vt​+2Ωu=−Py​/ρ, wt​=−Pz​/ρ, together with incompressibility. Put ζ=vx​−uy​. Taking the vertical curl gives ζt​=2Ωwz​. Taking divergence gives ∇2P=2ρΩζ. Applying the Laplacian to the vertical momentum equation therefore gives ∂t​∇2w=−2Ωζz​. Another time derivative yields
∂t2​∇2w+4Ω2wzz​=0.​
(24)
All three velocity components are retained, even for fields independent of y; rotation produces the transverse component.
Set m=π/h and seek w=ae−iωtW(x)sinmz. The rigid-wall conditions are then automatic and the scalar equation gives
W′′+m2(ω24Ω2​−1)W=0,W(0)=1.
(25)
For 0<∣ω∣<2∣Ω∣, define q=m4Ω2/ω2−1​. The two possible oscillatory factors are distinguished by energy propagation. With horizontal wavenumber k, the dispersion relation is ω2=4Ω2m2/(k2+m2) and cg,x​=−ωk/(k2+m2). An outgoing wave in x>0 must have ωk<0, giving
w=ae−iωt−isgn(ω)qxsin(πz/h).​
(26)
For ∣ω∣>2∣Ω∣, define κ=m1−4Ω2/ω2​. Reject the exponentially growing branch, leaving the evanescent response
w=ae−iωt−κxsin(πz/h).​
(27)
The real part is the physical velocity. The radiation or bounded-decay condition is needed in addition to the wavemaker boundary value to pick a unique half-channel response. Steady forcing (ω=0) is a singular limit: with nonzero rotation a steady geostrophic balance and impermeable top/bottom require w=0, incompatible with a nonzero imposed sine profile. At the cutoff equality, the bounded outgoing polarization also degenerates. These exceptional cases cannot be obtained by blindly substituting into the two strict-inequality formulas. The propagating sign is the radiation sign for an inertial wave.

Long-wave geometry makes vertical acceleration small, so pressure is approximately hydrostatic, p=patm​+ρg(h−z). The leading horizontal velocity is nearly depth-independent. Integrating incompressibility through the depth and using the impermeable bed and kinematic free-surface condition gives ht​+(hu)x​=0​. The horizontal Euler equation then gives ut​+uux​+ghx​=0​.
Linearizing about a uniform resting layer gives wave speed c=gh​​. Since h=c2/g, the nonlinear equations can be written ct​+ucx​+(c/2)ux​=0 and ut​+uux​+2ccx​=0. Combining them verifies
[∂t​+(u±c)∂x​](u±2c)=0.​
(28)
Let c0​=gh0​​ and V≥0 be the receding wall's speed, so its position is x=−Vt and its fluid velocity condition is u=−V. The outward-moving rarefaction has the other invariant fixed at its resting value, u−2c=−2c0​. In its varying part, self-similarity gives x/t=u+c. Therefore
c=3x/t+2c0​​,u=32​(x/t−c0​).​
(29)
The wall-adjacent state has u=−V and c=cw​=c0​−V/2. Its right-going characteristic curve speed is c0​−3V/2, whereas the undisturbed head travels at c0​. The complete solution in the fluid x>−Vt is
(u,c)=⎩⎨⎧​(−V,c0​−V/2),(32​(x/t−c0​),31​(x/t+2c0​)),(0,c0​),​−V<x/t<c0​−3V/2,c0​−3V/2≤x/t≤c0​,x/t>c0​.​​
(30)
Both edges match continuously and the wall velocity condition holds. The assumption V2<4gh0​ means V<2c0​, giving positive wall depth and placing the fan tail to the right of the wall by speed cw​>0. The depth everywhere is h=c2/g. This is a shallow-water rarefaction from a receding wall.

Work in the rest frame of the planar shock, with nonzero mass flux j=ρu. Conservation of mass gives [j]=0; the tangential and normal components of balance of linear momentum give [jv]=0 and [p+ju]=0. Thus [v]=0. For an ideal gas, specific internal energy is e=cv​T=p/[(γ−1)ρ], and specific enthalpy is e+p/ρ=γp/[(γ−1)ρ]. Total energy flux is j[(u2+v2)/2+e+p/ρ]. Its continuity, with v already continuous, gives
[ρu]=[v]=[p+ρu2]=[2u2​+(γ−1)ργp​]=0.​
(31)
Put c2=γp/ρ and M=u/c. Since p=ju/(γM2), the normal component of balance of linear momentum says u[1+1/(γM2)] is constant. Hence
u−​u+​​=M−2​(1+γM+2​)M+2​(1+γM−2​)​.​
(32)
The energy equation says u2[1/2+1/((γ−1)M2)] is constant, giving
(u−​u+​​)2=M−2​[2+(γ−1)M+2​]M+2​[2+(γ−1)M−2​]​.​
(33)
Set a=M−2​, b=M+2​, square the first relation and equate to the second. After clearing positive denominators the equation factors as
(b−a){b[2γa−(γ−1)]−[2+(γ−1)a]}=0.​
(34)
The first factor is the unchanged state. For a genuine shock the second factor vanishes, yielding
M+2​=2γM−2​−(γ−1)2+(γ−1)M−2​​.​
(35)
The physical selection principle is entropy increase in the direction of flow. For u±​>0, the upstream side is x<0 and the downstream side is x>0. A compressive entropy-admissible shock has M−​>1,M+​<1​; interpreting the printed “ahead” and “behind” spatial labels must respect these explicit side assignments. The expansion-shock branch would violate the second law.
Substitution gives the velocity ratio
u−​u+​​=(γ+1)M−2​2+(γ−1)M−2​​<1.​
(36)
Since v is unchanged while the normal component decreases, ∣v∣/u increases. Thus an oblique flow bends towards the shock plane, equivalently away from its normal. If v=0 there is no angular bending; the flow stays normal and simply slows. The tangential continuity, not a change in the tangential speed, causes the deflection.
