Activation energy (Ea​)

 Home Chemistry Chemical reaction Chemical kinetics
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 6
A small increase in temperature can produce a large increase in a reaction rate. In the simplest Arrhenius equation model, warming from 300 to 310K increases a rate constant with Ea​=80kJmol−1 by about 2.81 times, even though the absolute thermodynamic temperature rises by only about 3.33%. This sensitivity helps explain why cooling can slow unwanted chemical reactions and why a hot process can become difficult to control. The comparison assumes the same rate law and reactant concentrations; the activation energy characterizes k(T), rather than every change in the measured reaction rate.
The physical motivation is the distribution of molecular energies. Warming changes the Boltzmann distribution, increasing the relative population of energetically costly configurations. A transition state can therefore become much more accessible than the modest change in average kinetic energy might suggest. The exponential function in an Arrhenius equation captures such sensitivity in a simple model; it is not a universal formula for the fraction above every barrier in a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution.[ref]
Activation energy quantifies how strongly a rate constant depends on thermodynamic temperature. For a positive rate constant k(T), the empirical definition is
Ea​(T)=RT2dTd​lnkref​k(T)​,
(108)
where R is the molar gas constant and kref​>0 is a fixed reference with the same units as k. The ratio is a dimensionless quantity, keeping the natural logarithm meaningful without changing its derivative. Thus Ea​ is a molar energy, commonly in Jmol−1 or kJmol−1. The Arrhenius equation makes Ea​ constant over its assumed range. This rate-based definition, and the need for care when identifying it with a threshold on a potential energy surface, are stated in the activation energy.
In a simple barrier picture, reaching a transition state costs energy relative to the starting reactants. The height of that barrier is different from the difference between the reactants and reaction products. An energetically favourable endpoint can still be reached slowly. An energy profile makes the distinction visible, while an Arrhenius plot tests temperature sensitivity from rate constants. A measured Ea​ need not equal a barrier read from an arbitrarily chosen diagram.
For a computed example, assume a first-order reaction has k(300K)=0.100s−1, constant Ea​=80kJmol−1, and a temperature-independent pre-exponential factor. The Arrhenius equation gives
k(300K)k(310K)​=exp[8.31446261880000​(3001​−3101​)]≈2.81398.
(109)
Hence k(310K)≈0.281398s−1. Its half-life falls from ln2/0.100≈6.93147s to ln2/0.281398≈2.46322s. These are chosen model parameters, not measurements of an identified chemical reaction. The exponent uses kelvin, not unconverted Celsius temperatures, and 80000Jmol−1 must match R in Jmol−1K−1.
A microscopic reaction energy barrier belongs to a specified path on a potential energy surface. An experimental activation energy belongs to a specified rate constant and its temperature dependence. A multi-step reaction mechanism can change which step controls the observed conversion, and a temperature-dependent pre-exponential factor contributes to the observed activation energy. Even the Gibbs energy of activation of transition state theory is a different quantity: it incorporates entropy as well as enthalpy and depends on the standard states. These distinctions prevent a fitted Arrhenius plot from being mistaken for a direct microscopic energy measurement.[ref]
Catalysis illustrates the separation between a path and its endpoints. At the same temperature and with the same reactants, reaction products, and standard states, a regenerated catalyst changes the available reaction mechanism and rate constants, while the overall standard change in Gibbs free energy and the equilibrium constant remain the same:
K=exp(−RTΔr​G∘​).
(110)
For a reversible first-order reaction pair A⇌B, consistency at chemical equilibrium requires kf​/kr​=K. A model that multiplies only kf​ by a catalytic speedup while holding kr​ fixed would change this ratio and misrepresent catalysis. This simple ratio applies to that specified rate law; a multi-step catalytic reaction mechanism needs its own rate equations. A potential-energy profile alone cannot determine K, because its endpoint difference is not generally the standard change in Gibbs free energy.[ref]
